# Chapter 1 - A War Without Sound

When he opened his eyes again, the smell of death was gone.

Stewing vegetables had replaced it, earthy and sweet, along with the sharp tang of burning pine.

For a while, the boy lay still and breathed. Someone had wiped the mud and dried blood from his face. A heavy wool blanket covered him, and a straw mattress crackled softly whenever he moved.

A stone hearth popped and hissed nearby, throwing shadows up rough wooden walls and across thick ceiling beams. One of those shadows seemed to linger after the fire shifted. He blinked, and it behaved itself.

There was a small table with a candle burning low, iron pots hanging from hooks, and a shuttered window black with night. By every measure, it was an ordinary cabin.

The boy knew what the table was for. He understood why the pots hung beside the hearth and why the shutters had been closed against the night. He knew the purpose of everything he saw.

He could not remember where he had learned any of it.

The old man from the battlefield sat on a low stool beside the hearth. A dented metal mug rested between his hands, and his shoulders sagged as he stared into the fire.

The blankets rustled, and the man looked up. When the boy tried to push himself upright, he raised one broad hand.

“Easy now. You’ve been senseless since morning. If you mean to sit up, do it slowly. I’m too tired to catch you twice.”

The boy stopped halfway and waited for the room to settle.

“…Where?”

The man tipped his head. “Didn’t catch that.”

“Where am I?”

“My farm. In Mydea.” His eyes moved towards the shuttered window before returning to the boy. “Gerolt, by the way. If you’re wondering whose roof you woke under.”

“…Mydea?”

“Aye, Mydea. This kingdom we’re in.” Gerolt gestured vaguely with his mug. “My house, my farm, and what used to be my wheat field are all Mydean land. I haven’t moved you *that* far.”

A small laugh escaped him.

“Getting you from the field to this bed was work enough.”

*Mydea.*

It was the name of an entire kingdom, and the boy had never heard it before. His fingers closed around the blanket as he waited for the name to stir something inside him.

It did not.

“The name should mean something, shouldn’t it?” His voice trembled, and the words began coming faster. “But it doesn’t. I’ve never heard it before.”

Gerolt’s laughter stopped.

“Never heard—lad, you’re lying in it.” He leaned forward, the mug forgotten between his hands. “You must remember a road, a town, whose colours you marched under—something.”

“I remember opening my eyes in your field.” The boy pressed a palm against his forehead. “That is the first thing I have.”

Gerolt stared at him.

“By the Four.”

His gaze moved over the boy again and settled on his clothes.

“All right. Start with what we can see.” He pointed towards the boy’s chest. “You’re no soldier. Barely twenty, by the look of you, and what in the Last Dark are you wearing?”

The boy looked down at himself.

“Are they strange?”

“Strange?” Gerolt reached towards one cuff, then stopped before touching it. “No iron. No leather. Cloth soft enough for a lord’s bed, yet you came through that field without a tear in it. Look at the stitching. Thirty years of traders coming down this road, and I’ve never seen work like that.”

The boy rubbed the dark material between his fingers.

“Who made it?” Gerolt asked.

The boy’s face tightened. “I wish I could tell you. Looking at these clothes feels the same as looking at this room. I know what they are, but nothing in me says they belong to me.”

“Yet they fit you.”

“That only makes it worse.”

The answer left him more sharply than he intended. Gerolt drew his hand back and studied him for a moment.

“Aye,” he said. “I suppose it would.”

The boy ran his thumb over the seam and waited for a memory to come. When nothing did, his nail slipped beneath one of the stitches.

“Leave the cloth before you pull it apart,” Gerolt said. “We’ll start simpler. What do people call you?”

The boy opened his mouth.

The answer should have come without thought. Instead, he sat there with his lips parted while Gerolt waited.

Nothing came.

“Well?” Gerolt prompted.

“I…” The boy’s face tightened. “I can’t remember.”

“Your own name?”

“I know I should.” His voice cracked as he looked down at his hands. “Why can’t I remember it?”

Gerolt’s expression changed. Until then, some part of him had still suspected the boy was being difficult. That suspicion disappeared.

“All right,” he said, quieter now. “What about home? A village, a city, a river—anything you recognise?”

“I know what those things are. I can picture them when you say the words.” The boy gripped the blanket harder. “None of them feel familiar.”

“Nothing before this morning?”

“There has to be something.” The boy heard his voice rising and could not stop it. “I can’t have *nothing*.”

“Easy. You’re shaking badly enough without tearing your clothes to pieces.”

The boy barely heard him. His hands returned to his collar, then his cuffs, then every strip of stitching he could reach.

*Someone made these. Someone will recognise them.*

There was nothing hidden in the lining. He checked every seam twice anyway, then stopped himself before beginning a third search.

“Wait,” Gerolt said. “Your name might be gone, but your『Affinity』won’t be. That isn’t something a knock to the head can take.”

The boy looked at him. “My what?”

Gerolt’s brow furrowed.

“You don’t know that either?”

“Stop asking me as though I chose not to.”

Silence followed.

Gerolt sat back. Some of the suspicion left his face.

“Fair,” he said. “Fire, water, earth or wind, for most folk. Your element. You’re born with it, and it stays with you until you die.”

“And I’m meant to feel which one?”

“Aye. It’s part of you, not something you were taught.”

“And I’m supposed to know which one when I can’t even remember my own name?”

Gerolt considered that.

“You don’t hunt for it. Close your eyes and stop pulling at those seams for a moment. Sit still. Let it come to you.”

The boy closed his eyes.

He waited for heat to gather somewhere inside him, for a current beneath his skin, for the weight of earth or the movement of air.

None came.

“I can feel the blanket,” he said, opening his eyes. His face pinched with frustration. “I can feel the heat from the fire and my own heart beating. There is nothing else.”

“You were quiet for all of five breaths.”

“How long should it take?”

“For most folk? No time at all.”

The answer struck harder than Gerolt appeared to intend. He rubbed a hand across his mouth before continuing.

“But you’re frightened, and your breathing is all over the place. Settle it first, then try again.”

“I did try.”

“And I’m asking you to try once more.”

The boy shut his eyes again.

The fire cracked beside him. Gerolt shifted on the stool. Beneath the bed, something breathed so softly that he almost mistook it for the settling straw.

He pushed those sounds away and waited.

There was no heat in his blood. No current beneath his skin. No weight or movement straining to be noticed.

Only the same emptiness.

His eyes opened.

“It’s the same. There’s only the dark behind my eyes.”

Gerolt picked up the mug, then set it down again without drinking.

“That cannot be right.”

“You keep saying that.”

“I knew a man who came home from the Campaign unable to name his own wife. Looked at her as if she’d wandered in off the road.” Gerolt rubbed his thumb against the mug’s handle. “He could still light his pipe from his finger. Memory goes. An Affinity does not.”

“Then mine is gone.”

“An Affinity doesn’t simply go.”

“You keep telling me what must be inside me.” The boy’s hands shook, but he held Gerolt’s gaze. “I’m telling you what I feel.”

“Everyone has one.”

“Then what am I?”

Gerolt had no immediate answer. His eyes moved towards the door before returning to the boy, and this time he kept his distance.

“I have no name for this,” he admitted. “Weak, damaged, buried—I’d believe any of those. But where your Affinity should be…”

His voice lowered.

“It’s empty.”

“Empty,” the boy repeated.

Gerolt grimaced.

“Poor word. I meant the place where your Affinity should be, not the whole of you.”

The boy looked down at his hands.

He was not certain there was a difference.

Gerolt rose and crossed to the window. He opened the shutter a finger’s width and stared into the night, one hand braced against the frame.

“That field was wheat yesterday,” he said. “Poor wheat, but mine. I knew every ditch in it.”

The boy watched him.

“This morning it held more dead than I’ve seen in the rest of my life put together. Men under banners I didn’t know. Armour I didn’t know. Those silver things lying among them.”

“You were a soldier?”

Gerolt’s mouth tightened.

“Long enough to bury men under half the flags in Mydea. Not one banner in that field meant anything to me.”

He kept one eye to the opening.

“And men don’t die beside beastfolk. Not shoulder to shoulder. Not by the thousands.”

“Why not?”

“Because they don’t.”

The answer came too quickly. The boy waited, and Gerolt’s expression hardened.

“Not in any war I’ve known,” he amended. “And I’ve known enough.”

Something beneath the bed gave a thin whine.

The boy looked down.

“Wena?”

“That’s Wena.” Gerolt nodded towards the darkness beneath the frame. “She barks at owls, carts, wind in the shutters—anything that gives her an excuse. Last night, she made no sound at all.”

Two frightened eyes caught the firelight beneath the bed.

“Found her pressed against my door this morning,” Gerolt continued. “Could hardly get the fool thing inside. Now she won’t come out. Not even for me.”

The eyes vanished again.

Gerolt closed the shutter and turned towards the boy.

“I’m not blaming you. Understand that.” His voice had lost its earlier humour. “But something happened on my land, and you’re the only living soul who came out of it. I need you to think past the fear, if you can. Did you see anything I didn’t? Hear anyone speak?”

“Only what was there when I woke. Crows. Bodies. The machines. Those violet sparks.” The boy lowered his eyes. “You saw all of it before I did.”

“Anything before you opened your eyes?”

“The first thing I remember is waking in that field.”

Gerolt’s shoulders lowered.

“I wish I could tell you more,” the boy said. “You carried me here. You cleaned me. I can see that you’re afraid, and I can’t explain any of it.”

“Aye. I know.” Gerolt scrubbed a hand over his face. “Wanting an answer doesn’t make one appear.”

The boy’s stomach growled into the silence.

He froze.

Gerolt looked at him, and the boy pulled the blanket higher as his face grew hot.

“I’m sorry.”

“For what? Having a stomach?”

Gerolt stared another moment before an unsteady breath escaped him, almost becoming a laugh.

“Well. At least one part of you knows what it wants.”

He crossed to the hearth, took a wooden bowl from the counter, and filled it from the pot.

“It’s hot. Take it with both hands—there.”

Gerolt kept hold of the bowl until the boy’s grip was secure.

The first spoonful stung his cracked lips. He swallowed it anyway.

Root vegetables. Salted meat. Thick broth.

He recognised every taste.

“Thank you.”

“You’re welcome. Now eat slowly, or I’ll be seeing it again.”

The boy lowered the next spoonful and blew across it before eating.

Gerolt retrieved his mug and returned to his stool. He did not ask another question. He sat with both hands around the metal and watched the fire instead.

A dark nose appeared beneath the bed and sniffed towards the bowl.

“Oh, now you’re brave,” Gerolt muttered.

Wena’s nose disappeared.

The boy ate another spoonful. By the fourth, the shaking in his hands had begun to ease.

Then the cabin went white.

For one breath, he was looking across a battlefield.

Someone was running towards him, their face lost in the light. One hand reached for him while their mouth shaped a word he could not hear.

They were close—close enough that they should have reached him within seconds—but another step brought them no nearer. Nor did the one after that.

His hand lifted from the bowl and reached towards them before he realised he had moved.

Something cold passed through him in measured stops, pressing first behind his eyes, then at his throat, then beneath his ribs.

At the empty place where Gerolt had told him an Affinity should be, it stopped.

The pressure drew inward. His breath locked, and feeling drained from his outstretched hand until he could no longer tell whether it was still there.

Then the cold moved on.

His hand remained raised. His heart continued beating.

The figure was still running. Their lips formed the word again, and this time he almost heard it.

The white closed over them before the sound reached him.

The spoon rattled against the bowl.

“Something wrong with it?”

Gerolt sounded impossibly far away.

The boy tried to breathe, but his chest would not open.

“What is it? Is it your head again?”

The stool scraped across the floor.

“Put the bowl down. Can you hear me?”

The bowl slipped from the boy’s hands and cracked against the boards. Hot stew splashed across his knees as his body folded forward.

“Damn the bowl.”

Gerolt caught him before his head struck the floor, closing one hand around his shoulder while the other braced the back of his neck.

“Don’t tear at your throat. You’ll hurt yourself. Look at me—come on, look at me.”

The boy could not. His fingers continued clawing for a path that would not open.

“You’re in my house,” Gerolt said. Fear roughened his voice, but his hands remained steady. “The fire is beside you. I’ve got hold of you, and I’m not letting go. Can you hear that?”

The boy could hear him.

He could not answer.

Then the white released him.

The cabin returned at once—the fire, Gerolt’s face, the broken bowl between his knees—but his body did not recover with it.

He dragged at his throat until a thin breath scraped its way in. It hurt. He coughed it out and fought for another.

“There you are. Don’t fight for the whole breath,” Gerolt said. “Take what comes, then take the next.”

The boy pulled in another shallow breath.

“That’s it. I’m not letting go. Take your time.”

One breath followed another. His hands continued to shake long after his chest had loosened.

“Someone…” he managed.

Gerolt bent closer. “Someone was there?”

“Running towards me.”

“Who?”

The boy’s face twisted. “I couldn’t see them. I couldn’t hear them. They were right there, and I still—”

“All right. You don’t have to—”

“I should know!”

The words tore out of him. Gerolt fell silent.

“Their mouth moved,” the boy continued, struggling to keep his voice together. “They were trying to tell me something. I almost heard it, but then the light took them away.”

Gerolt’s hand remained on his shoulder.

“Was it someone from that battle?”

“How am I supposed to tell?” The boy’s face twisted as anger broke through his fear. “I woke up in your field. That is all I know about it!”

His voice cracked, but he could not stop.

“I don’t remember the battle. I don’t remember how I got there. I don’t even know whether I belonged with any of those people.”

The words left him shaking harder than before.

Gerolt did not flinch or try to contradict him.

“Aye,” he said quietly. “You’re right. I keep asking you for a beginning you never saw.”

The boy’s anger collapsed as quickly as it had risen.

“I didn’t mean—”

“You’re frightened. No harm done.” Gerolt tightened his hand once around the boy’s shoulder. “But I can see what chasing it is doing to you. Leave it for tonight. Whatever that was, it can wait until you can stand without falling over.”

The boy nodded, though he could still see the figure running towards him whenever he closed his eyes.

He tried to hear the word again.

He remembered the shape of their mouth and the hand reaching for him. He remembered how desperately they had run.

The sound itself was gone.

The darkness gave him back exactly what it had given him all day.

---

Gerolt stayed crouched beside him, one hand steady on his shoulder.

Then his fingers dug in.

His head turned towards the road, and every part of him went still.

At first, the boy heard nothing. Then a faint rhythm passed through the floorboards and into his knees.

*Hoofbeats.*

Far off, but coming fast.

Under the bed, Wena's whimpering cut off, as if a hand had closed around it.

Gerolt was at the window before the boy could ask what was wrong. He pressed one eye to the gap in the shutters, and whatever he saw out there wiped the exhaustion from his face and left only fear behind.

He moved without a word.

The iron poker scraped through the hearth. Ash fell over the coals, and their orange glow died, ember by ember. He crossed to the table and pinched out the candle between finger and thumb. The flame burned him. He did not pull away.

Darkness swallowed the cabin.

Outside, a torch flared to life. Orange light cut through the shutters and laid long bars across the floor.

One of them swept straight across the boy.

He froze.

*They saw me.*

Then he found Gerolt's face in the dark, and understood.

He flattened himself against the floorboards, dragging the blanket with him. Gerolt crossed the room in two strides, eased the bench onto its side, and pulled the boy in behind it.

"Not a sound," Gerolt breathed. "Not one. Do you understand me?"

"What's—"

"Riding openly across Mydean fields…" His voice was barely there. "They shouldn't be here. Not ever."

His mouth started to shape a name. Whatever it was, he swallowed it.

The boy's lips parted.

The riders might have crossed the field. They might know what had happened there, or recognise his clothes, his face. Before he could stop it, hope rose in him.

One of them might even know *him.*

He drew breath to call out—

—and Gerolt's hand tightened on his shoulder.

The boy looked at him. At the man who had walked through thousands of corpses that morning without breaking. Who had carried a stranger home across his own ruined harvest, and put food in his hands, and asked for nothing.

Now that same man crouched in the dark beside a smothered fire, hiding from whoever stood outside rather than let them know he was home.

Calling out wouldn't be the boy's choice alone. It would make it for both of them.

He looked once towards the door.

Then he closed his mouth.

He wanted answers. He chose silence.

Outside, the hoofbeats slowed. Harness creaked. Metal chinked against metal.

Then they stopped.

The boy held his breath. His heart hammered so hard against his ribs he was certain it would give them away.

A voice murmured beyond the wall.

Not a language he knew. The syllables overlapped, as if several throats were shaping the same sounds without keeping time with one another.

Gerolt's fingers dug harder into his shoulder. His eyes never left the door.

The boy gave up on the voice and listened beneath it instead. A buckle shifting. A hoof scraping dirt. Torchlight sliding between the shutters, vanishing, returning from a new angle.

None of it made a picture. He didn't know how many were out there, where they stood, or what they wanted. So he watched Gerolt instead.

If the farmer understood that tongue, his face didn't show it. But he knew enough to be afraid.

For now, that was enough for the boy too.

A stirrup rang softly. A mount released a long breath as weight lifted off its back.

Someone dismounted.

The boy waited for the ground to answer—the thud of boots, the crunch of dirt.

It never came.

*Tock.*

*Tock.*

*Tock.*

Knuckles on the door.

Gentle. Patient. As if whoever stood outside was willing to wait forever.

Gerolt's eyes went wide. He looked down at the boy, and the boy looked back. His heart battered his ribs and every breath shook on the way out, but he did not turn towards the door.

*Don't move,* Gerolt mouthed. No sound came with it at all.

No hoof scraped the dirt. No harness shifted. No one spoke.

Then the fourth knock came, no louder than the first three, no less patient.

*Tock.*

The boy's breath stopped halfway in. A thought surfaced from the same empty place that had given him the word『Magic』.

*Opening that door would be the last thing he ever did.*
