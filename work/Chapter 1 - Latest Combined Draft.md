# Chapter 1 - A War Without Sound

Scorched earth stretched towards the horizon, and the dead covered almost every part of it.

The boy tried to count the nearest bodies. The number fell apart before he reached twenty. Behind every corpse lay another, and beyond those were hundreds more, scattered among broken shields, abandoned weapons and armour split deeply enough to show what remained inside.

Some had died fighting. Their swords were still buried in shields or held above their heads, caught halfway through the final blow.

Others had simply fallen.

One soldier remained on his knees with both hands wrapped around a spear. There was no wound on him that the boy could see. Nearby, a horse lay with its forelegs stretched forward as though it had collapsed in the middle of a stride. Its rider was trapped beneath it, reaching towards another man whose fingers rested inches away.

Neither had reached the other.

The battle had been interrupted.

Above the bodies stood machines made from seamless silver metal.

The boy knew what a cart was. He understood what wheels were for and what ordinary hands could shape in a forge. These things belonged to none of that knowledge. He waited for the word machine to feel like his own. It didn't.

One had collapsed on jointed legs taller than a man. Another lay on its side with a narrow opening running across its smooth front. The metal surrounding that opening had blackened and curled inward.

He could not have explained how the machines worked.

He still knew they were weapons.

The bodies lying beneath them were not all alike. Pale soldiers with pointed ears had fallen among the men. Farther out lay creatures with horns, claws and shoulders broad enough to strain the armour wrapped around them.

One horned warrior had died shoulder to shoulder with a human soldier. Their weapons pointed in the same direction.

Were they fighting together?

Nothing answered him.

A crow landed on a broken shield and hopped onto the man beneath it. The bird buried its beak below the edge of his breastplate and pulled. The wet tearing sound carried across the field because there was nothing else to cover it.

The boy turned away—and saw where the destruction ended.

There had been no gradual retreat from ruin into untouched land. The bodies did not thin out, and the burned ground did not fade into trampled grass.

It simply stopped.

Beyond the final corpse, rows of wheat stood beneath the wind. Their golden heads moved together, close enough that the boy could hear them brushing against one another.

He searched for the path the army had taken into the field. Thousands of soldiers should have crushed the crop beneath their boots. Horses, carts and those enormous machines should have left roads of torn earth behind them.

There was nothing.

No wheel ruts. No abandoned supplies. No trail of broken wheat leading towards the horizon.

The entire battlefield had appeared in the middle of the crop without disturbing a single stalk beyond it.

The boy looked back at the banners lying among the dead. He understood what banners were supposed to mean. They marked armies, rulers and the place a soldier belonged.

None of them meant anything to him.

He searched the faces next. Human. Pale. Horned. Young enough to have been frightened and old enough to have learned how to hide it.

Every one of them was a stranger.

He looked down at the blood covering his clothes.

Was I here with them?

The question found the same blank place as everything else.

Behind him, the old man released a breath.

"That was wheat."

The boy turned.

The farmer was staring beyond the bodies at the untouched rows. His jaw had tightened so hard that the muscles moved beneath his beard.

"All of it. Poor wheat, but mine." His voice had gone rough. "I go to bed with a field I've walked every furrow of since I was your age, and I wake up to—this." He gestured once at the corpses without looking at them directly. "Hip-high. Nearly ready for the sickle. I could've told you the exact day I'd cut it."

"You were here?" The boy's throat scraped around the words. "Did you hear what happened?"

"Hear it." The farmer gave a short, humourless breath that wasn't quite a laugh. "Lad, a fox puts one paw wrong near my henhouse and I'm awake before the other three follow. Fifty winters on this land, and I know every sound it's ever going to make at me."

He crouched beside the nearest corpse. His hand moved towards the ruined breastplate, then stopped short of touching it, hovering there as if he'd only just remembered what it was.

"Last night I heard wind. Two owls arguing over the same tree, same as always." His hand came back to rest on his own knee. "No shouting. No horns. No steel on steel. You cannot put this many armed fools in one place and have them kill each other silently. It doesn't happen. It has never once happened."

"Then when did they come?"

"I went to sleep with a field—not a graveyard." He didn't look at the boy when he said it. "That's the whole of what I know, and I'd give a great deal to know more."

The boy looked across the bodies again.

"Have you found anyone else alive?"

The farmer didn't answer straight away. His eyes moved over the nearest dead as though another survivor might rise simply because he'd been asked to look for one.

"I found you."

"And the others?"

"No." The word came out flat, all the dryness gone from it. He looked away as soon as he'd said it, out towards the wheat, like there was something in that direction worth looking at instead.

A violet spark moved between two blackened stones near the boy's knee. It crawled over the surface of one, stretched across the space between them, and struck the other with a dry crack.

He flinched.

"Don't touch it," the farmer said, already reaching to pull him back by the collar before the boy had even leaned in.

"What is it?"

"If I knew that, I'd have given you a warning worth a damn instead of just grabbing you." He watched the spark die into the soil, his jaw still tight. "Whatever in the Last Dark it is."

The spark divided into several thin branches before disappearing into the soil. Others still moved farther across the field, flashing between armour and patches of burned earth.

Whatever had happened here had done more than burn the ground. In several places, frost remained beneath the midday sun. Light passed through it without reflecting properly, leaving those patches dimmer than everything around them.

Then the boy noticed the circle.

It lay only a short distance from where he had awakened.

A perfect boundary divided it from the surrounding destruction. Outside, the earth had burned, frozen or split apart. Inside, green grass survived beneath a clear layer of frost. Every blade had been pressed flat in the same direction, forming a spiral that tightened towards the centre.

There were no bodies inside.

No blood. No armour. No fragment of bone or metal.

The boundary was too exact to be chance. Nothing from the battle had crossed it—not a fallen weapon, not a footprint, not even ash carried by the wind.

The boy stared at its centre.

The grass was empty.

Some part of him insisted it should not have been.

Someone was there.

He didn't know who. He didn't remember seeing them. The certainty had arrived the same way cart and wheat and machine had—whole, unearned, belonging to no one he could name.

Then another word rose beside it, arriving with that same borrowed certainty.

『Magic』

The boy's breath caught.

He knew what the word meant.

How do I know that?

Something moved beneath the recognition. For an instant, he felt himself falling away from something he could not see. The distance opened too quickly. He tried to hold on, though he had no hands there and nothing he could name to reach for.

Then the feeling tore loose.

The battlefield returned all at once.

The boy drew a breath through his nose. Blood, ash and opened bodies coated the back of his throat. His stomach clenched hard enough to fold him forward.

He covered his mouth.

Too late.

Bile and bloody spit struck the scorched earth between his hands.

"Easy—by the Four, lad!"

The farmer dropped beside him, one hand at his back, the other closing hard around the boy's arm as though that alone might hold him upright. The first words reached the boy clearly. Everything after them sounded farther away.

The ground shifted beneath his knees. Crows lifted from the bodies, but their wings became black streaks across his failing sight. The machines leaned together, their silver shapes losing their edges.

"Look at me. Come on now, boy, stay with me."

The boy tried. The farmer's face wouldn't hold still long enough for him to find it.

His arms gave way.

The old man caught him before he struck the ground, swore under his breath, and hauled him up over one shoulder like a sack of feed he'd have complained about even on a good day.

"Ten paces," the farmer muttered, rising with a grunt he didn't bother to hide. "You couldn't have fallen ten paces nearer the house. Heavy little shite, and half-starved besides, where do you even keep it—"

He kept talking the whole walk back, low and constant, not really to the boy at all—the way a man talks to keep his own hands steady.

At the edge of the boy's narrowing vision, a violet spark stretched between two stones. It thinned until it was no wider than a strand of hair.

Then it snapped.

When he opened his eyes again, the smell of death was gone.

Stewing vegetables had replaced it, earthy and sweet, along with the sharp tang of burning pine.

For a while, the boy lay still and breathed. Someone had wiped the mud and dried blood from his face. A heavy wool blanket covered him, and a straw mattress crackled softly whenever he moved.

A stone hearth popped and hissed nearby, throwing shadows up rough wooden walls and across thick ceiling beams. One of those shadows seemed to linger after the fire shifted. He blinked, and it behaved itself.

There was a small table with a candle burning low, iron pots hanging from hooks, and a shuttered window black with night. By every measure, it was an ordinary cabin.

The boy knew what the table was for. He understood why the pots hung beside the hearth and why the shutters had been closed against the night. He knew the purpose of everything he saw.

He could not remember where he had learned any of it.

The old man from the battlefield sat on a low stool beside the hearth. A dented metal mug rested between his hands, and his shoulders sagged as he stared into the fire.

The blankets rustled, and the man looked up. When the boy tried to push himself upright, he raised one broad hand.

"Easy now. You've been senseless since morning. If you mean to sit up, do it slowly. I'm too old to catch you twice in one day."

The boy stopped halfway and waited for the room to settle.

"…Where?"

"Didn't catch that. Try using more than one syllable, you've got a whole mouth back there."

"Where am I?"

"My farm. In Mydea." His eyes moved towards the shuttered window before returning to the boy. "Gerolt, by the way. Seeing as it's my roof you've woken up under, seemed only polite to introduce myself before you started asking it questions."

"…Mydea?"

"Aye, Mydea. This kingdom we're in." Gerolt gestured vaguely with his mug. "My house, my farm, and what used to be my wheat field are all Mydean land. So no, I haven't moved you that far. Carrying you as far as this bed nearly finished me, and I'll be feeling it in my back for a week."

A small laugh escaped him, though it didn't reach far.

Mydea.

It was the name of an entire kingdom, and the boy had never heard it before. His fingers closed around the blanket as he waited for the name to stir something inside him.

It did not.

"The name should mean something, shouldn't it?" His voice trembled, and the words began coming faster. "But it doesn't. I've never heard it before."

Gerolt's laughter stopped.

"Never heard of it. Lad, you're currently lying in it." He leaned forward, the mug forgotten between his hands. "You must remember a road, a town, whose colours you marched under. Something. People don't usually turn up on my land from nowhere in particular."

"I remember opening my eyes in your field." The boy pressed a palm against his forehead. "That is the first thing I have."

Gerolt stared at him.

"By the Four." He rubbed a hand over his jaw. "Well, shit. That's inconvenient."

His gaze moved over the boy again and settled on his clothes.

"All right. Let's start with what we can actually see, since apparently that's all either of us has to work with." He pointed towards the boy's chest. "You're no soldier. Barely twenty, by the look of you, and what in the Last Dark are you wearing?"

The boy looked down at himself.

"Are they strange?"

"Strange is generous." Gerolt reached towards one cuff, then stopped before touching it. "No iron. No leather. Cloth soft enough for a lord's bed, and you came through that field without a tear in it. Thirty years of traders on this road and I've never seen stitching like that. Not from any hand I know."

The boy rubbed the dark material between his fingers.

"Who made it?" Gerolt asked.

The boy's face tightened. "I wish I could tell you. Looking at these clothes feels the same as looking at this room. I know what they are. Nothing in me says they belong to me."

"Yet they fit you."

"That only makes it worse."

The answer left him more sharply than he intended. Gerolt drew his hand back and studied him for a moment.

"Fair enough," he said, and for once, nothing followed it.

The boy ran his thumb over the seam and waited for a memory to come. When nothing did, his nail slipped beneath one of the stitches.

"Leave the cloth alone before you unpick the poor thing entirely," Gerolt said. "We'll start simpler. What do people call you?"

The boy opened his mouth.

The answer should have come without thought. Instead, he sat there with his lips parted while Gerolt waited.

Nothing came.

"Well?" Gerolt prompted.

"I…" The boy's face tightened. "I can't remember."

"Your own name."

"I know I should." His voice cracked as he looked down at his hands. "Why can't I remember it?"

Gerolt's expression changed. Whatever half-suspicion he'd been holding — that the boy was being difficult, or cagey, or playing some game a farmer wasn't clever enough to see through — dropped away entirely.

"All right," he said, quieter now. "What about home? A village, a city, a river. Anything you recognise."

"I know what those things are. I can picture them when you say the words." The boy gripped the blanket harder. "None of them feel familiar."

"Nothing before this morning?"

"There has to be something." The boy heard his voice rising and could not stop it. "I can't have nothing."

"Easy. You're shaking badly enough without tearing your clothes to pieces on top of it."

The boy barely heard him. His hands returned to his collar, then his cuffs, then every strip of stitching he could reach.

Someone made these. Someone will recognise them.

There was nothing hidden in the lining. He checked every seam twice anyway, then stopped himself before beginning a third search.

"Wait." Gerolt caught his wrist before he could start on another seam. "Leave the coat alone. There's one more thing worth trying before you unravel yourself down to the thread count. What's your『Affinity』?"

The boy stopped with his nail still hooked under a stitch.

"My what?"

"Your element. Fire, water, earth, wind. Whichever one decided it liked you best." Gerolt tapped his own chest. "Everybody's born with one, same as they're born with a face."

Everybody's born with one. The boy turned the words over, the way he'd turned over every other unfamiliar thing tonight — table, mother, home — waiting for some feeling of ownership to arrive alongside the meaning. It never did. He understood what fire was. He did not know whether fire was supposed to know him back.

"So it isn't something I learned," he said slowly. "Not like knowing what a spoon is for without remembering who put one in my hand. It's more like my height or the colour of my eyes. Something that's there whether I remember it or not."

"Aye. Close enough."

"Then how could I lose it?" His voice caught somewhere in the question, and he had to push through it. "How could there be nothing?"

"There isn't supposed to be." Gerolt's mug sat forgotten between his hands. "That's my whole point."

"Then try telling me how to find it, instead of just telling me it's there."

"Close your eyes. Don't chase after it — that only scares it off. Sit still and feel for whichever one already belongs to you."

"That explains nothing."

"I know. Nobody's ever had to explain it to me before. It's just — there. The way a man doesn't need someone to point out he's got hands."

The boy closed his eyes anyway, not because the explanation had helped but because refusing felt like one more thing he'd be taking from a man who had already given him a bed, a fire, half his dinner.

The fire ticked and settled somewhere to his right. The blanket was rough beneath his thumb. His pulse beat once, twice, past ten, behind the ache in his temple, and the wind pushed at the shutters like it wanted in.

That was all of it. Fire outside him. Wind outside him. Nothing on the other side of the wall answering back.

He opened his eyes. Gerolt was already watching him.

"Well?"

"There's a fire. I can feel it because I'm sitting three feet from it." The boy heard his own voice go flat, reciting the room back like directions to a stranger. "There's wind, because your shutters don't sit right in the frame. Is that what you meant, or am I only telling you about your own house?"

"You're only telling me about my house."

"Then there's nothing."

"Give it another moment. Sometimes it's shy the first—"

"You said I would know it." The words came faster now, and once they started he found he couldn't slow them back down. "You said I'd feel it before I could even put words to it. I don't feel fire. I don't feel water. I don't feel earth or wind or some fifth thing you haven't mentioned because you're hoping I won't need it. I feel a fire that isn't mine, and wind that isn't mine, and a headache that's at least probably my own, and that is the whole list, Gerolt. That is every single thing I have."

Gerolt said nothing. The mug sat between his hands, forgotten.

"So either I'm doing this wrong," the boy said, quieter now that the anger had burned through as fast as it had come, "or there's actually nothing there. Tell me which. Because I've spent the whole night finding out I'm missing things one piece at a time, and I would rather hear it plainly than keep discovering it the way I discovered I don't have a name."

Gerolt was quiet long enough that the boy almost took it back.

"I don't know which," he said finally. "That's the truth of it. I've seen men forget their wives. I've seen men forget the war. I've never seen one come up this empty." He set the mug down on the floor and let go of it. "I keep hoping if I say the right thing, something in you will just — answer. Like calling a dog that's wandered off. Maybe it didn't hear you the first time."

"And if it doesn't come the second time either?"

"Then you call again. Louder." Something almost like a smile crossed his face, though there wasn't much behind it. "I'm a farmer, not a scholar. Calling louder is the whole of my strategy."

The boy didn't answer that. He was too tired to find it funny, and too frightened to let it comfort him, and he sat there with both feelings pressing on him at once until neither one won.

Gerolt rose and crossed to the window instead of pressing further, opening the shutter a finger's width to stare into the night, one hand braced against the frame.

"I keep going back to the banners," he said, without turning around. "Not one of them meant a thing to me, and I could name every flag that's ever marched through Mydea in my sleep. Same with the armour. Same with those silver devils lying in among the rest." He shook his head slowly. "A man spends enough years at war, he thinks he's seen every way there is for people to kill each other. Apparently not."

"You were a soldier."

"Long enough." His jaw worked once. "And in all those years, I never once saw a man die shoulder to shoulder with beastfolk. Not fighting beside them. Not by the thousands."

"You said that already. Out in the field."

"I know I did." Gerolt didn't turn around. "I'm still saying it because I still don't have a better answer than I had this morning. It doesn't sit right in me, lad. Men and beastfolk don't fight on the same side. Not in any war I've ever heard of, not in any I've fought in myself. And there they were, laid out next to each other like it was nothing."

He kept one eye to the opening in the shutter.

"So either I've been wrong about how the world works for sixty years, or something out there made it true just long enough to kill every one of them."

Something beneath the bed gave a thin whine.

The boy looked down.

"Wena?"

"That's Wena." Gerolt nodded towards the darkness beneath the frame. "Barks at owls, carts, the wind changing its mind about which direction to blow. Give her half an excuse and she'll use it. Last night, not a sound out of her."

Two frightened eyes caught the firelight beneath the bed.

"Found her pressed against my door this morning like she was trying to become part of it. Now she won't come out. Not even for me, and she'd usually trade her own tail for a scratch behind the ears."

The eyes vanished again.

Gerolt closed the shutter and turned towards the boy.

"I'm not blaming you. Understand that." His voice had lost its earlier edge completely. "But something happened on my land, and you're the only living soul who walked out of it. Is there anything you remember that I don't? Anyone you heard speak, before I found you?"

"Only what was there when I woke. Crows. Bodies. The machines. Those violet sparks." The boy lowered his eyes. "You saw all of it before I did."

"Anything before you opened your eyes?"

"The first thing I remember is waking in that field."

Gerolt's shoulders lowered.

"I wish I could tell you more," the boy said. "You carried me here. You cleaned me up. I can see that you're afraid, and I can't explain a single piece of it."

"Aye. I know." Gerolt scrubbed a hand over his face. "Wanting an answer badly enough has never once made one show up."

The boy's stomach growled into the silence.

He froze.

Gerolt looked at him, and the boy pulled the blanket higher as his face grew hot.

"I'm sorry."

"For what? Having a stomach? That's the first normal thing about you all night."

Gerolt stared another moment before an unsteady breath escaped him, almost becoming a laugh.

"Well. At least one part of you knows what it wants, even if the rest of you's useless."

He crossed to the hearth, took a wooden bowl from the counter, and filled it from the pot.

"It's hot. Take it with both hands, there. And if you spill it, you're the one explaining the stain to my floor."

Gerolt kept hold of the bowl until the boy's grip was secure.

The first spoonful stung his cracked lips. He swallowed it anyway.

Root vegetables. Salted meat. Thick broth.

He recognised every taste.

"Thank you."

"Don't thank me yet. Thank me once you've kept it down. I've seen grown men give my stew right back before now, and I'd rather not add you to the list."

The boy lowered the next spoonful and blew across it before eating.

Gerolt retrieved his mug and returned to his stool. He did not ask another question for a while. He sat with both hands around the metal and watched the fire instead.

A dark nose appeared beneath the bed and sniffed towards the bowl.

"Oh, now you're brave," Gerolt muttered, not unkindly.

Wena's nose disappeared.

The boy ate another spoonful. By the fourth, the shaking in his hands had begun to ease.

Then the fire went white.

The cabin disappeared behind the glare. Across a field of broken earth, someone was running towards him.

Their boots struck the ground without a sound. He could not make out their face — only an outstretched hand, and the dark shape of a mouth moving around a word he could not hear.

They were close enough to reach him in three strides.

He watched them take all three.

The distance did not change.

The boy reached back. The spoon tilted in his other hand, spilling broth over his fingers, but he felt none of it.

The figure tried the word again. Their hand closed once on empty air, then opened and strained farther towards him.

Feeling left the boy's fingertips. It passed across his palm and into his wrist.

The spoon struck the rim of the bowl.

"Something wrong with it?"

Gerolt's voice came from somewhere beyond the white.

The boy tried to answer. His throat closed before a sound came.

Across the field, the figure shaped the word once more. This time, he almost heard it.

"Boy?"

The stool scraped against the floorboards.

White light crossed the space between them. The figure's mouth disappeared inside it. Their hand remained for one moment longer, fingers spread towards his, and then that vanished too.

The bowl slipped from his lap, struck the boards, and spun away. Hot stew splashed across his knees as he folded forward, both hands flying to his throat.

"Damn the bowl."

Gerolt caught him beneath the arms before his head struck the floor.

"Here, lad. Look at me if you can. I've got you."

The boy's nails dug beneath his jaw. He could feel the skin gathering under them, but he could not make his hands stop.

"Leave your throat alone, you'll have it bleeding." Gerolt closed one hand around both of the boy's wrists and pulled them down against his chest. His other arm stayed firm around the boy's shoulders. "You're in my house. The fire's beside you. Feel my arm around you and stay here with me."

Fear roughened Gerolt's voice. His grip did not shake.

The battlefield vanished.

The cabin returned — the fire, Gerolt's face, the overturned bowl — but the boy's lungs remained closed.

Then a thread of air scraped into him. He coughed it out and dragged in another.

"There. That one came," Gerolt said. "Let the next be small as well. I've got you."

The boy obeyed because there was nothing else he could do. One shallow breath followed another until his chest finally loosened. Only then did Gerolt ease his grip.

"There was someone," he managed.

Gerolt bent closer. "In the field?"

"Running towards me."

"Who?"

"I couldn't see their face." The boy caught at Gerolt's sleeve. "They were close enough that they should have reached me, but they kept running and never came any nearer. Their mouth kept moving. I almost heard—"

His breath caught again.

"I should know who they were."

Gerolt gave him a moment before asking, "Was it someone from that battle?"

"How am I supposed to tell?" The boy's face twisted as anger broke through his fear. "I woke up in your field. That is all I know about it!"

His voice cracked, but he could not stop.

"I don't remember the battle. I don't remember how I got there. I don't even know whether I belonged with any of those people."

The words left him shaking harder than before.

Gerolt did not flinch or try to contradict him.

"Aye," he said quietly. "You're right. I keep asking you for a beginning you never saw."

The boy's anger collapsed as quickly as it had risen.

"I didn't mean—"

"You meant every word." Gerolt tightened his hand once around the boy's shoulder. "Doesn't make you wrong. But I can see what chasing this is doing to you. Leave it for tonight. Whatever that was, it can wait until you can stand without falling over."

The boy nodded. When he closed his eyes, the reaching hand returned and the mouth formed the word again, but all he could hear was Gerolt's fire.

Gerolt stayed crouched beside him, one hand steady on his shoulder.

Then his fingers dug in.

His head turned towards the road, and every part of him went still.

At first, the boy heard nothing. Then a faint rhythm passed through the floorboards and into his knees.

Hoofbeats.

Far off, but coming fast.

Under the bed, Wena's whimpering cut off, as if a hand had closed around it.

Gerolt was at the window before the boy could ask what was wrong. He pressed one eye to the gap in the shutters, and whatever he saw out there wiped the exhaustion from his face and left only fear behind.

"Shit." It came out under his breath, barely a word at all.

He moved without another one.

The iron poker scraped through the hearth. Ash fell over the coals, and their orange glow died, ember by ember. He crossed to the table and pinched out the candle between finger and thumb. The flame burned him. He did not pull away.

Darkness swallowed the cabin.

Outside, a torch flared to life. Orange light cut through the shutters and laid long bars across the floor.

One of them swept straight across the boy.

He froze.

They saw me.

Then he found Gerolt's face in the dark, and understood.

He flattened himself against the floorboards, dragging the blanket with him. Gerolt crossed the room in two strides, eased the bench onto its side, and pulled the boy in behind it.

"Not a sound," Gerolt breathed. "Not one. Do you understand me?"

"What's—"

"Riding openly across Mydean fields…" His voice was barely there. "They shouldn't be here. Not ever."

His mouth started to shape a name. Whatever it was, he swallowed it.

The boy's lips parted.

The riders might have crossed the field. They might know what had happened there, or recognise his clothes, his face. Before he could stop it, hope rose in him.

One of them might even know him.

He drew breath to call out—

—and Gerolt's hand tightened on his shoulder.

The boy looked at him. At the man who had walked through thousands of corpses that morning without breaking. Who had carried a stranger home across his own ruined harvest, and put food in his hands, and asked for nothing.

Now that same man crouched in the dark beside a smothered fire, hiding from whoever stood outside rather than let them know he was home.

Calling out wouldn't be the boy's choice alone. It would make it for both of them.

He looked once towards the door.

Then he closed his mouth.

He wanted answers. He chose silence.

Outside, the hoofbeats slowed. Harness creaked. Metal chinked against metal.

Then they stopped.

The boy held his breath. His heart hammered so hard against his ribs he was certain it would give them away.

A voice murmured beyond the wall.

Not a language he knew. The syllables overlapped, as if several throats were shaping the same sounds without keeping time with one another.

Gerolt's fingers dug harder into his shoulder. His eyes never left the door.

The boy gave up on the voice and listened beneath it instead. A buckle shifting. A hoof scraping dirt. Torchlight sliding between the shutters, vanishing, returning from a new angle.

None of it made a picture. He didn't know how many were out there, where they stood, or what they wanted. So he watched Gerolt instead.

If the farmer understood that tongue, his face didn't show it. But he knew enough to be afraid.

For now, that was enough for the boy too.

A stirrup rang softly. A mount released a long breath as weight lifted off its back.

Someone dismounted.

The boy waited for the ground to answer — the thud of boots, the crunch of dirt.

It never came.

Tock.

Tock.

Tock.

Knuckles on the door.

Gentle. Patient. As if whoever stood outside was willing to wait forever.

Gerolt's eyes went wide. He looked down at the boy, and the boy looked back. His heart battered his ribs and every breath shook on the way out, but he did not turn towards the door.

Don't move, Gerolt mouthed. No sound came with it at all.

No hoof scraped the dirt. No harness shifted. No one spoke.

Then the fourth knock came, no louder than the first three, no less patient.

Tock.

The boy's breath stopped halfway in. A thought surfaced from the same empty place that had given him the word『Magic』.

Opening that door would be the last thing he ever did.
