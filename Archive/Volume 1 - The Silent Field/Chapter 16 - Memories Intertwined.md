# Memories Intertwined

On the third day of the crossing—the first on which Neldor had trusted her with a brush—the bucket escaped Seralune when the ship rolled beneath her. Its wooden base scraped across the deck, struck a coil of rope and emptied itself over Neldor’s boots.

He looked down.

Seawater ran from his ankles towards the narrow openings along the rail. The bucket continued without it, turning once upon its side before coming to rest against the mast.

Seralune kept both hands around the long-handled brush.

“You told me it would remain beside the brace.”

Neldor lifted one wet boot. “I told you to place it on the other side.”

She looked at the mast, then at the matching wooden support opposite the one she had chosen. The deck sloped towards it as the ship settled from the roll.

“You pointed.”

“I did.”

“At the mast.”

Neldor laughed before he could stop himself, deeply enough that the sailor repairing a line near the bow glanced over.

Seralune waited.

Neldor recovered, though amusement remained in his voice. “I thought you knew which side of a mast could hold a bucket.”

“Why would I know that?”

The question ended what remained of his laughter. He walked through the water she had spilled and retrieved the bucket.

“You wouldn’t,” he admitted. “That was my mistake.”

He placed it against the higher side of the brace and caught the rope coil with his foot before the water reached it.

“When the deck leans, anything loose goes with it,” Neldor explained. “Keep the bucket where the roll pushes it into something, not away from it. And use half that much water next time. You’re scrubbing the boards. You don’t have to drown them.”

Seralune looked along the section she had completed.

Pale streaks crossed the wood wherever her brush had moved. Several followed the grain. Most did not. Water gathered around her bare toes and soaked the hem of the trousers Nyana had found for her.

“You could have mentioned that before I began.”

“I did mention the grain.”

“You said to work with the wood.”

“That is what it means.”

“No,” Seralune replied, irritation sharpening her certainty. “That is what it means to someone who already knows.”

Neldor studied the brush as if deciding whether to take it back.

Instead, he crouched and ran two fingers along one of the boards.

“These lines,” he said. “Follow them towards the bow. Short strokes where the pitch rises. Longer ones across the middle. If the brush catches, change the angle instead of forcing your shoulder.”

Seralune lowered the bristles and copied the movement. Her injured palm objected beneath Nyana’s clean wrapping. The bruising near her shoulder pulled when she reached too far, so she shortened the stroke and tried again.

The pale crust along the wood thinned beneath the brush.

Neldor nodded. “That.”

“That is considerably clearer.”

“I’ll remember that the next time a lady asks to scrub my deck.”

The brush stopped.

“I gave you the name Sera.”

“And I’ve used it,” Neldor replied. “That does not mean I believe you grew up carrying buckets.”

“Was that intended as mockery?” she asked.

“No.” Neldor returned the bucket to her side. “I have simply never had one ask before.”

He moved towards the stern, leaving wet footprints behind him.

Seralune considered calling him back to dry them himself.

Then she looked at the clean strip beneath her brush.

It was scarcely wider than her hand. She looked at the remaining boards and imagined the whole stretch clean.

Seralune filled the bucket halfway and set it against the correct brace.

This time, it remained there when the deck rolled.

---

She had nearly finished the space between the mainmast and the forward hatch when Nyana appeared at the top of the companionway.

The medic climbed backwards, one hand extended below her. Nereth followed slowly, her fingers fixed around Nyana’s wrist.

Seralune set the brush down.

Nereth wore a loose green shirt beneath a dark wrap Nyana had cut away from the healing bite. Two days of rest had returned some colour to the uninjured half of her face. On the other side, the black-purple veins no longer moved beneath her skin. They had settled into long dark scars running from below her left eye, across her temple and beneath her hair. A second branch disappeared under the collar towards her shoulder.

The eye remained closed.

Nereth reached the deck and released Nyana’s wrist before both feet had settled.

Nyana kept her hand raised. “You let go too early.”

“I had both feet beneath me.” Nereth leaned against the companionway.

“One was still on the step.”

Nyana addressed Seralune. “She has been awake since dawn and spent most of it arguing that walking is a form of rest.”

“I said it was preferable to lying down,” Nereth corrected.

Seralune came closer. “Why are you walking?”

“Because I need to know whether I can. We will not have this ship or Nyana’s room once we reach land.”

“And finding out had to happen today?”

Nereth’s good eye found the brush, the wet trousers and the section of deck Seralune had been cleaning.

“You seem to have chosen today as well.”

The familiar dryness in her voice warmed Seralune more than Nyana’s morning assurances had.

“I have become useful,” Seralune said.

Nereth considered the cleaned boards. “Then I am glad one of us has.”

Nyana placed a folded blanket upon a low chest beside the mast. “She may walk from here to that chest. Slowly. With one of us beside her.”

“I can reach it alone,” Nereth said.

Six ordinary steps separated Nereth from the chest. The deck between them was clear, though it never remained level for long.

“Then try,” Seralune said.

Nyana drew breath to object.

Nereth straightened.

Seralune moved out of the direct path but stayed close enough to reach her. “I will stay beside you. I will not hold you unless you need me.”

“Thank you,” Nereth said.

Her first step landed carefully. The second followed before the deck could shift beneath her. The third took longer, but she continued.

Seralune watched her feet rather than her face. Nereth placed each one flat before trusting it. She had nearly reached the chest when a stronger gust filled the green sail above them.

The deck leaned.

Nereth’s next step searched for a place that had moved. Her knee folded, and the hand she threw towards the mast found only air.

Seralune caught her around the waist.

The weight pulled across Seralune’s injured shoulder. She turned with it instead of resisting, letting Nereth fall against her good side while Nyana reached them from behind.

Nereth’s boots dragged once across the boards. Nyana braced them until the deck levelled beneath their feet.

“Please do not tell me I should have listened,” Nereth said.

Seralune adjusted her hold so Nereth could place more weight upon her. “I was going to ask whether you could reach the chest with me.”

“I nearly reached it by myself.”

“You did. Now let me help you finish.”

Nyana moved Nereth’s arm across Seralune’s shoulders and checked the bandage beneath the dark wrap. “Did the scars pull?”

“They always pull.” Nereth’s answer lost some of its edge. “It is worse beneath the eye.”

“Worse than this morning?”

Nereth considered the pain before answering. “No. The same.”

Nyana touched the skin beside the lowest scar, careful not to press it. “No new heat. No movement beneath the skin.”

“They still throb,” Nereth said. “You simply cannot see it now.”

Seralune felt the admission through the arm across her shoulders. Nereth had walked while that pain kept time beneath her face and had mentioned it only when asked directly.

“We are sitting down,” Seralune said.

“I can still walk if you help me.”

“Then we will finish together.”

Seralune and Nyana lowered Nereth onto the blanket. Seralune remained beside her.

Nyana finished checking Nereth’s pulse. “You two have become very close, haven’t you? It is rather sweet.”

Seralune looked down at Nereth’s arm, which remained around her shoulders.

Nereth followed her attention and removed it.

“I was falling over,” Nereth said. “I’d have held on to your brother if he’d been nearest.”

“She also carried you through half a mountain,” Nyana replied. “And you have asked where she is every time you wake.”

“I needed to know whether she had created another disaster while I slept,” Nereth said.

“Of course,” Nyana said. “Nothing sweet about that.”

Colour touched the unscarred side of Nereth’s face.

Seralune sat beside her upon the chest. “I asked about you as well.”

“I know,” Nereth muttered.

“Your patient would like to rest without being discussed,” Nereth told her.

“Then my patient should stop giving me reasons to discuss her.”

Nyana laughed and unfolded the blanket across Nereth’s knees. Seralune drew one corner over the hand Nereth had hidden beneath her opposite arm.

Nereth did not remove it.

---

Neldor brought the road to them that afternoon.

He spread a narrow piece of sailcloth across the same chest while Nereth rested against the mast. Charcoal lines crossed its surface: the northern harbour, the road leaving it and a dark mark where a second route joined from the west.

“Do not use this to count miles,” he warned. “It shows where the road bends, nothing more. Follow it too literally and you will end up walking into the hills.”

Seralune crouched on the opposite side. “Then what should we count?”

“Landmarks. The harbour road follows the coast until it reaches a stone shrine with a broken bell. Take the inland fork there. Two days after that, you should meet the pilgrim road leading towards Favale.”

“Why do you say should?” Nereth asked.

Neldor tapped the charcoal line. “The road should still be there. I cannot promise who controls it. If Mydean soldiers turn you away, do not argue over the map. Find a farm and ask which route the supply carts are using.”

He placed two small packs beside the sailcloth. Oatcakes filled one. The other held dried fruit, smoked fish, clean cloth and a stoppered jar that smelled faintly of Nyana’s medicines.

“Those are from my stores,” Neldor said before Seralune could look towards the hold. “Nothing inside the king’s crates has been touched.”

“This is more than a little food,” Seralune said.

“It will feel like less after a week between two people. Ration it. Carry what water you can and refill it whenever someone offers. There are long stretches between the inland farms.”

Seralune rested a hand upon one pack. “What do you want for it?”

Neldor indicated the cleaned section of deck. Sailors had crossed it throughout the day, but the pale salt marks had not returned.

“Finish what you started before we reach land,” he said. “And help Nyana sort the medicine stores. She has been complaining about my labels since before Waluna.”

“Your labels are numbers,” Nyana called from beside the forward hatch. “Half the bottles are not.”

Neldor raised his voice without turning. “You have always known which numbers mean fever medicine.”

“That does not improve the labels.”

Seralune looked down at the map to hide her smile. “I accept.”

Neldor reached behind him and brought out two hooded travelling cloaks. Both were plain brown, worn pale along their fastenings. A pair of long scarves lay folded on top.

“Those are not for the weather,” Nereth said.

“Not only for the weather,” Neldor answered. He picked up one scarf and folded it lengthwise. “Mydea is already frightened of Natharul. Two elves leaving a ship carrying royal supplies will be remembered before you reach the end of the pier.”

Seralune touched the pointed edge of her ear.

“You want us to hide them.”

“Until you’re clear of the harbour, at least. I’d rather they remembered my cargo than your faces.”

Seralune took the scarf.

“Show me.”

Neldor demonstrated upon himself, drawing the cloth over his hair and fastening it beneath the hood so neither ear disturbed the line of the fabric. Seralune copied him. Her first attempt pulled too tightly beneath her chin. The second left one ear visible whenever she turned her head.

After Seralune’s second failed attempt, Nereth reached out.

“Bring the fold lower,” she said. “Your hair can cover the edge.”

Seralune followed the instruction. Nereth tucked two strands across the cloth and drew the hood forwards.

Neldor studied the result. “At a glance, that will do.”

“What happens when someone looks more closely?” Seralune asked.

“Keep moving and keep the hood low. Most people will not look again.”

Nereth touched the scars crossing her face. The hood could shadow them, but not remove them.

“They will look twice at me.”

Neldor did not offer a false reassurance. “Then let them believe you were burned. There are enough wounded people on Mydean roads that most will understand without asking.”

Nereth lowered her hand.

Seralune examined the dark line of the route to Favale. “How many people know we are aboard?”

“My crew knows Nyana took responsibility for two injured passengers. They know you are leaving at the northern harbour. Nothing more.” Neldor folded his arms. “That is all they need unless danger follows you onto my ship.”

“It will not,” Seralune said.

“The southern slope tore open two days ago,” Neldor replied. “I am asking what you know, Sera, not what you hope.”

“I do not believe anyone has followed us.”

“Thank you.”

He began rolling the sailcloth map from one end.

“Why Favale?” Neldor asked. “You told Nyana you were searching for your mother, but not why the road begins there.”

Seralune hesitated. Neldor waited with the map half-rolled, and she remembered the lie she had told him in Waluna.

“A man helped us leave the palace,” she said. “He was hurt. I had to leave him there.” She kept her attention upon the map. “My mother left instructions with his family. He told me someone in Favale could help me find her.”

Neldor let the loose edge of the map curl over his thumb before he finished rolling it.

“Do you know that your mother is alive?”

“No.” The word hurt differently now that she could say it without demanding another answer. “I know only that she left something for me and that the trail begins in Favale.”

“And if it ends there?”

Seralune looked towards the horizon beyond the bow. No land interrupted it.

“Then I will learn why.”

Neldor tied the map and placed it across the two packs. “Keep these below until we are closer to shore. Leave them on deck and the sea will claim them before you need them.”

He returned to the stern.

Once he was out of earshot, Nereth said, “He respects you more than he did in Waluna.”

Seralune pulled the scarf loose from beneath her hair. “He knows I can clean a deck.”

“A rare royal talent.”

“I intend to become unbearable about it.”

Nereth began to smile, then winced when the movement pulled at the scars.

---

Night left only one lantern burning near the bow.

The watch remained at the stern with Neldor. Two sailors worked quietly beneath the forward sail, their voices lost whenever the canvas filled. Everyone else had gone below or taken shelter among the covered cargo.

Nereth had refused the medical store after waking from her evening sleep.

“Nyana said you were to remain lying down,” Seralune reminded her as they settled against a lashed stack of empty barrels.

“I am sitting. The difference is mostly direction.”

“Nyana will not appreciate that argument.”

“Then you may improve it before she hears.”

Seralune arranged the blanket across Nereth’s legs. The sea had cooled with the loss of the sun, and wind found every space the barrels left between them.

Far beyond the bow, the horizon divided black water from a sky crowded with unfamiliar stars. Seralune had known their names once. Several remained where she expected. Others had shifted so slightly that looking at them felt like seeing an old friend wearing someone else’s expression.

Nereth rested her head against the barrel behind them.

“Does it still hurt?” Seralune asked.

“Yes.”

“How badly?”

Nereth considered it. “Less than the aqueduct. More than I would choose.”

Seralune looked at the scars disappearing beneath her hair. “Nyana cannot tell whether they will fade.”

“I know.”

“Does that frighten you?”

Nereth’s fingers moved over the blanket until they found a loose thread. “The pain does. The face is still mine, whether people enjoy looking at it or not.”

The thread survived a single pull. Nereth released it before the weave opened.

“They do not make me want to look away.”

“Nyana has infected you with sentiment,” Nereth said.

“I was capable of it before.”

“You concealed it better.”

The wind moved around the barrels.

Seralune thought of the map waiting below. “What will you do when we find my mother?”

“That depends upon what she is like.”

“She is kind.”

“She was kind.” Nereth’s correction remained gentle. “A thousand years is a long time to become someone else.”

Seralune drew the blanket farther over Nereth’s knees. “It did not feel long to me.”

“No. But it belonged to everyone else.”

Seralune remembered her mother beside the south-garden fountain, the silver signet hanging against her throat. The face remained young in memory. The hands around Seralune’s hair had not carried the marks of another century, much less ten.

“I want to ask why she left,” Seralune said. “Then I want to know why Thaer would not tell me.”

“In that order?”

“I think so.”

“Good. Your brother is farther away.”

Seralune almost smiled.

The stars vanished.

---

Her room held the last warmth of evening.

One curtain moved beside the open window. Each time the wind entered, the loose latch lifted from the frame and tapped against the wood.

Seralune had meant to sit upon the edge of her bed until he arrived.

She was standing near the door instead.

She had spent the last quarter-hour arranging her anger into the greeting he deserved. It had not stopped her from crossing to the door twice and listening for footsteps she refused to admit she recognised.

The handle moved.

She opened the door before he could.

Dark-brown hair had fallen across his forehead. He wore no armour, and no sword hung at his side. Lamplight caught in green eyes level with her own and along the rounded edge of an unmistakably human ear.

Relief destroyed the greeting she had prepared.

Seralune laughed and caught his hand.

“You’re late again.”

“I know.”

He was trying not to smile.

She pulled him over the threshold before his hesitation could return. His fingers closed around hers with the ease of long practice, and his quiet laugh followed them into the room.

The door closed.

The latch tapped against the window.

Seralune lifted his left hand between them. No cloth covered it. No blood marked the palm. His skin was warmer than hers, rougher beneath the fingers, and she knew where each small ridge would meet her touch before it did.

She pressed his palm against her cheek.

The warmth entered her without resistance.

A strand of her hair lay between his thumb and her skin. He moved it aside. Seralune turned into his hand as though she had been waiting all evening to complete that single movement.

Perhaps she had.

He had stopped smiling. Whatever had kept him outside the door had followed him across the threshold.

Her thumb rested against the inside of his wrist.

She tightened her fingers and began to speak—

The room tore away from her.

---

Pain opened beneath Seralune’s breastbone.

It spread before she could draw away from it, passing through her ribs and spine as though every part of her had been caught inside the same invisible grip. The deck remained beneath her, yet her body seemed to be pulled across a distance too vast to survive.

Her hands stopped belonging to her.

The sea vanished beneath a white pressure that contained no light. Pain drove through the place where the young man’s hand had rested and spread behind her eyes until she felt it through her teeth. It had no direction she could resist. It only kept pulling.

She struck the deck upon one hip.

Tears reached her mouth before she knew she was crying.

“Seralune!”

Nereth’s voice broke across the name. The forward sail cracked full at the same instant, swallowing the cry before it could reach the stern.

Nereth caught Seralune’s shoulders, then released the injured one immediately when pain forced a sound from her.

“What’s happening?” Nereth asked. Fear stripped every trace of dryness from her voice. “Tell me what to do. Seralune, are you hurt?”

The bedroom remained behind Seralune’s eyes.

The human face.

His palm.

The sentence she had lost.

Nereth moved in front of her and nearly fell in the attempt. One hand struck the deck. The other found Seralune’s wrist.

“Look at me,” Nereth pleaded. “Please. I don’t know what to do.”

Seralune tried to answer. The pain pulled the sound apart before it reached her mouth.

Mana rushed towards her panic without shape or direction. It passed through the agony without changing it. Nothing left her body. No flame opened. No wind moved beyond what already filled the sails.

Nereth’s fingers pressed against her wrist.

“I’m here,” Nereth said. The words shook, but she repeated them. “You’re on the ship. I’m here.”

Seralune fixed upon the pressure of those fingers.

The deck returned first: hard boards beneath her hip, one seam pressing against her palm. Then the salt upon her lips. Canvas strained overhead. Water opened and closed along the hull.

The distance inside her began to narrow.

Pain remained, deep enough to make her stomach turn, but it no longer pulled every part of her in a different direction.

Seralune closed her hand around Nereth’s.

“Stay,” she managed.

“Good. You can hear me.” Nereth kept hold of her hand. “I’m staying. Should I call Nyana?”

Seralune waited until the deck completed another slow roll.

“Not yet. If it happens again, call her.”

“You don’t know what happened.”

“I know. But it is passing, and you cannot cross the deck alone.”

Nereth looked towards the forward hatch, measuring the distance against what her legs could manage. She remained where she was.

Seralune drew her knees closer. Tears continued leaving her eyes without permission. She wiped them with the back of her wrist and found more behind them.

Nereth shifted beside her, close enough that their shoulders touched.

“What happened to you?” Nereth asked.

Dark water moved beyond the rail.

“I was in my room.”

Nereth waited for the rest rather than filling it for her.

“The room I had before I woke,” Seralune continued. “The curtain beside the window. The loose latch. I knew where everything was.”

“Was someone there?”

“A young man came to the door.”

Nereth’s fingers turned beneath hers until they could hold properly. “Who was he?”

“I don’t know.”

She searched for a name and found only the shape of his hand against her cheek.

“He was human,” Seralune said. “I could see one ear beneath his hair. It was rounded. He stood outside my room as though he had done it many times before.”

“There has not been a human inside Natharul’s royal castle for as long as I have been alive,” Nereth said. “Not one who could have walked into your rooms as though he belonged there.”

“He did belong there.”

She had answered before deciding whether she believed it.

Nereth did not challenge her. “Did he say anything?”

“I told him he was late. He said he knew.” Seralune could still hear the smile he had tried to hide. “Then I took his hand.”

“Why did you take it?”

“Because seeing him made me happy. I wanted him close.”

Seralune touched her cheek. No heat remained there now, yet she knew the exact breadth of his palm and the rough place beneath his first finger.

“I knew his hand, Nereth. I knew he would move the strand from my face before he did it. I knew I had been waiting for him.”

Nereth looked towards Seralune’s empty palm. “But you cannot remember his name.”

“I cannot remember him at all.”

“Outside what you just saw?”

Seralune shook her head.

“I remember that room. I remember the south garden beneath it and the corridor outside. I remember my mother complaining about the evening bells and Thaer leaning against my doorway when he wanted to avoid whatever Father had asked him to do.” The final admission came out smaller. “But he is not in any of those memories.”

The ship rolled. Their joined hands moved against the blanket between them.

“Could it have been a dream?” Nereth asked, though doubt weakened the question before it ended.

“It was not.”

“How can you be certain?”

“Because I miss him.”

She had not meant to admit that much. Now she could not withdraw it.

Nereth spoke more slowly. “Do you think the memory came from before you were gone?”

“The room was exactly as I remember it. It must have come from before.”

Neither woman spoke the number.

Even the longest human life would have ended within a fraction of the years Seralune had lost. She did not know whether the young man had died soon after the moment she had seen or lived peacefully for decades before anyone now living had been born.

She knew only that the memory had reached her tonight.

“Something happened to him—to both of us—and I cannot reach it.”

“Perhaps your mother knew him,” Nereth said. “If he came to your rooms that often…”

“She might have.” Seralune pressed her fingertips against the place upon her cheek. “She must have known something.”

“All right,” Nereth said. “What do you want to do?”

Seralune turned towards the horizon.

Her mother had left an order that survived ten centuries. Cyrandor had paid in blood to place its first direction in Seralune’s hands.

“We find my mother’s trail,” she said. “If she left Natharul after I disappeared, she may know what happened around me. She may know why no one would answer when I asked what came before.”

“And if she knows him?”

Seralune’s fingers closed around the queen’s signet beneath her shirt.

“Then I ask his name.”

Nereth rested against the barrels again. Exhaustion had returned to her face, but she kept her hand inside Seralune’s.

“Favale first,” she said.

“Favale first,” Seralune agreed.

The forward sailors finished with the line and moved towards the middle of the ship. Neither looked behind the lashed barrels. At the stern, the watch lantern remained a small yellow point beside Neldor.

Seralune and Nereth stayed where they were.

The coast remained beyond sight. Somewhere past the dark water lay Favale and the first surviving piece of her mother’s trail. If any part of Seralune’s missing life had endured there, perhaps his name had too.

Seralune watched for land anyway.
