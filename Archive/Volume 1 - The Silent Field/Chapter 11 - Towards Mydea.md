# Towards Mydea

The coastal settlement came closer without becoming easier to reach.

Its roofs had separated from one another sometime during the descent. What had appeared from the crater as a gathering of pale shapes now possessed chimneys, courtyards and narrow roads descending between them towards the sea.

Seralune learned each part of the settlement through the spaces between Nereth’s breaths.

Three steps.

A pause.

Two more while the breath held.

Then the broken sound of air forcing its way back into her.

Nereth’s arm remained across Seralune’s shoulders, though it no longer tightened when the path slipped beneath them. Her head rested against the side of Seralune’s neck. The black-purple veins beneath her left eye had darkened in the growing day, branching past her hairline and disappearing beneath what remained of her collar.

The marks pulsed.

Seralune stopped.

Nereth did not open her eyes.

“Smaller teeth?” Seralune asked.

Nothing answered.

She shifted Nereth’s weight higher. Pain travelled from her injured shoulder through the hand holding Nereth’s waist. Her bruised thigh tightened beneath them both, and the cut across her foot had soaked through the strip Nereth tied around it beneath the aqueduct.

The mountain path curved through low coastal trees. Their leaves moved beneath a wind that carried salt rather than ash.

Birds called from somewhere beyond them.

Ordinary birds.

No roots opened beneath their feathers. No dead mouths carried their voices through the earth. They moved between the branches because they wished to move, and when one disappeared, nothing beneath the ground followed it.

Seralune watched until Nereth’s knees folded.

Both women struck the path.

Stone entered Seralune’s already bruised thigh. Her injured palm caught their weight, opened again and left blood across the dust.

Nereth lay half across her lap.

“Nereth.”

The good eye opened by the width of a thread.

“You continue saying my name,” Nereth whispered, “as though I have misplaced it.”

Relief weakened Seralune before it strengthened her.

“Can you stand?”

“You disliked my answer the last time.”

“I am prepared to dislike it again.”

Nereth’s mouth moved faintly. “No.”

The honesty left Seralune nowhere gentle to place it.

She drew Nereth upright against her. The path ahead descended in long turns towards the first houses. Beyond them, masts rose above the harbour, their rigging drawn in black lines across the water.

The bells behind them had changed.

They no longer rang from every tower. One began. Two others answered. Silence held for several breaths before the sequence repeated farther down the mountain.

Orders travelling through sound.

Seralune did not know what they meant.

She placed Nereth’s arm across her shoulders again and stood.

This time Nereth’s feet dragged.

---

The first person they passed was running towards the mountain.

He wore a green working coat with one side fastened incorrectly and carried a wood axe despite there being no tree near the road large enough to require it. His gaze found the blood across Seralune’s face, travelled to Nereth and lifted towards the black smoke behind them.

“What happened?”

Seralune continued walking.

“The southern slope opened,” she said.

The answer stopped him more effectively than any order.

“Opened?”

Seralune did not have enough breath to explain a mountain to someone who could see it.

She passed him.

Others followed as the path became a road. A pair of harbour workers climbed with coils of rope over their shoulders. An elderly woman held a kitchen knife in one hand and the wrist of a frightened child in the other. Three men in light armour hurried north without horses, their green cloaks clasped before they had settled correctly across their shoulders.

No one stopped Seralune again.

The settlement had begun emptying itself towards the bells.

Its streets descended in narrow levels towards the harbour. White stone covered most of the walls, stained grey where rainwater found the same route year after year. Wooden balconies leaned above the road. Nets hung drying between them, heavy with beads of seawater that caught the late-morning light.

Nothing belonged to Seralune’s memory.

The windows were too broad. The roof tiles overlapped in an unfamiliar pattern. Even the lettering painted above the nearest store had changed—still Elven, but the strokes had become narrower and several endings had been removed.

She read the word twice before it settled.

**Waluna.**

The name gave the harbour shape.

It gave Seralune no knowledge of where to go inside it.

Nearly every face beneath the balconies was elven. The only human Seralune saw stood beneath a cooper’s awning with his hood raised despite the clearing sky, waiting for the road to empty before crossing it.

A wagon crossed the street ahead. Two young elves walked beside it, both guiding the load with Wind that moved in short, visible turns beneath the wheels.

Neither looked at the women collapsed against one another beside the wall.

Seralune drew breath to call after them.

Nereth’s body changed against her.

Not a seizure.

An absence.

The little weight Nereth had still carried disappeared from her legs. Her head rolled against Seralune’s shoulder, and her next breath did not come.

“Nereth?”

Seralune lowered her against the wall.

The good eye remained closed. Black-purple light passed beneath the other in a slow, thick pulse.

Seralune placed two fingers beneath Nereth’s jaw.

There.

Too fast.

Then nothing she could separate from her own shaking hand.

“Nereth.”

She brushed the singed hair away from the bite. The wound remained open, its edges dark and swollen. A clear fluid moved through the blood, too thick to run down Nereth’s face.

“Move your hand.”

The voice came from behind Seralune.

She turned.

An elf woman stood in the middle of the street carrying a wooden case against one hip. Copper-brown hair had escaped the knot at the back of her head and moved freely around a face made bright by the effort of hurrying. A green scarf crossed one shoulder.

Her eyes had already left Seralune.

They remained upon Nereth.

Seralune tightened her arm around Nereth. “Who are you?”

The woman placed the case upon the road and dropped to one knee. “I know how to help her. Move your hand and let me see the wound.”

She opened the case.

Small glass bottles occupied one side. Folded cloth filled the other beside a narrow knife, needles, cord and bundles of dried leaves bound by coloured thread.

Seralune did not move.

The woman looked up.

The brightness in her expression remained. The softness did not.

“Tell me what you know about the bite,” the woman said. Urgency had hardened her voice, but her hands remained open where Seralune could see them. “Then let me see it.”

Seralune looked at the wound.

At the dark branches moving beneath Nereth’s skin.

“I don’t know anything about it.”

“Then I need you to trust me.” The woman’s gaze dropped to Nereth’s unmoving face. “She does not have long for us to argue.”

Seralune removed her hand.

The woman’s fingers replaced it immediately. She tested Nereth’s pulse, opened the good eye and held a polished piece of metal near her mouth.

A thin cloud gathered upon it.

“Breathing,” she said. “Poorly. How long since the bite?”

“Several hours.”

“What did it look like?”

Seralune looked back towards the broken mountain.

“Something that should not have been alive.”

The woman followed her gaze for one heartbeat. Concern deepened the line between her brows.

“Whatever it was, this is not behaving like an ordinary bite.” She took a dark glass bottle from the case and held it beneath Nereth’s nose. “Help me lift her.”

“Where are you taking her?” Seralune asked. Suspicion kept her grip firm beneath Nereth’s shoulders.

“My treatment room is behind the chandlery. It is close, and I have what your friend needs.”

*Your friend.*

Seralune looked down at Nereth. She had known her little more than a day and could no longer bear the thought of losing her.

Seralune took her beneath the shoulders. The stranger lifted beneath the knees.

“Do not lift until I reach three,” the woman said, adjusting her hold beneath Nereth’s knees. “One. Two. Three.”

They lifted.

---

The room stood behind a chandlery close enough to the harbour that the tide moved beneath its floorboards.

Every wave entered through the wood as a low complaint.

**Krrrk.**

Shelves covered two walls. Bottles, cloth and dried plants occupied them without the disorder of Cyrandor’s service passages or the careful emptiness of the palace room. A narrow cot stood beneath the only window.

They placed Nereth upon it.

The woman pushed the remaining cloak from Nereth’s shoulders and stopped.

Burned cloth crossed the attendant’s back beneath Seralune’s bandage. The black-purple lines beneath her throat disappeared into the collar of the ruined dress. Bruising had spread along one side of her ribs, where the corruption had forced each breath wider.

The woman released a slow breath through her nose.

“You carried her down the mountain like this?” the woman asked. Concern had stripped the brightness from her face.

“There was no one else.”

“No one stopped to help?”

“They saw us.”

The woman’s mouth tightened. Whatever she thought of that answer, she kept it behind her teeth and turned towards the stove.

She crossed to a small iron stove, opened the vent and touched two fingers to the cold coals. Wind passed from her hand with a low whistle. It entered the stove, stirred beneath the ash and found a buried ember.

Orange light opened.

Wind.

Small. Controlled. Useful.

The woman set a kettle above it.

“What is your name?” she asked without turning.

Seralune’s hand found the chain beneath her grey overdress.

The closed lily pressed against her skin.

No one in Waluna had recognised her face. That did not mean they would fail to recognise her name.

“Sera.”

The missing syllables remained behind her teeth.

The woman glanced back.

If she heard the hesitation, she did not reach for it.

“And your friend?”

“Nereth.”

The woman repeated the name once, committing it to memory. Then she brought a bowl from the shelves and poured clear liquid into it.

“I am Nyana,” she said while washing her hands. “Stay close to Nereth. If she wakes, do not let her touch the wound.”

“Are you a physician?” Seralune asked, watching every movement of Nyana’s hands.

Nyana shook her head. “A medic.”

“Can you still help her?”

“I can clean the bite, lower her fever and keep her breathing while I learn what the poison is doing.” Nyana dried her hands upon a folded cloth. “That is what she needs now.”

The certainty in Nyana’s answer steadied Seralune more than a kinder promise would have.

Nyana returned to the cot.

“Her『Affinity』?” she asked.

“Fire.”

“Rank?”

“I don’t know.”

Nyana looked at her.

“You travelled together without knowing?”

“Only yesterday. Before we went beneath the mountain.”

Nyana glanced towards the window. Her mouth tightened, but she returned to Nereth without asking what had happened there.

Nyana placed two fingers against Nereth’s wrist.

“There is almost no mana left in her,” Nyana said, concern drawing her brows together.

“Almost none?” Seralune asked.

“I can barely feel it,” Nyana said.

Nyana laid a warm, damp cloth beneath Nereth’s eye. As she eased the dried blood from the wound, Nereth’s back lifted from the cot.

A sound caught behind her teeth.

Seralune reached for her.

Nyana’s forearm blocked the movement.

“At her shoulders,” Nyana instructed, guiding Seralune’s hands into place. “Hold her still.”

“You are hurting her.” Anger sharpened Seralune’s voice, born from having nothing else she could place between Nereth and the pain.

“I know. I’m sorry.” Nyana kept her eyes upon the bite. “There is dirt caught beneath the dried blood. I have to clean it.”

“Can’t it wait?”

“She’s already feverish, Sera. I don’t want to leave it.”

Seralune heard Nereth beneath the aqueduct.

*So he had to stay behind.*

She moved to the head of the cot and placed both hands upon Nereth’s shoulders.

“Do it.”

Nyana poured warm water over the bite.

Nereth screamed without waking.

The sound passed through Seralune’s hands. Her own injured shoulder tightened until she could no longer tell which pain belonged to whom.

Nyana worked.

She cleaned the wound, cut away burned cloth and pressed crushed leaves into the torn skin. No magic entered her hands. Nothing glowed. Her skill lived in the order of what she reached for and the speed with which she discarded anything no longer clean.

Nereth’s breathing settled into a shallow, regular rise. Seralune loosened her hold enough to let Nyana secure the bandage. Beyond its edge, the black-purple veins continued pulsing.

“Can you remove it?” Seralune asked.

Nyana washed her hands. The water turned pink, then grey where ash left Nereth’s skin.

“I do not recognise it. I can treat the fever and the wound, but I have no antidote for what is in her veins.” Nyana looked back at Nereth. “She is breathing more easily. That gives us time, not a cure.”

Seralune watched Nereth’s chest rise. “What do you need?”

“More medicine than I keep here. Time to watch her. Somewhere she can rest without the palace bells drawing everyone into the streets.”

Nyana looked towards the harbour window.

“My brother’s ship carries enough medicine for the crossing,” Nyana said. “It leaves before midday.”

Hope moved before caution could stop it.

“Take us to him,” Seralune said. Desperation gave the request the shape of an order.

Nyana studied her for a moment. “He will not want strangers aboard.”

“Ask him to come here, then. Please.” Seralune glanced at Nereth. “Let him see her before he decides.”

Resolve replaced the last of Nyana’s hesitation. She stepped into the lane and called to someone Seralune could not see.

“Find Neldor,” Nyana ordered. “Tell him I need him in the treatment room. Now.”

Footsteps broke into a run outside. Nyana returned to Nereth without waiting for an answer.

---

Nyana treated Seralune while they waited.

She pulled a stool close enough that Seralune could still reach the cot.

“Sit,” Nyana said.

Seralune looked towards Nereth. “I should remain beside her.”

“You will be beside her.” Nyana pointed to the blood seeping through the cloth around Seralune’s palm. “But you will be sitting while I stop that from opening again.”

Seralune sat.

Nyana cleaned the cut across her brow, rewrapped her palm and replaced the filthy strip beneath her foot. When she reached the shoulder, Seralune pulled away.

“We do not have time.”

Nyana kept one hand around her arm without forcing it back. “If that shoulder fails while you are carrying Nereth, both of you fall. Let me check it.”

Seralune’s jaw tightened. Every moment spent sitting felt like another moment in which the bells could find them, but Nyana had already been right once when Seralune’s fear resisted her.

“Be quick,” Seralune said.

Nyana pressed carefully along the shoulder until Seralune’s breath changed.

“Bruised. Possibly cracked near the joint. Nothing displaced.” Nyana looked towards Seralune’s thigh. “What happened there?”

“A piece of stone struck me.”

“Can you place your full weight upon it?”

“Enough to keep walking.”

“That isn’t quite what I asked.” Nyana waited while Seralune tested her weight, then helped her back onto the stool. “Tell me if it gets worse.”

Nyana opened the grey overdress enough to tie a support beneath it. Her hands slowed when they found the gown underneath.

The pale cloth had been made for Seralune’s body one thousand years ago. Its fastenings, hidden beneath the borrowed overdress, belonged to a court whose clothing had become history.

Nyana touched one seam, following the unfamiliar stitching with her thumb.

“Where did you get this?”

“My mother.”

That answer was true enough to hurt.

Nyana looked at Seralune’s face. The distance there must have warned her away from the next question, because she let the outer cloth fall across the gown.

“Keep the shoulder still until I return,” Nyana said.

The door opened before Seralune could decide whether Nyana believed her.

The man who entered carried the harbour with him.

Salt had dried white across his boots. A coil of rope hung over one shoulder, and his dark green coat remained open above a linen shirt darkened by old sweat at the collar. Wind had pushed his hair backwards so often that it no longer seemed willing to fall forwards.

His eyes found Nyana first.

Then Nereth.

Then Seralune.

The order removed any uncertainty about who mattered to him.

“No, Nyana.” Weariness roughened the man’s voice, but it was concern rather than annoyance that held his attention upon his sister.

Nyana remained beside the cot. “You have not heard her breathe.”

“The dock boy said you wanted space aboard my ship for two strangers.”

“I need the medical stores, and I need somewhere I can watch her through the crossing.” Nyana pulled the blanket lower, exposing the dark veins beneath Nereth’s throat. “Look at her before you refuse.”

Neldor did.

His jaw tightened.

“Moving her could kill her.”

“Leaving her here could do the same,” Nyana answered. Fear briefly entered her voice before discipline closed around it. “I can do more for her aboard.”

Seralune stood.

The room shifted beneath her. She waited until it returned.

“We need passage to Mydea.”

“We can work for the passage,” Seralune continued when he did not answer.

Neldor watched her steady herself against the stool. “You can hardly stand, and your friend cannot wake. You are not offering me workers. You are asking me to hide you.”

“The vessel sails under a royal contract,” Neldor said. His voice remained level, but one hand closed around the rope upon his shoulder. “Every crate is recorded. Every person aboard is named. If the quartermaster finds two hidden passengers, I lose the cargo and the ship. My crew may be taken with them.”

“No one is searching for us,” Seralune said.

The lie came more easily than her name had.

The mountain answered behind her with another distant bell.

Neldor looked towards it, then back at Seralune.

“Do not lie to me while asking me to endanger my crew,” he said. Anger did not raise his voice; it made each word more deliberate. “The southern slope has torn open. Every bell between here and the palace is sounding a different warning, and you arrived covered in its ash.”

“They were beneath it when it broke,” Nyana said.

“That explains the blood,” Neldor replied. His eyes remained upon Seralune. “It does not explain why you need to leave Natharul before the harbour receives clear orders.”

Seralune met his gaze.

“We have family near the northern harbour.”

“Names?”

Seralune hesitated. She had no name to give him.

“People who will know ours.”

Neldor lowered the rope from his shoulder and placed it beside the door. “You are asking me to risk everyone aboard for people you will not name.”

Seralune reached beneath the overdress.

The chain caught briefly against the support Nyana had tied around her shoulder. She drew it free. Silver appeared between her fingers.

The signet remained hidden inside her fist.

Her mother had worn it beside the south-garden fountain. Cyrandor’s family had kept it through ten centuries because a queen ordered them to believe her daughter might one day need it.

Seralune could buy a place upon the ship with silver.

She did not know whether she could buy back the only proof that her mother had waited for her awakening.

Her hand did not open.

Neldor watched the closed fingers.

“If you cannot release it, do not offer it.”

Seralune lowered her hand.

“I will repay you in Mydea.”

“With what?” Neldor asked. His doubt held no cruelty. He was measuring an offer made by someone who possessed almost nothing.

“I can read, write and keep accounts,” Seralune said. “Whatever else the ship requires, I can learn.”

“You have never worked aboard a ship.”

“No.” Seralune kept her back straight despite the room shifting again at its edges. “But if someone shows me, I’ll learn.”

Neldor’s gaze passed over her again. Not the injuries this time.

The way she stood.

The words she had selected.

“Who are you?” Neldor asked.

“Sera.”

“That is your name. It is not what I asked.”

Seralune felt her mother’s signet against her palm. “It is all I can safely give you.”

The truth inside the refusal remained between them.

Neldor looked away first, but only towards his sister.

“No.”

Nyana’s shoulders lowered, not in surrender but decision. “Then I will stay with them.”

He stared at her.

“The ship leaves before midday,” Neldor said. Disbelief drew him one step closer to his sister. “You would let the crew sail without its medic?”

Nyana did not look away. “I know.”

She placed two fingers against Nereth’s wrist again. “She is my patient. She stopped breathing in the street, and she may do it again. I will not leave her alone when she does.”

Neldor stepped closer.

“Nyana.” His voice dropped, carrying a warning that belonged to years Seralune had not witnessed.

Nyana met his gaze. “I know what I am asking you to risk. I also know what happens if I leave her here.” Her hand remained against Nereth’s wrist. “I need my brother to understand both.”

“You decided before you sent for me,” Neldor said.

Nyana glanced at Nereth.

“I knew I couldn’t leave her.” Nyana looked back at him. “I was hoping you would help.”

Neldor closed his eyes.

For several breaths, the harbour moved without him. Wheels crossed the boards outside. A man shouted for another coil. Rigging knocked softly against a mast.

**Tak. Tak-tak.**

His eyes opened.

“The medical store,” he said at last. “Both of you remain below until we pass the southern headland. Your names do not enter the crew book, and no one aboard hears more than they need to protect the ship.”

Relief loosened Nyana’s breath. She did not turn it into victory over him.

He looked at Seralune.

“At the Mydean harbour, you leave my ship. Until then, you remain in the medical store unless Nyana says otherwise.”

“I will repay what we owe,” Seralune said.

Neldor lifted the rope onto his shoulder. “Keep your friend alive through the crossing. If I still possess a ship when we reach Mydea, we can discuss what you owe me.” He opened the door. “We sail in less than an hour.”

---

They carried Nereth through the harbour beneath a canvas sheet.

Neldor had found a narrow litter used for injured sailors. Nyana held the front. Seralune reached for the rear, but Neldor looked at her shoulder and took it himself.

She walked beside Nereth instead, one hand beneath the canvas and two fingers resting against the pulse in her wrist.

It remained.

Weak.

Too quick.

Waluna had begun returning to its work.

The bells still called above, but grain could not remain beneath rain because a mountain had broken. Barrels continued rolling towards open holds. Nets rose dripping from narrow boats. Sailors lifted their faces whenever the highest bell sounded and returned to their ropes when no order followed it.

Neldor’s vessel waited near the end of the second pier.

It was larger than the fishing boats surrounding it, its dark hull built broad for cargo rather than speed. Three masts rose above it. Green canvas remained bound along the crossbeams while workers loaded crates through an opening near the stern.

A royal mark had been burned into each crate.

The sigil belonged to her house.

The lines had changed around it. The crown had gained another point, and the old leaves beneath it had become spears.

Seralune looked away before recognition reached her face.

A porter waited beside the next stack with one hand tucked beneath his arm. Grey cloth hung loosely from his shoulders. His other hand held a wooden tally towards a soldier seated behind a narrow desk.

“The quarterman sent me,” the porter said. “He said you could mark me finished.”

The soldier examined the tally. “Six loads remain.”

The porter drew his injured hand from beneath his arm. The fingers had swollen until they could no longer close. A strip of cloth crossed the palm, dark and wet where it passed beneath the thumb.

“It went under a barrel. I cannot hold the ropes.”

The soldier set the tally down. “Then carry crates.”

“May I have it bound first?”

Seralune stopped beside the litter. The porter kept his hand extended while the soldier reached for his quill.

“When the stores are aboard. Your shift does not end because your hand hurts.”

“Sir—”

“I can enter a refusal if you prefer.” The soldier looked up at him. “But then your custodian will have to explain why a Faint under his charge delayed the king’s supplies.”

The porter took back the tally.

Seralune drew breath. He needed a bandage and time to sit, and the man behind the desk had made both sound like things he had failed to earn.

She stepped towards him.

“Sera.” Nyana waited at the foot of the gangplank. “Her shoulder. Keep her from rolling while we lift.”

Neldor had turned the litter to clear the mooring ropes. Nereth lay beneath the canvas with her head tilted against the injured side. Seralune returned and steadied her. A small sound escaped Nereth when the litter rose.

The soldier’s quill scratched across the page behind them.

Seralune could still call him back. She could make the guards look at her, at Nyana, at the two women Neldor had agreed to hide. Her hand tightened against the litter until Nereth stirred beneath it.

“Gently,” Nyana said.

Seralune loosened her grip and walked beside them up the plank.

Below, the porter struggled to slide his swollen hand beneath a crate. The worker at its other end shifted closer to take more of the weight. Together they lifted it, the injured man keeping his eyes upon the boards while blood darkened the cloth around his palm.

Seralune held Nereth steady until they reached the deck. She had said nothing to the soldier.

---

The medical store had no window.

Crates formed a narrow wall around the cot Neldor’s crew lowered Nereth onto. The room smelled of dried mint, sealed oil and the damp wood of the hull. One lantern hung from a hook overhead, its flame moving whenever footsteps crossed the deck above.

Nyana secured bottles inside padded compartments.

Seralune sat beside Nereth with her knees nearly touching the opposite wall.

The ship announced every preparation through its body.

Rope tightened.

**Krrrk.**

A wooden block struck the mast above.

**KLAK.**

Men shouted to one another. Boots crossed from stern to bow. Somewhere beyond the hull, water folded repeatedly against the same boards.

Then Neldor’s voice reached through them all.

“Loose forward!”

The vessel moved.

Not much.

Enough for Seralune’s stomach to understand before her eyes could.

The floor shifted beneath the cot. Glass touched glass inside Nyana’s case. Nereth rolled towards one edge, and Seralune caught her shoulder before the movement pulled at the bandage.

Wind entered the sails.

**WHUMPF.**

Canvas opened above them.

The hull turned away from Waluna.

Seralune felt the direction change through the wall against her shoulder. The sea no longer struck them from the side. It passed beneath, lifted and released.

Waluna was falling behind them.

Nyana listened to the sounds overhead until some of the tension left her shoulders.

“He rushed the departure,” she said while grinding herbs into a shallow cup.

Seralune looked towards the ceiling. “Because of us?”

“Partly. Those bells will become orders once the right person reaches Waluna.” Nyana glanced at Nereth before adding, “We should be beyond the headland before they decide which ships to stop.”

“How long is the crossing?”

“About a week, if the wind holds.”

Seralune looked towards the wall as though Mydea might be visible through it. Cyrandor had given her a city to search beyond that sea.

“And from the northern harbour to Favale?”

Nyana’s hands stopped above the cup.

Seralune heard the mistake after it had left her.

Nyana lowered the pestle. “You told Neldor your family lived near the harbour.”

“They do not,” Seralune admitted.

Disappointment replaced the easy warmth in Nyana’s face. “My brother risked his ship and every person aboard it for you.”

“He would have refused if I told him where we were going.” Seralune heard the defence hardening her voice and hated that she still believed it.

“You don’t know that. You never gave him a chance.” Nyana put the pestle down. “And if the harbour officials ask where you’re going? What is he supposed to tell them?”

“I was trying to protect her,” Seralune said, looking towards the cot.

“So was he, Sera. There are people aboard who depend on him.”

Seralune’s first answer rose from pride. She let it die before it reached her mouth.

“You are right,” Seralune said.

Nyana watched her for another moment, then resumed grinding. “Tell me the truth now. Where are you going?”

“Favale.”

“Then you have another week overland after we reach the northern harbour—if you have coin, shoes and a road that is not occupied by soldiers. Longer if you possess none of those.”

Seralune looked at her bare feet.

Nyana followed her gaze.

“You will not cross Mydea barefoot,” Nyana said. Practical concern began repairing what the lie had damaged. “We will find shoes before you leave the ship.”

“Why did you help us?” Seralune asked. “You did not know either of us, yet you argued with your brother and risked your place aboard his ship.”

Nyana kept her eyes upon the herbs. “I did not decide all of that when I saw you. Nereth stopped breathing, and everyone else kept walking. So I stopped.”

“And everything after?” Seralune asked.

“I did not think as far as the ship.” Nyana looked towards her patient. “First she needed to breathe. Then she needed a room and medicine. By the time Neldor arrived, leaving her felt too much like choosing what happened to her.”

Nyana mixed the herbs with water and covered the cup for when Nereth woke. As the ship passed beyond the shelter of the harbour, she checked the bandage and tucked a folded coat between Nereth and the cot’s wooden edge.

The hull rose beneath a longer wave. Seralune steadied Nereth with one hand and waited for the next breath. It came without help.

Above them, the crew settled into Neldor’s orders. No one called for the ship to turn.

---

The physician had said Cyrandor would live.

Thaeroval had made him repeat it.

The old man remained beneath guard in a locked infirmary, the stump below his elbow bound tightly in clean linen. No one would question him until he woke. No one would move him without Thaeroval’s order.

It was not forgiveness.

The distinction mattered to Thaeroval.

It would not matter to Cyrandor.

He entered the southern aqueduct alone.

The stair Seralune had taken no longer reached it. The eruption had torn the lower passages apart and folded three levels of stone into the route. Thaeroval descended through the opening left behind.

Dust gathered upon his armour.

The sword remained inside its sheath.

He did not need to draw it.

Where fallen stone blocked the way, he placed one hand against it and found the fractures already travelling through its weight. Where the path ended, he stepped through chambers the mountain had opened for him.

The first corpse waited beneath an arch.

It wore a worker’s tunic so old that cloth had become part of the pale roots passing through it. The growth around its throat had burned away. Its mouth remained open above a tongue blackened by fire.

Thaeroval stopped.

Another body lay beyond it.

Then dozens.

Some had fallen where the roots released them. Others remained fixed inside the walls, their limbs visible through ash. None belonged to soldiers sent below after Seralune. The metal upon them had lost every mark by which a house, trade or century might be named.

They had been beneath Natharul for longer than anyone remembered to search.

The bells reached the aqueduct through broken earth.

Muted.

Accusing.

Thaeroval continued.

The remains of the rooted creatures covered the channel ahead. White fire had opened them from within. Their hollow limbs had collapsed into rings of ash. Black-purple residue remained upon the stone around several, pulsing faintly before his approach caused it to withdraw into the cracks.

He had never seen it.

That frightened him less than the fact that it had grown beneath his sister’s prison.

The lower wards had carried away what the seal could not hold. Thaeroval had known that much.

He had never asked what waited at the end.

Heat strengthened as he entered the ruined basin.

Four channels had once divided there. Three were open to the sky now. The fourth remained packed with roots burned to white ash.

At the centre, the stone was green.

Thaeroval stopped at its edge.

A perfect circle remained untouched inside the destruction. Moss covered its floor. One white flower stood near the boundary, its petals folded beneath the late-morning sun.

Everything around it had burned.

Nothing within it had.

He knew that shape.

Not the aqueduct.

Not the flower.

The refusal.

Power reached Seralune and became something else before it could touch her.

He had believed that ended.

The thought did not contain a face. It contained a place where one should have been.

Thaeroval stepped into the circle.

No memory answered.

His hand found the sword at his side.

For the first time since the mountain broke, the bells above him seemed very far away.

Blood marked the stone beyond the circle.

Not much.

A narrow line dried near a piece of burned grey cloth. Farther up the slope, two sets of tracks entered the fallen earth. One remained deep and uneven. The other disappeared often, leaving only the deeper set where one body had carried the weight of both.

Seralune had climbed.

Someone had climbed with her.

Thaeroval followed.

The crater opened above Waluna.

Sunlight struck his armour as he reached the torn edge of the mountain. Below, the harbour extended into a sea crossed by white sails. Fishing vessels crowded the water near the piers. Merchant ships moved beyond them, some turning east, others south before the coastline hid their course.

Seralune was ahead.

He could not see her.

The broken wards had left her passage through the mountain like heat held in stone. At the crater’s edge, the residue turned towards the harbour and scattered across the water.

Her impossible mana had left the land.

That was all the trail could tell him. Beyond the coast, wind and salt had thinned it until every ship crossed it.

Thaeroval searched the sails.

Nine vessels moved upon the water beyond Waluna.

Four were fishing boats close enough for Thaeroval to distinguish figures upon their decks. Two merchant hulls had already passed the southern headland. Three others travelled east, one with green canvas full of wind.

Any one of them might carry her.

His hand closed around the sword.

He could cut the harbour.

The piers would open. Masts would fall. Every vessel still within the mouth would become wood upon water.

He could cut the wind across the headland.

Ships would lose their sails against the rocks. Sailors who had never spoken Seralune’s name would drown beside whatever vessel held her.

He could send the edge farther.

Far enough to reach the green canvas.

Far enough to reach his sister.

Thaeroval’s thumb pressed against the guard.

The sword did not leave its sheath.

He had spent one thousand years believing the choice he made for Seralune had been necessary.

He would not defend it by cutting through her now.

The green sail passed behind the headland.

Thaeroval said nothing.

Below him, Waluna’s harbour bell began to ring.

Nereth woke when Natharul had become a dark shape above the water.

Her good eye opened first.

It found the ceiling, the moving lantern and Nyana asleep upon a folded coat beside the medicine case. Then it found the rough blanket covering her and Seralune seated near the cot with one hand around a cup she had forgotten to drink from.

Nereth’s gaze narrowed.

“What did you promise?” Nereth asked.

The voice scarcely disturbed the room.

Seralune leaned closer, relief making her answer useless. “You are awake.”

“That was not my question,” Nereth whispered. Even half-conscious, impatience remained easier for her to show than fear.

Relief entered Seralune’s chest and found every injury waiting for it.

“Work. Repayment. That we would leave the ship at the northern harbour.” Seralune watched Nereth’s face for any sign that the price frightened her. “Nothing more was decided.”

Nereth considered this.

“You promised before learning what the work was.”

“You were unconscious,” Seralune said, a defensive edge entering her relief.

“That explains why no one stopped you. It does not make the bargain better.”

Nyana stirred beside the medicine case.

Nereth’s eye shifted towards her.

“Nyana,” Seralune said quietly. “The ship’s medic. She treated the poison.”

“Did she remove it?”

The black-purple marks answered with one slow pulse.

Seralune did not lie.

“No.”

Nereth closed her eye.

Her fingers moved against the blanket until they found the edge of the bandage beneath her left cheek.

“What happened after the fire?” Nereth asked.

“It destroyed the aqueduct,” Seralune answered. “Part of the mountain with it.”

Nereth’s fingers stopped against the bandage. “Did it reach anyone else?”

“I saw no one beyond the creatures in the aqueduct.” Seralune could not offer more certainty than that. “Waluna was still standing when we reached it.”

Nereth released the breath she had been holding. “I could not have made that fire.”

“I know.”

Nereth opened her eye again.

Nereth looked at the bruises upon Seralune’s throat, then drew her hand back beneath the blanket.

Neither woman looked away.

“We’re travelling to Mydea,” Seralune said. “Cyrandor told me to begin looking for my mother in Favale. I don’t know what we’ll find there.”

Nereth’s throat moved around a painful swallow.

“You intend to reach Favale alone, Your Highness?” Concern briefly overcame the exhaustion in Nereth’s voice.

“Nyana calls me Sera,” Seralune whispered, glancing towards the sleeping medic.

“You intend to reach Favale alone, Sera?” Nereth corrected, lowering her voice around the false name.

The correction came without effort.

Seralune looked at her.

“You owe me nothing,” Seralune said. “You nearly died because you followed me.”

Nereth’s good eye held hers. “Do you want me to leave?”

The question exposed the wish Seralune had hidden beneath concern.

“I want you to live.”

“That was not my question either.”

“No,” Seralune admitted. “I don’t. I’m frightened of what might happen to you if you stay with me.”

Nereth settled deeper into the pillow. Some of the strain left the corner of her mouth.

“I’m frightened too,” Nereth whispered. “And I would rather not wake up in Mydea and find you’ve gone without me.”

The ship lifted beneath them.

Water moved along the hull in one long rush.

Nereth’s fingers tightened weakly around the blanket.

“I’ll go with you to Favale,” Nereth said. “We can work out the rest when we get there.”

“Why?”

Nereth studied her with the single eye the poison had left clear. “You walked towards a creature because you thought it was suffering. Then you carried me down a mountain on a bleeding foot. Someone should remain close enough to tell you when compassion has become stupidity.”

Seralune heard something gentler beneath the bluntness, whether Nereth intended her to or not.

“Then you may tell me,” Seralune said. “I cannot promise I will enjoy hearing it.”

“You rarely do.”

Seralune stayed beside her. For the first time, the week ahead contained something beyond waiting for Nereth to wake.

Nyana opened one eye. Relief softened her sleep-creased face when she saw Nereth watching her.

“Good,” the medic murmured, pushing herself upright and reaching for a covered cup beside the case. “If you can argue, you can swallow. I need you to drink this.”

Nereth looked towards the cup Nyana held out.

“What is it?”

“For the fever and the pain,” Nyana said. “It tastes worse than it smells. I will not lie to you about that.”

Seralune helped Nereth lift her head.

The medicine smelled bitter even before the cup reached her. Nereth swallowed once, stopped and fixed Nyana with the full force of the eye that remained clear.

Nyana steadied the cup rather than forcing it closer. “All of it, if you can.”

Nereth looked at Seralune.

“You trust her?” Nereth asked Seralune.

Seralune looked at the woman who had stopped when everyone else continued walking. “Yes.”

Nereth obeyed with the expression of someone recording a debt.

When the cup was empty, exhaustion closed her eye again. Her breathing remained shallow, but each breath arrived without being summoned.

Seralune waited until sleep took her.

Nyana drew her stool beside the cot. “I will stay with her. You can go up for a little air.”

Seralune hesitated, watching Nereth breathe. Then she left her in Nyana’s care and climbed to the deck.

The sun stood high above the sea.

Waluna had disappeared behind the headland. Natharul had not. The kingdom rose from the water in distant layers of mountain and white stone, its highest towers too far away to distinguish from the light around them.

Seralune rested both hands upon the rail.

Her brother was somewhere within that distance.

Perhaps beside Cyrandor.

Perhaps beneath the mountain, looking at what had grown there.

Perhaps still standing where she had left him, one hand raised towards a sister who had chosen not to take it.

She had escaped him.

The thought brought no victory.

Only the knowledge that he had loved her enough to call a locked room protection, and not enough to let the life beyond it belong to her.

Wind crossed the deck.

Above, green canvas tightened between the masts. Neldor stood near the stern with one hand lifted, Wind moving from his fingers into the sail in narrow, controlled streams.

The vessel turned farther from Natharul.

Seralune touched the signet beneath her clothing.

Her mother’s lily remained there.

Behind her, the kingdom diminished without disappearing.

Ahead, Mydea remained beyond the horizon.

No wall framed the distance between them.

No one had chosen how much of it Seralune was permitted to cross.
