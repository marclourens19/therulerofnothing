# What Remained

Redd was there whenever Alaric remembered waking.

Once, with a cup held against his mouth and a hand beneath his head. Water had escaped down his chin. Redd had wiped it away with the corner of a cloth and waited before letting him drink again.

Another time, Alaric had found himself sitting upon the edge of the bed, unable to remember how he had reached it. His foot rested upon Redd’s knee. The cloth around his ankle was coming loose beneath patient fingers.

“Leave that to me. You’ll have both hands bleeding again.”

He remembered obeying.

There had been darkness beyond the window. Then light. Wena’s weight against the bed frame, a bowl upon his lap, someone reminding him to swallow what was already inside his mouth before trying to speak.

Now the light lay amber across the ceiling, and Redd sat upon the chest with his back against the wall. His chin had fallen towards his green waistcoat. One hand hung open beside his knee.

Alaric watched him breathe.

He knew this room.

The sword remained above the chest. The table stood too close to the bed. Beneath the front window, Silas sat with one boot braced against the wall and his bandaged hand resting upon his thigh. His eyes were closed, but they opened when Alaric moved.

“How long?” Alaric asked.

His voice came out rough with sleep.

Silas lowered his boot. “Two days.”

Alaric turned towards the window.

“Since we got here?”

“Since you lay down.”

The answer made the scraps of light and darkness rearrange themselves. Alaric remembered the cup again. Had he thought each waking belonged to the same afternoon?

“We were going to leave.”

“You couldn’t get your boot on that evening. Yesterday you made it to the door and needed Redd to bring you back.” Silas sat forwards carefully. “We waited.”

Redd stirred upon the chest. His eyes opened, found Alaric upright and stayed open.

“You’re making more sense,” he said, rubbing one hand down his face. “That’s encouraging.”

“What was I saying?”

“Mostly that you could manage things.” Redd pushed himself up. “Usually while I was managing them for you. Do you want water?”

Alaric nodded. When Redd brought the cup, he closed his own hands around it. The fresh cloth across his palms pulled against tender skin, but his fingers held.

Redd did not release the cup until he was certain.

“Have you been here the whole time?” Alaric asked.

“It is my house.” The answer came with a small smile. Then Redd noticed that Alaric had not smiled back. “Silas took over when he was here. I’ve slept.”

Alaric looked down at the water.

He remembered being angry inside Darcy’s cell because she could not leave with him. He remembered expecting everyone who helped him to want something. Redd had wanted to see a battlefield. Instead, he had spent two days holding a cup for someone who kept spilling it.

“Thank you.”

Redd touched the rim with one finger. “Finish that. You can thank me by not falling over when I turn around.”

Alaric drank the remaining water and handed the cup back. Redd set it upon the table.

Wena rose from beside the bed. Her ears lifted as Alaric lowered a hand towards her, and she pressed her head into the space beneath his fingers. Her fur was warm. When he moved the blanket, she stepped back to let him put his feet down.

His ankle had changed colour.

The swelling no longer hid its shape entirely, though the skin above the wrapping remained dark. Alaric tested the floor with his heel. Pain answered, duller than he remembered, and he waited until it settled before placing the rest of his foot down.

Silas watched without reaching for him.

Alaric stood.

His ribs tightened. For a moment, the room moved a little farther away. He kept one hand against the bed frame and let the feeling pass.

Then he took a step.

Not far. Not well.

But the ankle held.

Redd drew the chair out of his way. Alaric reached the table, rested there, and returned before either man asked him to. Sitting hurt almost as much as standing had, but he sat without being caught.

“Better,” Silas said.

Alaric nodded, breathing carefully through the ache beneath his shoulder.

“You went out.”

“Last night.” Silas shifted his right hand higher upon his knee. “I went as far as the farm.”

“What did you see?” Alaric asked.

“Soldiers. Mydeans on the road, elves closer to the field. Wagons coming in while I watched. They’ve cut paths through the wheat and put lamps around the larger wrecks.”

“Are they taking them away?”

“Not while I was there.”

Alaric pressed his foot against the floor again. Gerolt’s token rested in the pocket at his hip, its edge catching between cloth and skin as he leaned forwards.

“I want to see it.”

Silas’s expression did not change. “You could barely stand a moment ago.”

“I know.” Alaric forced himself not to look away. “I’m not asking you to take me through the soldiers. I want to see what’s there. Where they are.”

He had meant to stop speaking. Instead, the rest came out more quietly.

“It’s the only place I know I was before any of this.”

Silas studied him for a while. Behind him, the light beneath the shutter had narrowed to a thin yellow line.

“There’s a bank above the field,” he said. “Trees behind it. We can look from there without walking into their lamps.”

Alaric rested his hand upon the bed beside him.

“Tonight?”

“After dark. Slowly. And if that foot won’t carry you, we turn back while we still can.” Silas leaned closer. “We’re only going to look. I need you to understand that before we leave.”

Alaric thought of the machines, of Darcy drawing a dark circle inside another upon the back of a sheet.

Then he thought of the cabin.

“I agree.”

---

Redd cut the rough end from an ash stick while the last daylight remained. He shaved away a knot near the top, tested the place with his palm and wrapped it in a strip of old linen before passing it to Alaric.

“Try that.”

Alaric planted it beside the bed. With the stick in his left hand, he could take some weight off his foot without lifting the injured shoulder.

“Too long?” Redd asked.

“No. It helps.”

“Good. I can make it shorter, but putting the end back on would take more skill than I’ve got.”

He collected the shavings and tipped them onto the hearth. Alaric ate the bread Redd had put aside for him, slowly enough that no one reminded him to. Across the room, Silas fastened the stolen sword belt around his borrowed coat. He used his left hand and his teeth to settle the loose end through its loop.

The right hand remained wrapped. He could bend the fingers a little now, but stopped before they closed.

Alaric’s boots waited beneath the table. Redd had dried them and loosened their fastenings. He helped Alaric work the swollen foot into its boot, pausing whenever Alaric’s breath caught, and left that side looser than the other.

The charcoal messenger’s coat came from the bundle upon the chest. Alaric put it on over the borrowed shirt and trousers. The familiar high collar brushed his jaw, and for an instant the room smelled of the river despite the dry cloth.

He pulled the front closed.

Redd folded Alaric’s black garments back into their covering and packed them with the remaining provisions. A second small bundle held the clean dressings Marta had left. He added a stoppered water bottle, tightened the straps of his travelling sack and set it beside the door.

“Those stay,” Silas said when Redd reached towards the covered armour.

Redd lifted one corner of the sacking and considered the bent breastplate.

“I wasn’t going to wear it. I was wondering what to say if anyone asks why I own it.”

“Don’t let them see it.”

“That was my first thought as well.”

Redd pushed the plates against the wall beside the hearth, tucked the remaining glove beneath the sacking and covered the last exposed edge. When he straightened, his attention settled upon the sword above the chest.

He took it down carefully.

Alaric had expected him to check the blade. Redd only pressed a thumb against the repaired mouth of the scabbard before fastening the belt around his waist. He adjusted it twice, settling the weapon where it would not strike his knee.

Wena came to the door before any of them called her.

Redd found a length of soft rope in the kitchen and tied it to Wena’s collar. He tested the knot, then offered the free end to Alaric.

“Near the roads,” Redd said. “She can smell something we can’t and be halfway towards it before we notice.”

Alaric passed the rope to Silas instead.

“I can’t hold her if she pulls.”

Silas took it without comment. Wena followed his hand with her nose, then waited beside Alaric again.

Outside, the remaining light had withdrawn from the grass. Redd closed the cottage door and fastened a simple wooden catch upon its outer face. It would keep the wind from opening it. Alaric could see that it would do little else.

Redd tested it once.

“Ready?” he asked.

Alaric glanced at the dark window above the bed he had borrowed. The room beyond it was empty now.

“Yes.”

They left the cottage standing quietly among the others.

---

Kelmend remained behind the low ground for longer than Alaric expected. He could no longer see its walls, but light held above them, a faint glow against the underside of the clouds. The sound of a bell reached them after the path had narrowed to a track between hedges.

He tried to count the strokes and lost one beneath Wena’s breathing.

Silas did not ask him to hurry.

That helped until Alaric noticed how often the man shortened his own stride. Redd walked behind with the provisions, close enough to offer an arm where the ground dipped. Neither mentioned the pace. Alaric found that harder to bear than being told he was slow.

At the first low wall, he tried to step over without help.

His boot caught the upper stone.

Redd steadied him beneath the left elbow before the stumble reached his ankle. He waited while Alaric freed the boot, then stayed beside him until both feet were upon the ground again.

“Thank you,” Alaric muttered.

“You can stop saying it every time.” Redd shifted the sack higher upon his back. “Tell me before you try the next one. That would help more.”

Alaric nodded. At the next wall, he waited.

They were resting beneath a stand of hazel when lights appeared upon the road above them.

Silas lowered the hand holding Wena’s rope. Redd crouched, and Alaric sat back against the roots instead of attempting the same movement. Through the branches, he could see the road where it crossed a gap between two fields.

Horses came first. Then a wagon beneath a dark covering, followed by more riders.

Pale hair caught the lanterns. A voice travelled down the line, its syllables overlapping in a way Alaric remembered too well. One rider turned in the saddle to answer. The wagon continued without slowing.

No one searched the hedge.

Alaric kept waiting for a face wrapped in white cloth. The last rider passed without one.

Silas waited until the wheels could no longer be heard before standing.

“More of them than last night,” he murmured.

“All going the same way,” Redd said.

He had stopped smiling. His fingers rested against the strap across his chest, well clear of the sword.

Alaric pushed himself up with the stick. “Towards the farm?”

“That road reaches it,” Silas answered. “We’ll stay below it.”

He led them away from the rise and along the lower edge of the hazel. The detour took them through grass wet enough to darken their boots, but no more lanterns crossed their path. After a while, running water began to fill the spaces between their footsteps.

Alaric knew the sound before the trees opened.

His hand tightened around the stick.

---

The river was wider here.

It divided around a low island covered in willow, the two channels spreading across pale shelves of stone before joining again farther down. Moonlight appeared whenever the clouds thinned, catching the broken white edges of the current.

Alaric stopped above the bank.

For a moment, he could feel soaked cloth dragging against his legs. His body remembered that weight more clearly than the walk that had brought him here.

Silas pointed downstream.

“There.”

At first, Alaric saw only another line of disturbed water. Then he distinguished the tops of broad stones laid across the nearer channel. Some leaned against their neighbours. Others had sunk enough that water split around their edges, but their upper surfaces remained exposed.

The line reached the island. Beyond the willows, a second stretch continued towards the opposite bank.

“There used to be a road,” Silas said. “The bank went, and they stopped repairing this end after the bridge was built.”

Redd went to the bank’s edge and studied the descent. Roots held a narrow shelf where the earth had fallen away.

“You crossed here last night?” he asked.

“Both ways. Step where I step.”

Silas went down first with Wena. She resisted the sight of the water, spreading her forelegs until he stopped pulling and crouched beside the first stone. He waited with the rope slack.

Wena smelled the air. Then the stone.

Her first paw touched it reluctantly. The second followed. Silas rose and led her towards the island, letting her choose her footing while he kept her from straying to the edges.

Redd had remained beside Alaric.

“I can take the stick while you come down,” he said.

Alaric gave it to him. He turned sideways and used the roots for balance, lowering his good foot onto the shelf before bringing the other after it. His shoulder tightened when he reached too far. Redd told him to stop, moved closer, and steadied his left arm until he could let go of the root.

No one fell.

Alaric stood at the crossing and waited for his breathing to understand that.

The first stone was broad enough for both boots. Redd returned the stick, then followed a pace behind as Alaric tested the next surface with its end.

Water filled the gap.

It was only a gap. He could step across it.

He watched the water anyway.

“Keep your eyes on the stone,” Redd said quietly. “The water keeps moving. That doesn’t.”

Alaric lifted his gaze and placed his foot where the stick had rested.

The stone held.

They crossed slowly. Once, spray struck the side of Alaric’s boot, and he stopped so abruptly that Redd almost touched his back. But no water entered above the leather. The cloth around his ankle remained dry.

On the island, Wena pressed her nose against his knee and waited until he touched her head. Silas let them rest among the willows before leading them over the second channel.

The far bank was lower. Alaric stepped onto grass without noticing that he had reached it until the stick sank into earth.

He turned towards the river.

The stones remained where he had left them.

Silas was watching his face. “We’ll come back this way if we need to.”

Alaric nodded. He could not have answered while he was still swallowing against the tightness in his throat.

Redd took the water bottle from the sack and passed it around. They drank, checked that the wrappings were dry and continued upstream beneath the trees.

This time, the river stayed beside them.

---

The smell reached them before the lights.

Alaric had thought he remembered it. He had carried pieces of it into the inn and the fort. It had followed him into Redd’s bed whenever his thoughts returned to the dagger. But the air moving through these trees was thicker, sweet beneath the smoke in a way that made his mouth fill with saliva.

He stopped to swallow.

Silas waited beneath the next tree. Redd took the bottle out again, but Alaric shook his head. He could not imagine putting anything else inside his mouth. Redd returned it to the sack.

Wena had raised her nose.

She pulled once against the rope, then again when Silas did not follow. Her ears pointed towards a break in the trees, and a low sound began in her throat.

Alaric moved beside her.

“I know,” he whispered, though he did not know whether he did.

She pressed forwards until his hand reached her neck. Then she turned against him, briefly, before trying to face the same direction again.

Beyond the trees, light lay against the underside of smoke.

Silas guided them away from it, following a shallow fold in the ground. They climbed slowly, using roots where the soil had slipped. Twice, Alaric needed to stop. The second time he said so before Redd touched him.

No one objected.

The fold ended behind a bank overgrown with alder and thorn. Through the branches, Alaric could see a pale tent roof and the upper part of something metallic.

Silas turned back.

“Leave the sack here. Nothing above the bank unless I tell you.”

Redd lowered the provisions into the hollow behind the roots. Alaric laid the ash stick beside them. Silas secured Wena’s rope to the low trunk of an alder, leaving enough length for her to settle in the hollow but not reach the bank’s crest.

She tested it immediately.

Alaric crouched beside her with more difficulty than he wanted either man to see. He kept his good foot beneath him, easing the other leg forwards until his ankle no longer carried his weight.

“Stay with me, girl.”

Wena’s nose moved against his collar. He rested his forehead against hers for one breath, then lifted his head as Silas beckoned.

Silas lay along the upper slope, his burned hand held clear of the soil. Redd settled below his left shoulder. Alaric eased himself down beside them, lying upon his uninjured side with his legs extending down the slope. Wena remained close enough for him to keep one hand upon her neck.

Gerolt’s farm lay beneath the lamps.

The wheat had been cut into roads.

Wide strips crossed the field, their stalks crushed beneath wheels and hooves. Tents occupied ground where the crop had remained untouched when Alaric fled. Soldiers moved between them carrying poles, coils of rope and lamps whose pale light did not tremble like flame.

The dead remained beyond the tents.

Some had been laid in rows near the edge of the destruction. Others still lay where Alaric remembered them, too numerous and too tangled beneath the wrecks to distinguish one body from another at this distance.

He searched for the horse with its forelegs stretched towards a stride it had never completed.

He could not find it.

Something in him had expected it to be waiting exactly where he had left it. The thought made him ashamed. Soldiers were walking through the field now. They lifted things, moved them, covered them. Whatever Alaric had failed to understand was not being kept untouched for his return.

Then he found the cabin.

It stood to their left, beyond an open stretch between two tents. At first, he recognised only the stone hearth. The roof had fallen around it. Black beams crossed the place where the table had been, and one surviving portion of wall leaned inwards beneath its own weight.

There should have been a window beside it.

Gerolt had stood there with one hand braced against the window frame.

Alaric’s fingers closed in Wena’s fur.

He could remember the old man’s mug more clearly than the shape of the cabin from outside. The dent near its rim. His broad hands around it. The way he had set it down and kept holding the handle after the mug had reached the floor.

He could not picture it lying among those beams.

“Alaric.”

Silas had turned towards him.

“Keep your head down.”

He lowered it. He had not noticed himself rising.

Wena made a thin sound beneath his hand. Alaric rubbed the place between her ears, but she continued to face the hearth.

He had been inside that house for less than a day.

Gerolt had lived there.

The difference would not leave him alone. There had been mornings before Alaric, work waiting in the fields, a dog beneath the table and food Gerolt had expected to eat himself. He had brought a stranger inside, and now soldiers stepped over the remains of his walls without slowing.

If he had left Alaric where he found him—

The thought tightened around his throat.

Another room followed it. Stone beneath his shoulder. A man trying to force his wrist down. His own hands pushing because he wanted to breathe.

Alaric opened his fingers carefully.

Wena’s fur sprang back between them.

“Can you see him?” he whispered.

Silas followed his attention towards the cabin.

“No.”

Alaric tried to search the ground around the hearth. There were too many dark shapes beneath the broken wood.

“We cannot go down there now,” Silas said, more quietly.

“I know.”

He did know. He had agreed before leaving. But agreeing inside a room had not required him to look at the place where Gerolt might still be lying.

Alaric lowered his hand to the rope and made himself remain beside Wena.

Farther into the field, a lamp moved across seamless silver.

Pain tightened behind his eyes.

He shut them at once. For a moment, the pressure felt as though something had been placed inside his skull while he slept and was now being pressed against the bone.

His heartbeat struck through it.

**THOOM.**

Alaric gripped his own sleeve instead of reaching for his head. He waited with his mouth open, allowing each breath to enter without forcing it.

When he opened his eyes again, the lamp had moved on.

The machine remained.

The ache stayed behind his eyes. He waited for it to ease before looking again.

Beside him, Redd drew breath to speak.

Then the ground began to hum.

---

At first, Alaric thought the sound belonged inside his head.

It was too low to separate from the pressure behind his eyes. He felt it through the ground beneath him, a slow vibration that made a dry leaf tremble against his sleeve.

Wena lifted her head.

Below, soldiers stopped working.

One soldier left a pole standing crooked in the earth and hurried towards the centre of the camp. Another began pulling people away from the broad cleared strip beside the tents. A mounted soldier in a silver-bordered cloak turned his horse towards the dark sky.

The stars disappeared above the farther trees.

Not behind cloud.

A dark curve advanced across them, edged with lights. Alaric followed it until the movement hurt his neck. Behind it came another sweep of metal, then another, each overlapping the one before.

He waited for a gap between the vessels.

There was none.

The ship at Fort Kelmend had strained against its cables and chains like something the town was afraid to release. He had been able to see both ends of it. Here, armour continued beyond the branches whichever way he turned his head.

Its leading edge had passed over the far fields before the rear of the hull emerged above the trees.

All of it belonged to one ship.

The body rose broad and heavy above an underside crowded with curved ribs. Some enclosed deep recesses where blue-green light travelled through the dark. Others swept down into armoured housings, their pointed ends hanging beneath the hull like the towers of a fortress built towards the earth.

Along the outer flanks, thick metal mouths projected between the plates. Each sat within a ringed mounting, angled away from the machinery around it. Alaric remembered the opening upon the dead machine below. These were unbroken, arranged in rows.

The hum deepened.

**KRRRMMMM.**

Air pressed through the alders. Leaves turned their pale undersides towards the ship, then flattened against the branches. Alaric lowered his head as dust and fragments of dry grass swept over the bank.

Wena pushed against his chest.

He closed his arm around her shoulders and kept his face against her fur until the first rush passed.

When he could see again, the vessel had moved beyond the nearer trees. Its stern followed for so long that he had time to watch a soldier run between two tents before the last armoured projection cleared the treetops.

From the bank, Alaric could follow the camp’s lamps along the cut roads. The ship extended beyond them in both directions. Its underside stretched above several fields, and the tents beneath it looked like scraps of pale cloth.

Gerolt’s cabin had seemed small when the soldiers surrounded it. Now Alaric had to search between their lights to find the hearth again.

Redd’s mouth had opened slightly.

Silas pressed his left hand against the bank and drew himself lower.

“That’s one of Natharul’s royal warships,” he murmured. “Stay beneath the branches.”

Alaric tried to lower his head without losing sight of it.

The ship slowed above the far side of the camp. Blue-green light brightened between its ribs, spilling over the layered armour. Its forward movement dwindled until the same row of lights held above the same trees.

It remained there, high above the ground.

The air against the bank eased. The hum did not. Alaric could feel it through his ribs whenever he drew breath.

Below the hull, a line of white appeared.

It widened as two curved plates drew apart. For a moment, Alaric could see only light inside the opening. Then something dark detached itself from it and began descending.

Another vessel.

It looked small enough to fit between two of the larger ship’s hanging ribs. As it came lower, its windows separated into individual lights, and Alaric made out an enclosed cabin, a pointed prow and the short armoured fins curving along either side. It was longer than Gerolt’s house.

Above it, the opening closed. The immense hull remained motionless against the sky.

The smaller craft crossed towards the cleared strip beside the tents. Soldiers waved the last wagon away and drew back behind a line of lamps. Short metal supports unfolded beneath the descending body.

**KLANG.**

The last support locked into place. Dust rose as the craft settled, rolling outwards across the cut stalks. The soldiers waiting beyond the lamps lifted their cloaks against it, then stepped forwards when the air cleared.

The warship remained overhead. Its low throb carried through the orders passing between the tents.

A door opened in the smaller vessel’s side, and a short stair lowered towards the ground. Light fell across the steps. Figures moved inside the cabin.

Two descended ahead of the others.

Their armour caught the lamps in narrow white lines. More followed, forming a path between the foot of the stair and the waiting soldiers. A standard opened above them, dark green around a pale branching shape. Gold formed a closed crown above it.

Silas went still.

“What?” Alaric whispered.

“The king’s standard,” Silas answered quietly.

Another figure appeared at the top of the stair.

He wore a long dark mantle over armour that reflected the light without shining as brightly as the guards’. Pale hair lay against its collar. A narrow gold band caught the light across his brow when he turned towards the field.

The soldiers below him bowed.

Not all at once. The elves moved first, followed a moment later by the Mydeans waiting beside them. One human soldier remained upright long enough for another soldier behind him to touch his sleeve.

Then he bowed too.

Alaric stared at the descending figure.

He had known kings existed. The word belonged to crowns and kingdoms and all the things other people seemed to understand without needing them explained. It had not belonged to a man walking towards Gerolt’s wheat.

The king reached the ground. The soldier in the silver-bordered cloak had dismounted. He approached with his head lowered and one arm extended towards the field. The king listened briefly, then passed him.

Everyone turned to follow.

A scraping sound came from beside Silas.

Alaric turned.

Redd’s hand had closed around his sword.

---

The blade had emerged no farther than the width of his palm. Even so, light caught it through the leaves.

Redd did not seem to notice.

He was staring down the slope, his lips drawn back from his teeth. His left hand pressed into the ground as he began to rise. Soil gathered against his fingers. A small stone rolled uphill towards his wrist.

Alaric felt the earth shift beneath his knee.

“Redd—”

Silas moved before the name had properly left him.

He drove into Redd from the side, catching the back of his coat with his left hand and forcing him below the bank’s crest. Redd struck the soft earth with a breath that broke in his throat. Silas followed him down, his left forearm across Redd’s upper chest and his burned hand held tight against his own body.

The partly drawn sword scraped through the grass.

Redd tried to push himself up.

“Stay down,” Silas hissed.

His voice carried more anger than Alaric had heard since the fort.

Redd’s fingers dug into the soil. A narrow ridge lifted beneath his palm, no higher than the roots around it, and Alaric felt Wena tense against his arm.

He held her close before she could make a sound. Her rope remained fixed to the tree, but he could feel the breath gathering inside her chest.

“Quiet, girl. Please.”

Overhead, the warship’s deep hum held steady. A voice called from beside the smaller craft’s stair. No one upon the lit strip faced the bank.

Silas pressed Redd harder into the earth.

“You draw that sword and you get all of us killed. Put it away.”

“He gave the orders.” Redd’s voice was low and strained, each word forced between his teeth. “The attacks. All of it. He sits there giving orders, and—”

He tried to wrench his shoulder free.

Silas stopped him with the weight across his chest.

“You cannot reach him. His guards can reach us.” The words came close to Redd’s face. “Put it away.”

Redd’s right hand trembled around the hilt. The ground beneath his left moved again.

Alaric remembered those fingers steadying the cup against his mouth. Redd had waited when he could not swallow. He had stayed when Alaric slept.

Now Alaric was frightened to move near him.

“Redd,” he said quietly. “Wena’s going to bark.”

The man’s eyes moved towards him.

Alaric kept his hand upon Wena’s neck. “Please stop.”

For several breaths, Redd did nothing.

Then his left hand opened.

The raised soil broke apart beneath it. Small stones settled into the hollow his fingers had made. He lowered his eyes to the sword, and the blade slid back into its sheath with a sound almost lost beneath the throb overhead.

Silas did not release him immediately.

“Are you staying down?”

Redd nodded once.

Only then did Silas lift his forearm. He shifted back carefully, his breath catching when the dressing around his right palm brushed his coat.

Redd remained where he had been pressed into the bank. Dirt clung to his cheek and the open edge of his waistcoat. He wiped his left hand upon the grass, but kept the other over the sheathed sword.

Alaric wanted him to take his hand off the hilt.

He did not know how to ask.

After a while, Redd moved the hand himself and laid it flat beside the other.

No one spoke.

Wena’s breathing eased against Alaric’s sleeve. Silas checked the field before allowing either man to move back towards the narrow opening in the thorn.

Redd came last.

---

The king had reached the dead.

He walked along one of the cut paths with the higher-ranking soldier a step behind his shoulder. Guards moved ahead of him, turning the lamps so their light fell where he was about to place his feet. Soldiers stepped out of their path before the guards reached them.

Alaric could not hear the conversation.

He could see the soldier speaking, one hand indicating the rows of bodies, then the torn ground beyond them. The king continued walking. Once, he stopped beside something upon the earth and bent his head. After a moment, he moved on.

An attendant kept pace behind him with a pane of black crystal held inside a narrow silver frame. No marks showed beneath its surface.

The group turned towards the machine nearest the bank.

It stood at the edge of the devastation, half sunk upon one side. From where Alaric lay, its front remained in shadow. Then a guard carried a lamp around the far corner, and he recognised the long opening, blackened at the edges.

He had seen it while sitting in the blood beside Gerolt.

The ache behind his eyes sharpened. Alaric lowered his face against his sleeve until it eased, then lifted it again. He would not look towards the place where the green circle had been. He was not certain he could bear finding it changed—or finding it exactly the same.

Below, two soldiers moved aside to let the king approach the wreck.

The higher-ranking soldier was still speaking.

The king stopped before reaching them.

At first, Alaric thought something upon the ground had obstructed him. But the guard ahead continued to the machine without difficulty and raised his lamp. Pale light travelled across the silver side, uninterrupted by a seam.

The king did not follow.

The soldier took another step before noticing. He turned back at once. Whatever he said was lost in the low throb overhead.

The king lifted one hand.

The soldier’s pointing hand dropped.

Alaric shifted closer to the opening between the branches. He could not distinguish the king’s expression. The light lay behind him now, leaving his face dark beneath the narrow band at his brow.

But he could see that the raised hand remained in the air after everyone had stopped.

The attendant moved nearer with the framed crystal.

The king lowered his arm sharply. The attendant stopped as well.

For several breaths, nothing happened.

Then the king approached the machine alone.

He did not take the open path to its front. He moved along its side, passing the higher-ranking soldier without looking towards him, and stopped where the silver surface curved beneath a broken edge. He pointed. A guard brought the lamp closer.

Whatever lay there was hidden from Alaric by the wreck itself.

The king bent towards it. The soldier moved closer behind him.

The king reached out and rested his hand against the metal.

Then he stepped backwards.

Not the measured withdrawal of someone making room for a guard. His whole body moved, abruptly enough that the man behind him reached towards his arm. The king pulled away from that hand and took another step.

The guards closed around him.

Steel appeared beneath the lamps.

Alaric held his breath.

Nothing emerged from the wreck. No light opened inside it. The long black mouth remained empty, and the silver flank held the same pale reflection it had held before the king touched it.

Silas’s left hand tightened against the roots.

Beside him, Redd’s hands remained flat upon the ground.

The king stood between his guards, one arm held close to his side. The higher-ranking soldier asked something. Alaric heard the rise of the voice without catching a word.

No answer carried back to him.

Instead, the king pointed towards the machine. The soldier turned, hesitated, and took the lamp from the nearest guard.

This time, he went forwards alone.

The king stayed where he was.

Alaric’s fingers found Gerolt’s token through his pocket. He could still feel the pressure behind his eyes, but he no longer wanted to close them.

The soldier raised the lamp against the seamless silver.

Nothing in the machine had moved.
