# Into the Great Expanse

The road to Favale divided beneath Silas’s finger.

One black line continued east across the map before bending south. Another disappeared beneath a painted mass of green. The third had been drawn more faintly than either, as though the person who made the map had expected the ink to regret going there.

Silas held the curling parchment flat with his cup.

“The main road is the easiest,” he said. “It has wells, farms and somewhere to sleep every few miles. That also makes it the first place they’ll look.”

Alaric leaned closer. The satchel Silas had given him pressed between his coat and the edge of the table. Its strap crossed his chest beneath the dark travelling cloak Silas had bought from the innkeeper.

The wrapped orb inside had not moved since Redd transferred it from the sack.

No light had returned to its centre. No warmth reached through the cloth. It rested against him with the ordinary weight of glass and metal, as though it had never carried him into someone else’s room.

“How long would the road take?” Alaric asked.

“A little over a week if we had horses.” Silas shifted his finger along the black line. “Longer on foot. Longer still if we spend every mile avoiding people.”

Redd turned the map slightly towards himself. He had pushed his hood back far enough for dark hair to escape around his face, though the rest of him remained wrapped in a cloak that had once belonged to someone broader.

“And this cheerful green stain?” he asked.

“Inrandeel.”

“That’s a forest.”

“It’s a rainforest,” Silas corrected. He took a drink. “Twice the rain, three times the trees and enough things beneath them to make you miss soldiers.”

Redd examined the painted canopy. “Trees sound manageable.”

“Trolls?”

Redd studied the painted trees again. “Less manageable.”

“Forest imps travel in groups. There are things farther in that don’t have names outside Inrandeel because the people who meet them rarely come back to provide one.”

Alaric looked beneath the table. Wena lay across their feet with her nose near Redd’s boot. The bench, their cloaks and the table’s solid side concealed most of her from the room. One ear shifted when someone laughed near the hearth, but her eyes remained closed.

“You’ve crossed it before?” Alaric asked.

“Alone.” Silas tipped his cup towards Wena, then across the rest of the table. “I can hide one person from something I can’t kill. I can’t hide two Faints, a dog and myself while one of you argues that a troll might listen to reason.”

“Why am I the one speaking to the troll?” Redd asked.

“Because Alaric would apologise to it, and then we’d all die politely.”

Redd covered a laugh with his cup.

Alaric tried to smile with them. The word **Faint** remained after the joke had passed. Redd believed it described him. Anyone who saw two young men travelling under Silas’s protection would probably make the same assumption.

They would still be wrong.

Faint meant something existed inside a person, however weakly. Three drops of water. A piece of earth that moved when fear reached it. Alaric had felt Marta search for even that much and find nothing.

He pushed the thought aside and indicated the faded route.

“What’s along this route?”

Silas moved the cup from the map and flattened the corner with two fingers.

“The Great Expanse.”

Redd’s amusement disappeared. “That’s a real place?”

“You thought people invented it?”

“I thought people made it larger every time the story reached a new tavern.”

“They usually do.” Silas followed the faint line east. “The stories about this place don’t need much help.”

Around them, the unnamed inn carried on without noticing the map. A woman near the hearth argued about the price of a room. Two wagon drivers shared bread at the bar. The innkeeper wiped the same portion of wood while watching neither conversation closely enough to become involved.

The smell of cooked onions clung to the low ceiling. Alaric had eaten until the emptiness in his stomach stopped hurting. He had expected every mouthful to pull against his ribs.

It had not.

He drew a deeper breath beneath his cloak.

No pain met it.

Alaric sat straighter and waited for the ache to arrive late.

Silas tapped the faded route. “The Expanse is barren in places. Water is unreliable. Some of the life inside it grew wrong, and most of what lives there has learned that travellers carry food.”

“You’re describing Inrandeel with fewer trees,” Redd said.

“There are open stretches where we can see what’s coming. Inrandeel doesn’t give us that.”

“Have you crossed it?” Alaric asked.

“Twice, keeping to the outlying ruins. I wouldn’t take you through the middle.”

Redd traced the route without touching the parchment. “Does it reach Favale?”

“It brings us close enough to join the eastern road beyond the worst checkpoints—unless the paths have shifted since I last crossed it.”

“Paths shift?”

“Sand moves. Old stone gives way. People remove markers because they believe getting someone else lost will improve their own chances.” Silas folded one side of the map towards the middle, then opened it again. “We shouldn’t take it unless the road leaves us no choice.”

Alaric considered the three lines.

The road offered water, shelter and soldiers. Inrandeel offered cover from people and creatures Silas could evade only when travelling alone. The Expanse offered no safety at all, only a danger the search might refuse to follow.

“If you were looking for us,” Alaric asked, “where would you wait?”

“The road.”

“And if we weren’t there?”

“I’d send riders along it until someone found our tracks leaving.”

“Would you enter the Expanse?”

Silas studied the map. “Only if I knew you’d gone that way.”

Redd rubbed the side of his cup against his lower lip. “I could try keeping things away from us in the forest.”

“With Earth?” Silas asked.

“It works when it feels like it.”

“Trolls are famously respectful of effort.”

Redd lowered the cup. This time he did not answer with a joke.

“You really don’t think we’d make it?”

Silas turned towards him fully. “I could hide myself. With four of us, something would hear us sooner or later. And if it did, I’d need both of you able to run. Look at Alaric’s foot.”

Redd looked down at Wena. Her head had shifted until it rested across the toe of his boot.

“All right,” he said. “Not the forest.”

Silas turned the map towards Alaric.

Alaric touched the satchel through his cloak. Favale lay beyond every choice upon the map. Helmi waited there, if Darcy’s message could reach him and if he was willing to listen. Somewhere inside the city stood the Great Library. Darcy had believed her father could help.

Perhaps something remained of the woman too.

“The soldiers expect us to choose the road,” Alaric said. “If the Expanse gets us closer and you know how to cross it, I think we should take it.”

Silas looked between Alaric and Redd.

“Once we pass the last farms, we can’t decide we’ve had enough and find an inn,” he said. “If the water is bad, we keep moving. If we see something in the ruins, we do not investigate because Redd thinks dying somewhere new counts as adventure.”

“I’ve never said that.”

“You were preparing to.”

Redd considered denying it, then smiled. “The Expanse, then.”

“The Expanse,” Alaric agreed.

The front door struck the wall.

Cold air crossed the room ahead of six Mydean soldiers.

Their entrance displaced every conversation nearest the door. Wet road dust covered their boots and the lower edges of their blue-grey cloaks. One had left his breastplate unfastened beneath the right arm. Another carried his helmet under an elbow and nearly knocked a cup from a wagon driver’s hand as he passed.

“Ale,” the first soldier called. “Six, and whatever you’ve kept warm.”

The innkeeper placed his cloth upon the bar. “Payment first.”

The soldier laughed and dropped several coins hard enough for one to roll across the wood.

“There. Now find something that doesn’t taste like horse piss.”

Silas folded the map without looking down.

Alaric lowered his chin beneath the cloak. Across from him, Redd eased one foot away from Wena’s head. Her eyes opened.

He placed two fingers beside her nose before she could rise.

The soldiers crowded around the bar. None had begun looking at the other patrons. Their voices filled the room as though arriving first had become another privilege of rank.

“Eastern post is already doubled,” one of them said. “Captain wants another pair at every bridge before morning.”

“He can grow the men himself, then.” The soldier with the loose armour took the first cup the innkeeper filled. “Half the company’s moving wagons for the elves.”

“Keep your voice down.”

“Why? Everyone knows who we’re protecting.” He drank and wiped his mouth upon his wrist. “Beastfolk cross the border, Natharul comes to help, and we’re the ones standing in mud while they decide where the war belongs.”

The first soldier claimed the chair nearest the bar and turned it around before sitting. “You can complain after we have the boy. Until then, every cart heading east gets checked.”

Alaric felt Redd’s boot touch his beneath the table.

“They’ll have him before midday,” another soldier said. “Bad foot, dark hair and a farm dog large enough to pull a plough. There’s only one road he can take.”

“Unless he enters Inrandeel.”

Several men laughed.

“Then the imps save us the trouble.”

“Or the Expanse,” the man with the helmet added.

“An injured boy and a dog?” The first soldier reached for the cup being passed towards him. “No one is that stupid.”

“Elf riders join us at first bell. If he leaves the road before then, they’ll find the tracks.”

Silas tucked the folded map beneath his cloak.

Silas indicated the narrow passage beside the kitchen with a slight turn of his head. At its far end, a half-door stood open upon the stable yard. A serving woman passed through carrying an empty bucket. The soldiers demanded another cup before she had taken three steps.

Silas rose while their attention followed her.

Redd found Wena’s rope beneath the table without letting its buckle scrape the floor. Alaric drew the ash stick from beneath the bench and waited until Redd had guided the dog behind it, then stood between her and the bar. His foot accepted his weight before he could prepare for pain.

None came.

They moved towards the passage one at a time. Silas went first. Alaric followed with his cloak closed over the satchel. Redd came behind him, keeping Wena against the wall and beneath the shadow of his cloak as far as the fabric reached.

One soldier shouted that the bread was stale.

“It was fresh before you arrived,” the innkeeper said.

Laughter broke across the bar. Alaric reached the passage before anyone turned.

The stable yard smelled of wet straw and dung. Silas crossed it without hurrying until the inn wall stood between them and the windows. Then he broke into a run.

Alaric followed.

Behind him, Wena’s claws scraped across the stones while Redd kept her rope short.

His foot struck mud, stone and the hard edge of a wagon rut.

It did not hurt.

---

They stopped beneath an abandoned lime shed after the inn’s roof had disappeared behind two folds of land.

Only three walls remained. The fourth had collapsed outward and left a low opening facing away from the road. Silas entered first, checked the broken kiln and the weeds behind it, then motioned the others inside.

Redd lowered Wena’s rope. She circled twice upon the dry earth before sitting beside him.

Alaric rested the ash stick against the remaining wall and stayed on his feet.

The run from the stable yard had been short, yet his ribs had allowed every breath and his foot had met the ground without folding beneath him.

Silas noticed.

“Sit down.”

“It doesn’t hurt.”

“That’s why I want to see it.”

Alaric sat upon a fallen block. Silas crouched over the fastenings of his boot. Dirt had hardened around the knots after several days of use. He worked them loose with the tip of his knife, then drew the boot carefully from Alaric’s foot.

Redd brought the covered lantern nearer.

The swelling around Alaric’s ankle had almost disappeared.

Silas removed the inner wrapping. Where the torn skin had remained red and open that morning, a thin new line had closed across it. Bruising still coloured the side of his foot, but even that had faded towards yellow.

Silas pressed beside the injury.

“Anything?”

“Pressure.”

“Pain?”

“No.”

Silas pressed again, harder.

Alaric shook his head.

Redd leaned over Silas’s shoulder. “Did Marta replace the foot while he was asleep?”

“She treated it,” Silas said.

“She treated your hand too. Look at it.”

Silas ignored him and looked at Alaric. “Breathe as deeply as you can.”

Alaric did.

Air reached the bottom of his lungs without catching beneath the ribs. The simple fullness of it frightened him more than the pain had.

“Again,” Silas said.

The second breath came as easily.

Silas checked Alaric’s shoulder next, then unwrapped his palms. The joint remained stiff, but the deeper pain had gone. Thin pink skin had closed beneath both dressings, and flexing his fingers did not pull either wound open.

Silas wrapped both palms again more loosely, then sat back upon his heels. “Marta must have done more than I realised.”

“Wouldn’t she have told him?” Redd asked.

Silas examined the new skin once more. “I don’t know. I’ve never seen her do this.”

Alaric looked at the closed skin. “Could the core have done it?”

Silas pointed at the satchel.

“Has it warmed again?”

“No.”

“Any light?”

“Nothing.”

Silas retied the cleanest portion of the wrapping around Alaric’s foot. “Then we don’t give it credit for something we can’t prove. Keep the cloth around it, and don’t let it touch your skin.”

He adjusted the satchel so its weight rested higher against Alaric’s coat. The orb remained still inside it.

Redd sat beside Wena and scratched through the fur behind her ear. “If you wake tomorrow with a third foot, I’m blaming the orb.”

Alaric put his boot back on.

When he stood, the ground felt level beneath both feet.

Alaric took the ash stick when they left, though he no longer leaned upon it as heavily.

---

The following afternoon, after a day spent moving between low hills north of the road, they stopped inside the remains of a shepherd’s enclosure. Far to their right, wagons and mounted patrols travelled east in a line that appeared and vanished behind the land. None turned towards them.

The stone wall reached Alaric’s knee in places and his waist in others. Wena had claimed the only patch of grass not covered in burrs.

Silas was checking the edge of his sword when Alaric approached him.

“Can you teach me?”

Silas looked from Alaric to the weapon across his knees. “Teach you what?”

“How to use one.”

At the wall, Redd stopped dividing dried meat. Wena raised her head.

Silas returned the whetstone to its pouch. “Why?”

Alaric had prepared an answer during the walk. The question made the prepared words sound borrowed.

“At the fort, I pulled the guard off you, and he fell on me,” Alaric said. “If I hadn’t reached the knife first… I didn’t know what else to do.”

The memory reached him without the room around it: the guard’s weight, the resistance beneath the blade and the warmth spreading over Alaric’s fingers.

“I thought he was going to kill us.” Alaric struggled to keep looking at Silas. “If it happens again, I want to be able to help. Not just grab at someone and hope.”

Silas slid his sword into its sheath.

“Learning to fight won’t guarantee that.”

“I know.”

“Do you?” Silas stood. “A sword won’t stop you being afraid, and carrying one may put you in fights you could have escaped without it.”

Alaric remembered the knife slipping between the guard’s ribs. “Then teach me how not to make things worse.”

Silas considered him before walking to the supplies.

He unfastened a narrow roll bound beneath his pack and laid it across the wall. Inside was the second-hand sword he had bought along with their cloaks at the inn. Its leather grip had darkened from years of use, and several shallow marks crossed the plain guard. The sheath bore no crest or ornament.

Silas held it towards him.

Alaric accepted the sheath with both hands. It weighed more than he expected.

“It’s yours,” Silas said. “As long as you learn how to carry it without cutting yourself.”

“That means you practise whenever we have ground and time,” Silas continued. “You don’t draw it to threaten someone. You don’t put your hand on it because you’re angry. And you don’t assume having it makes you useful in a fight.”

“All right.”

“Say yes after the first lesson.”

Redd had settled beside Wena with the food untouched between them. “You’re doing wonders for his confidence.”

Silas led Alaric beyond the wall. “Draw it.”

Alaric closed his right hand around the grip and pulled.

The blade moved halfway before the sheath followed it upward.

Redd began laughing.

Alaric tried again, holding the sheath lower. The point cleared and swung towards the ground with enough weight to drag his shoulder after it.

“Not towards your foot,” Silas said.

“I wasn’t aiming there.”

“That’s the concern.”

Silas moved behind him and shifted the angle of his right elbow. He placed Alaric’s feet farther apart, then nudged the left one forwards with the side of his boot.

“Don’t stand as though the ground has offended you. Bend your knees.”

Alaric did. His recovering foot held.

Silas stepped in front of him. “Keep the point where you can see it without staring at it. I’m the person trying to hurt you. The sword isn’t.”

He drew his own blade with his left hand, keeping the dressing around his burned right palm clear of the hilt.

Alaric’s attention went straight to the edge.

Silas tapped the flat against Alaric’s sword and sent the point aside.

“Again.”

Alaric returned it to the position Silas had shown him.

Silas moved first. The attack came slowly enough to follow, a descending line towards Alaric’s left shoulder. Alaric raised his sword and stopped before the blades met.

Silas’s edge rested against his coat.

“You stopped.”

“I thought you were changing direction.”

“I wasn’t.”

“You could have.”

“Then you would have learned something else. Again.”

The second attempt ended with Alaric stepping backwards over his own heel. The third placed his sword too high. On the fourth, he watched Silas’s hands and missed the movement of his shoulders. Each failure produced another correction. None made the next movement easy.

Redd offered suggestions until Silas threatened to let him demonstrate them.

“I’m preserving the dignity of the lesson,” Redd said.

“You’re eating all the meat.”

Redd looked down and discovered that he had, in fact, eaten most of his portion. He broke the remaining piece and offered part to Wena.

“Traitor,” Silas told the dog when she accepted it.

Wena chewed without concern.

Alaric reset his feet.

Silas lifted his sword. “Watch me, not the blade.”

The attack came from Alaric’s right. He moved to meet it, heard the edges strike and loosened his arm at the sound.

Silas pushed through. Alaric’s point dropped.

“You committed until the blades met,” Silas said. “Then you pulled away.”

Alaric looked at the two blades.

The guard beneath Fort Kelmend had made a noise when the knife entered him. Alaric had felt the man’s body resist and pushed harder, desperate to get the arm off his throat.

Silas lowered his sword.

“We can stop.”

“I don’t want to.”

“You don’t have to prove anything to me.”

“I’m not trying to.” Alaric kept the sword raised. “I need another try.”

Silas gave him one.

Alaric failed again.

Redd leaned back against the wall. “Move your left foot, Al. At this rate the rest of you will arrive in Favale without it.”

Alaric corrected his stance before the next attack began.

Then he realised what Redd had called him.

“Al?”

Redd shrugged. “Alaric is too long when a sword is heading towards you.”

Silas waited with his blade lowered.

Alaric said the shortened name once inside his head. Gerolt had given him Alaric beside the dying fire.

“Al is fine,” he said.

Redd smiled. “Good. Move your foot, Al.”

Alaric moved it.

Silas attacked again.

---

The inhabited land thinned over the following days.

Fields became strips cut between long reaches of scrub. Houses turned away from the eastern track, their doors facing west even when that meant placing them against the wind. They passed wells capped with fitted stones and one bridge whose sign directed travellers south towards the royal road, though the lesser path continued east.

Silas kept them far from every door.

The patrols upon the main road grew larger. From the shelter of a dry streambed, Alaric watched twelve Mydean riders escort two wagons east. An elven banner hung beside the Mydean colours upon the second wagon, pale enough to catch light through the dust.

No one left the road to search the scrub.

The soldiers at the inn had been right. An injured boy and a farm dog were expected somewhere safer.

Alaric stopped using the stick on the second day. By the third, he could carry his own share of the water without pain in his ribs. Silas checked his foot each evening until the closed line became difficult to distinguish from the skin around it.

“Marta should charge more,” Redd said after the final inspection.

Silas retied the boot. “She already does.”

They continued practising when the ground allowed it.

Alaric learned that a sword became heavier long before his arms admitted they were tired. He learned that Silas could strike the same place five times while making each approach look different. He learned that watching a person meant more than watching their eyes. Hips turned. Weight shifted. A rear heel rose before the body committed to moving.

Knowing these things did not let him stop them.

Silas disarmed him twice on the fourth morning and once before breakfast on the fifth. Redd counted every failure until Alaric asked how many times he had practised with the sword at his hip.

“Enough to know I’m safer over here,” Redd replied.

Wena agreed by remaining beside him.

She had begun following Redd when he left camp to refill the water skins. The first time, Alaric almost called her back. He stopped before her name left him and watched Redd lead her back by the rope. The next time, the rope hung over Redd’s shoulder while she walked at his knee. When Alaric trained, she settled where Redd sat and watched the moving blades without trying to reach him.

On the fifth night, they camped beneath a stone shelf overlooking the last occupied farm they would see before the Expanse.

Redd was keeping first watch. It should have belonged to Alaric, but Redd had traded places with him without telling Silas so Alaric could sleep.

“You can’t keep taking Alaric’s watches just because you’d rather be useful than asleep,” Silas said.

“I’ve spent enough of my life being useless. I thought I’d try being tired instead.”

Silas handed him the covered lantern. “Two hours. Then wake me.”

Redd accepted it. “You’ve become very comfortable ordering me around.”

“You keep following us.”

“Where else would I go?”

The question outlasted the humour in his voice.

Alaric sat with his back against the shelf. Wena lay between him and Redd, though her head rested upon Redd’s folded cloak.

“Do you wish we could’ve gone back for your things?” Alaric asked.

Redd turned the lantern once between his palms.

“I wish the elves hadn’t put their hands on them.” His voice carried more anger than grief. “I wish they hadn’t walked through my door like the house already belonged to them.”

He looked towards the west. The land had hidden Kelmend days ago.

“But it was a house,” Redd continued. “A bad one. The chimney smoked whenever it rained, and the north wall made a noise at night that convinced me someone was dying inside it. I’m alive. I have my father’s sword, and I get to see what’s at the end of this road.”

“Favale,” Silas said.

“I was hoping for something less obvious.” Redd scratched Wena’s neck. “Maybe adventure. Perhaps a better chimney.”

Alaric smiled.

Redd glanced between him and Silas. “I was alone there, you know. Now I have friends. If that requires sleeping beneath rocks and being insulted during every meal, I’ve endured worse arrangements.”

Silas placed the provisions beside him. “Then take second watch tomorrow without trading it away. If we’re travelling together, everyone takes a turn.”

“That sounded almost kind,” Redd said.

“It was an instruction.”

“Of course.”

Silas lay down with his cloak beneath his head.

Redd kept the lantern. When Wena shifted closer, he made room without interrupting the hand moving through her fur.

The next morning, the farms ended.

A stone marker stood where the final fence turned west. Its lettering had been cut in Mydean, deep enough that windblown grit had not erased it.

**THE GREAT EXPANSE**

**NO ROAD BEYOND THIS MARKER IS KEPT BY THE CROWN.**

**NO CLAIM OF RESCUE WILL BE HONOURED.**

Redd read the final line twice. “Well, that’s comforting.”

Silas checked the water skins and continued east.

The land did not change at once. Scrub survived beyond the marker, and insects still moved beneath the stones. During the afternoon, the soil lightened until their tracks looked dark upon it. By evening, the clouds had thinned into long strips that seemed unwilling to cross farther.

On the second day beyond the marker, the wind began carrying dust that clicked softly against the buckles of their packs.

Silas wrapped cloth across Wena’s nose and showed Redd how to knot it loosely enough for her to pant. Alaric covered the lower half of his own face. Fine grains gathered in his hair and shone like ground glass before losing their light inside his palm.

The sun remained visible. Its warmth did not.

Colours thinned beneath it. Redd’s brown cloak turned almost grey. Silas’s fire, when he used a small flame to light their evening meal, appeared painfully bright against the drained earth.

Sound behaved differently in the open land. A stone dislodged by Wena travelled down a slope, each strike arriving clearly long after Alaric could no longer see it. Later, Redd called from behind a ridge and his voice reached them as though he stood beside Alaric’s shoulder. The sound carried on after he stopped speaking, thinning across the empty ground.

Vegetation returned in scattered patches near dry channels. The plants resembled thorn bushes until Alaric saw that every branch bent towards the ground, growing downwards before turning up again at its tip. Their pale leaves showed veins upon both sides.

They gave the first patch a wide distance.

They saw no creatures. Silas kept one hand near his sword.

On the seventh evening after leaving the inn, the track climbed between two ridges of dark stone.

Alaric had spent the last hour practising the same movement whenever the path widened: left foot forwards, weight centred, blade meeting an attack without stopping before contact. The worn sword rode at his hip now. It no longer struck his knee every tenth step.

At the top of the ridge, Silas drew his sword and turned towards him.

“Once.”

Alaric set down his share of the water and drew.

Redd moved aside with Wena. He did not offer advice.

Silas approached from Alaric’s right.

Alaric saw the change in his weight before the blade moved. Fear tightened low in his stomach. He stepped, raised the sword and completed the motion anyway.

The edges met.

Silas changed direction. Alaric followed without looking at his feet. Their blades struck again, and Silas withdrew before Alaric’s arms could lose the position.

“Better,” he said.

The single word pleased Alaric more than it should have.

Redd lifted the water skin. “A week in, and Al has survived two entire attacks made slowly by a man trying not to injure him.”

Alaric sheathed the sword. “You could try.”

“I’m responsible for the water.”

Silas took the skin from him. “Now you’re free.”

Redd started to object.

Then he saw beyond the ridge.

The last light of day lay across the first city.

It filled the land below them.

Alaric had expected walls scattered across sand. A few towers. Streets buried until only their direction remained.

The city had once been larger than Kelmend many times over. Its surviving districts extended across the basin and climbed the opposing slope, crowded with buildings whose roofs leaned towards one another. Towers rose from them at angles no builder could have intended. Bridges curved downwards between upper floors and disappeared through broken windows.

Half of the city was gone.

The absence began near its centre and continued beyond the eastern edge of the basin. Streets ended at a sheer drop. Foundations protruded from the exposed earth like cut bone. Several buildings remained with their front rooms open to the air. Furniture still stood inside chambers whose other halves no longer existed.

There was no rubble large enough to account for what had vanished.

The surviving city bent towards the emptiness.

Walls curved inward. Towers had stretched until their upper floors pointed towards the missing ground. Rows of houses appeared to have moved upon their foundations before hardening in place, each one pulled a little farther than the last.

Pale growth covered sections of the outer district. Trees emerged through roofs with trunks divided into flat ribbons. Vines climbed down walls and entered the soil before their leaves opened. Nothing moved among them.

Alaric stood beside Silas without reaching for the satchel.

He could only look at the streets and imagine them filled.

People had built those houses. They had crossed the bridges, opened the doors and believed the earth beneath them would remain where it had always been.

“How many people do you think lived here?” Alaric asked.

“More than Kelmend could ever hold,” Silas said. “Beyond that, I couldn’t guess.”

Redd came to the ridge beside them. Wena remained close to his leg.

The sun lowered behind the group, and the warped city received their shadows.

A thin column of smoke rose from the surviving outer district.

Silas leaned forwards. “I didn’t see smoke when I crossed before.”

Another thread appeared farther along the ruined street. Beneath it, sheltered between two buildings drawn towards the missing centre, a small orange fire became visible.

Then a second.

Far below them, inside the city the world had abandoned, someone was preparing for night.
