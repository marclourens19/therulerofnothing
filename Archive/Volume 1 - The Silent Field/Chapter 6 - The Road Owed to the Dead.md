# The Road Owed to the Dead

“So,” the stranger said over the river’s roar. “You’re the one they’re hunting.”

Wena’s growl deepened.

Alaric placed one hand against the wet fur at her neck. She stood in front of him with her legs spread and her head low, water still falling from her belly. The sound inside her was too tired to become a threat. It trembled almost as much as the rest of her.

The stranger looked towards the river again.

Torchlight moved between the trees on the opposite bank. Another flame had appeared farther downstream, its reflection opening and closing across the water.

“Keep her quiet.” His attention remained upon the moving lights.

Alaric’s fingers tightened beneath Wena’s collar.

“Who are you?”

The stranger’s gaze cut back to him. “I said keep her quiet. The rest can wait until those torches are farther away.”

The man pushed away from the alder. The weather-dark cloak shifted from his shoulders, revealing worn leather beneath it and a short blade resting at his hip. A pale scar crossed the left side of his face from cheekbone to jaw.

He tilted his head.

Alaric heard only the river.

Then something changed beneath it.

Hooves pressed into wet ground somewhere upstream. Nearer than the lights across the water. Already upon their side.

Wena heard them too. Her growl broke into a breath.

The stranger looked at the mud beneath Alaric’s feet. Water had gathered around him. Each step he had taken from the river remained dark and deep among the roots.

“They’ll follow those prints straight to you.” The man stepped backwards into the trees. “Come with me or stay. I’m leaving.”

He turned and entered the trees.

Alaric did not follow.

The man had a weapon. He had watched them crawl from the river without offering help. Nothing in his face suggested that Alaric’s survival mattered beyond whatever use he had found in it.

A layered voice travelled through the forest.

Wena pressed back against Alaric’s legs.

The stranger stopped several paces ahead and looked over one shoulder. Impatience tightened the scarred side of his face.

“If you’re coming, move.”

Another voice answered, close enough that Alaric heard tack shifting beneath it.

He followed.

The stranger moved across the slope without disturbing more than the lowest branches. Alaric struck nearly all of them. Wet leaves crossed his face. Roots caught his shoes, and the pain beneath his ribs sharpened whenever he twisted to remain upright.

Wena stayed close now. Once, she turned towards the river. Alaric touched her neck, and she continued.

The ground rose around a shelf of pale stone. The stranger slipped behind it and disappeared.

Alaric stopped.

A narrow break divided the limestone from the earth beside it, scarcely wide enough for a man’s shoulders. Darkness waited inside.

“In,” the stranger whispered.

Alaric looked back. Torchlight moved through the trees they had crossed.

He guided Wena into the opening and followed.

The passage widened after several steps. The ceiling remained low enough that Alaric’s wet hair brushed stone. A smell of old smoke, leather and damp earth filled the hollow. Beneath one wall lay an oilskin bundle, a dented cup and enough split wood for a small fire.

Someone used this place.

The stranger pulled a loose branch across the opening, hiding most of the moonlight. He crouched and pressed two fingers into the mud Alaric had carried inside.

“Is this yours?” Alaric whispered.

“A place I use when armed elves cross too close to my home.”

He rubbed dry soil across the wet marks, then pointed towards the deepest part of the hollow.

“Get behind the stone. Take the dog with you.”

Alaric moved behind a shoulder of stone. Wena resisted until he lowered himself beside her. Her body pressed against his chest, cold passing through his soaked clothing.

Outside, a horse released a long breath.

Leather creaked. A boot touched earth.

The stranger stepped into a narrow fissure beside the entrance. His cloak became another fold of darkness.

Wena’s ears lifted.

One of the layered voices spoke just beyond the cave. Another answered from farther among the trees. Alaric could not understand the words, but the second voice receded with the sound of moving horses.

One rider had remained.

Light entered beneath the branch.

A gloved hand pushed it aside.

The Natharul scout ducked into the cave with a torch in one hand and a narrow sword in the other. Pale hair lay bound against the back of his armour. Two pointed ears rose through it.

The torchlight found the damp floor.

Found the soil brushed across it.

Stopped.

Wena drew breath to bark.

Alaric closed both arms around her. One hand wrapped carefully around her muzzle while the other locked across her chest. She fought him at once. Her paws struck his ribs, and the pain almost opened his grip.

“Please,” he breathed against her wet fur.

The scout took another step.

His torch passed the fissure.

The stranger came out behind him.

A thin cord crossed the scout’s throat and snapped tight. His head jerked backwards. Before the sword could rise, the stranger drove one knee behind his leg and pulled him down against his chest.

The scout’s mouth opened.

No voice escaped it.

The stranger’s short blade entered beneath his raised arm, between two fitted plates of armour. Once. Deep.

Dust lifted from the cave floor.

Wind pulled towards the dying scout in a single sharp breath. The torch flame stretched sideways. Loose grit struck Alaric’s cheek.

Then the air released.

The stranger held the body until it stopped moving. He lowered it without allowing armour to strike stone, caught the falling torch and pressed its burning end into the wet earth.

Darkness closed again.

Wena trembled inside Alaric’s arms. Her growl had become a small sound trapped behind his fingers.

Outside, the horse shifted.

The stranger listened for several breaths, then crawled from the cave. Alaric heard the quiet scrape of a blade through leather.

The horse snorted.

A hard slap followed.

The riderless horse broke away through the trees and fled downstream. One distant rider called after it. Another answered, and the search moved with the sound.

The man returned.

He wiped the short blade upon the dead scout’s cloak and sheathed it.

“They’ll follow the horse for a while. Then they’ll come back looking for him.” He nodded towards the body. “We leave before they do.”

Alaric released Wena’s muzzle. She turned in his arms and pressed her nose beneath his jaw. He could feel her heart striking through both their bodies.

The dead elf lay only a few paces away.

Alaric looked at the face. The eyes remained open, fixed upon the low ceiling. He wondered whether someone had waited for that rider to return. Whether another voice would call his name and receive nothing.

Gerolt had killed one of them.

This man had killed another.

Both deaths existed because Alaric had remained alive.

His stomach tightened.

The stranger crouched in front of him.

“Show me what you keep touching.”

Alaric’s hand moved to his pocket before he could stop it.

“I don’t know what you mean.”

The lie sounded weak even to him.

“You held it through the river. Checked it when you came out. Covered it when you saw me.” The man glanced towards the hidden entrance. “If it belongs to the elves, I need to know before I decide whether you are worth the trouble.”

Alaric closed his fingers around the token beneath the wet cloth.

“It doesn’t.”

“Then show me.”

The stranger had killed the scout before he could make a sound. Refusing him might be brave. It might also be useless.

Alaric drew the token from his pocket but kept it inside his hand. He opened his fingers only far enough for the split shield to catch what little light reached the cave.

The man went still.

His gaze sharpened upon the iron.

“Where did you get that?”

The question came too quickly.

Alaric closed his hand again.

“Gerolt gave it to me.”

For the first time, calculation left the stranger’s face without something replacing it.

“Gerolt?”

“You know him.”

The man ignored that.

“Where is he?”

Alaric looked towards the cave entrance. The orange glow beyond the river no longer reached through the trees, but he could still see the cabin opening around the flames whenever he closed his eyes.

“He stayed behind.”

The stranger waited.

Alaric forced the rest through the tightness in his throat.

“There were two riders. He killed one. The other put a stone through his side. More riders came after she escaped.”

“And he gave you the shield.”

“He told me to cross the river. To find an inn near Kelmend’s southern gate.”

The stranger’s face had emptied again.

“Ask for Marta,” Alaric said.

Silence held the hollow.

The stranger’s gaze returned to him.

“And what did Gerolt call you?”

“Alaric.”

Wena lowered her head onto Alaric’s knee.

“He could still have escaped,” Alaric said.

“No.”

The stranger spoke without cruelty. That made the word worse.

“You didn’t see him.”

“A stone beneath the ribs. Natharul riders in the field. A burning house.” He looked towards Wena. “The dog knows.”

Wena did not lift her head.

Alaric pressed the token into his palm until its edges hurt.

The stranger leaned back upon his heels.

“One dead Natharul would have been enough.”

“Enough for what?”

“For them to burn every roof that ever gave him shelter. Two dead will make them thorough.”

Alaric looked at the body in the cave.

“You killed him.”

“Because he entered my hole with a sword.”

“Won’t they punish people for that too?”

“Only if they learn who did it.”

The answer came too easily.

The stranger stood and crossed to the oilskin bundle.

“If the rider who escaped saw you, Mydean guards will have your description by sunrise.”

“They’re human.”

The stranger untied the bundle. “They’re afraid of Natharul. If handing you over keeps the elves from calling this an act of war, that is what the guards will do.”

Alaric looked down at the split shield.

“Do you know Marta?”

The stranger paused over the open bundle.

“I know the inn.”

“Can you take me there?”

“I can get you as far as her door.”

He returned to the bundle without offering anything more.

Alaric understood the silence.

“What do you want?”

The man’s eyes settled upon the closed fist around Gerolt’s token.

“I need to ask Marta for something. Arriving with Gerolt’s token may persuade her to listen.”

“What do you need from her?”

“That is between Marta and me.” The man pulled a folded coat free. “Your choice is whether you want me to get you there.”

He threw the folded coat at Alaric. It struck his chest and slid into his lap.

“I take you to the inn. No farther.” He held Alaric’s gaze until the limit was clear. “If the riders find us, I choose the road that keeps me alive. If you stop moving, I will not carry you.”

His gaze shifted towards Wena.

“The dog cannot come.”

Alaric’s fingers sank into the coat.

“No.”

“She leaves tracks. She barks. She eats. All poor qualities in cargo.”

“Gerolt told me to take her.”

“Gerolt is dead.”

The words entered Alaric cleanly.

He felt them after.

Wena remained against his knee, waiting without knowing what he was being asked to choose.

“I’m not leaving her.”

The stranger studied him.

Alaric’s fear did not lessen. The man could refuse. The riders could return. Kelmend might lie beyond roads Alaric could not survive alone.

None of it changed what Gerolt had placed in his hands.

“Then I can’t go with you,” he said.

For several breaths, only water moved somewhere beneath the stone.

The stranger looked at Wena again—not with affection, but as though altering a sum.

“Fine. Bring her.”

He rose and secured the oilskin bundle. “But you are cargo, not company. Do not expect me to die keeping either of you alive.”

Alaric did not thank him.

He pulled the coat around his shoulders.

It was dry.

“Mydean messenger’s coat,” the stranger said. “Keep the collar high.”

Warmth settled against his wet clothing, and his body began shaking harder beneath it. The high collar rose almost to his jaw. One side crossed the other over his chest, closing beneath a line of hidden fastenings. A second layer of dark cloth fell from one shoulder like the shortened edge of a cloak.

The coat was a little broad, but it concealed the strange fabric beneath it.

The stranger caught one of Alaric’s original sleeves before it disappeared.

His thumb passed across the smooth stitching.

No branch had torn it. No stone in the river had opened a seam. Beneath Gerolt’s blood and the mud, the fabric remained whole.

The man released it.

“Where did you get this?”

“I don’t know.”

The stranger studied Alaric’s face. Whatever he found there prevented another question. He tore two strips from a cloth inside the bundle and dropped them beside Alaric.

“Wrap your hands. You’re bleeding on my floor.”

Alaric wound the cloth around his opened palms. His fingers had stiffened from the cold, and the first knot slipped twice before holding.

By the time he finished, the stranger had taken the dead scout’s torch, broken its burned end and buried both pieces beneath loose stone.

He dragged the body into the narrow fissure where he had waited and pulled loose rock across it. Blood still darkened the floor, but the corpse could no longer be seen from the cave mouth.

He lifted the hidden branch.

“Move.”

---

The night narrowed into the ground beneath the stranger’s feet.

Alaric followed because there was nothing else he knew how to follow.

They did not use the road. The man led them through drainage cuts, between low walls and across fields whose crops had already been gathered. When the forest thickened, he chose the places where roots broke the soil least. When it opened, he waited beneath cover and watched before crossing.

He explained none of it.

Alaric tried to remember each turn.

The first three remained clear. River behind them. A split oak to the north. A stone wall curving west.

After that, exhaustion folded one path over another. Every stand of trees resembled the last. His wet shoes rubbed skin from his heels. The coat kept the wind from his chest but could not warm the water trapped beneath it.

He stumbled upon a low wall and fell across it.

Stone drove into his bruised ribs. Breath left him. For several heartbeats, he could do nothing but hold himself against the wall and wait for the dark to withdraw from his sight.

The stranger stopped ahead.

He did not return.

“Get up,” he said without turning. “The riders are still behind us.”

Alaric planted both wrapped hands against the stone. Pain travelled through them. His arms shook once, then held.

He stood.

The stranger continued.

Wena remained beside Alaric until he moved.

Later, Wena stopped and turned towards the south.

The stranger crouched.

Hoofbeats arrived several breaths later.

He looked at Wena, then changed direction before the first torch appeared between the trees.

“Keep her close,” he said. “She heard them before I did.”

It was the nearest he had come to approving Wena’s presence.

They continued.

A low roof appeared between the trees sometime before dawn. No light burned behind its shutters. A narrow chimney stood against the paling sky, and split logs lay stacked beneath one wall.

The stranger looked towards it once.

Then torchlight moved along the distant road.

He turned away from the roof and led them into another field.

“Is that where you live?” Alaric asked.

The man watched the roof disappear behind them. “Near enough that I won’t lead the elves to it.”

He did not look back again.

“What is your name?” Alaric asked.

“You don’t need my name to reach Kelmend.”

“You know mine.”

“I know what Gerolt called you.”

The distinction unsettled him.

“You knew him.”

The stranger stepped over a fallen branch.

“Enough to know he did not give that shield away lightly.”

“He saved me.”

The stranger’s pace shortened by half a step. “I know.”

Alaric’s hands tightened inside their wrappings.

“Do you think he was a fool?”

“Yes.”

The answer came without hesitation.

Alaric stopped.

The stranger took two more steps before noticing.

“He died for someone he had known less than a day,” Alaric said.

The man finally looked back at him. “Gerolt decided you were worth staying for. That does not mean staying was wise.”

Anger rose before Alaric understood what to do with it.

“Then why are you helping me?”

“Because bringing you to Marta is not the same as dying for you.” The man’s gaze dropped briefly to the hidden shape of the token. “And because I need something from her.”

He turned away.

Alaric looked down at Wena. She was staring into the east, where the darkness had begun to thin.

Gerolt had made a foolish decision.

The stranger had not said it was worthless.

Alaric followed.

---

Kelmend appeared beneath the last colour of night.

The town occupied a low rise beyond the fields, enclosed by a wall of dark stone. Two square towers guarded the southern entrance. Lamps burned above the gate, their light falling across carts already gathered upon the road.

Roofs climbed behind the wall in uneven rows. Smoke had begun to rise from the lowest chimneys. Farther within, something struck metal in a slow working rhythm.

Alaric saw people first.

Then the horses among them.

The stranger drew him down behind a bank overgrown with thorn and dead grass. Wena crawled between them without being told.

Three Natharul riders stood before the gate.

Their pale hair caught the lamps. Dark armour separated them from the Mydean guards gathered beneath the arch, though not by much. Both groups carried weapons. The elves stood as though the ground already belonged to them.

“You have crossed a sovereign border under arms,” the Mydean captain said.

The Natharul rider nearest him did not lower his head.

“One of ours was murdered upon that sovereign soil. Another was mutilated.”

The captain’s jaw tightened. “By a farmer, according to your own account.”

“Then find the boy the farmer died protecting.”

“Young,” the rider continued. “Human. Dark hair. Unfamiliar black clothing. He travels with a large farm dog and crossed the river south of the bridge.”

Alaric touched the high collar. The coat hid the unfamiliar cut of the clothing beneath it. It could not hide his hair or Wena.

“Your messenger demanded our southern gate close because of one boy.”

“One scout is dead. Another carries the farmer’s blade across her face.”

The words remained controlled. Something beneath them did not.

The Natharul rider stepped closer.

“If he passes your wall, Kelmend chooses to shelter those responsible. Decide whether that is the report you wish carried home.”

For a moment, the captain looked towards the soldiers behind him.

Then he turned.

“Search every cart. No dog enters unexamined. Send two men to the western postern and double the wall patrol.”

The orders moved at once.

Human soldiers pulled the first driver from his seat. Sacks opened beneath spear points. A woman holding a sleeping child was ordered down from a wagon while another guard searched beneath it.

Alaric watched the Mydean captain stand beside the Natharul riders.

“They’re helping them.”

The stranger kept his attention upon the guards searching the carts. “The captain is trying to keep Natharul outside his wall.”

“By giving me to them.”

“If he has to choose between one stranger and everyone inside Kelmend, he will give them the stranger.” There was no satisfaction in the answer. No apology either.

Alaric looked at the gate Gerolt had sent him towards.

Crossing the river had not placed him among allies.

It had only changed which people would surrender him.

The stranger moved backwards beneath the thorn.

“We cannot use the gate.”

“Then how do we get inside?”

“There is a tannery runoff beneath the southern wall.” The stranger looked towards the paling sky. “If the morning sluice has not opened, we can crawl through it.”

He turned south.

They followed the wall until the road disappeared behind them. The wall descended to a lower section stained dark by years of runoff. The smell reached Alaric before he saw its source—rotting bark, old urine and something sour enough to tighten his throat.

An abandoned tannery leaned against the outer wall. Half its roof had fallen inward. Empty drying frames stood behind it, black and thin against the approaching dawn.

The stranger entered through a gap where boards had been removed from one wall.

Inside, shallow pits crossed the floor. Rainwater had gathered in them beneath skins of green. Wena approached the nearest and recoiled.

“Not drinking water,” the man said.

At the rear of the tannery, a brick channel descended beneath the town wall. Iron bars covered its mouth. Mud, hair and strips of ruined hide had gathered against them.

The stranger pushed both hands into the filth and found a pin concealed beside the lowest bar.

It did not move.

“You knew this was here,” Alaric said.

“I helped open it years ago.”

“For Marta?”

The stranger drew the short blade from his hip.

“Hold the grate.”

Fire opened across his fingers.

No tinder caught. No spark fell from stone. One moment his hand was empty; the next, orange flame turned between his knuckles as though it had risen from beneath his skin.

Alaric stepped back.

The cabin roof opened inside him again. Flame rolled along the beams. Gerolt remained somewhere beneath it.

The stranger glanced at his face but said nothing.

He passed his hand over the blade. Fire followed, narrowing along its edge without touching the leather wrapped around the grip.

Fire『Affinity』.

The word surfaced with the same unwanted certainty as『Magic』had upon the field.

The man pressed the heated edge into the black pitch around the pin. Smoke rose in a thin, bitter line. The pitch softened. He wrapped part of his cloak around the metal and twisted.

Rust broke with a quiet crack.

The flame vanished.

The stranger pulled the lowest section of grate inward. It opened scarcely wider than Wena’s shoulders.

“She goes first.”

Wena lowered herself towards the channel, smelled what waited inside and pulled back.

Alaric crouched beside her.

“Come on.”

She looked at him.

He placed one hand between her eyes.

“I’m coming too.”

Wena entered.

Her body disappeared beneath the wall one careful step at a time. Alaric followed upon his stomach. The new coat caught against the iron until the stranger pulled its lower edge free.

“Keep your elbows in.”

Alaric dragged himself forwards.

Cold filth closed around his forearms. The channel narrowed immediately beyond the grate. Brick pressed against both shoulders, and the ceiling remained close enough that he could not lift his head without striking it.

Ahead, Wena’s paws disturbed the shallow flow.

Behind him, the stranger pulled the grate back into place. The pin did not follow.

“They’ll see that,” Alaric whispered.

“Only if they search the runoff. We’ll be through it first.”

A bell sounded above the wall.

The stranger entered the channel behind him.

“Move.”

Alaric crawled.

The passage stripped distance of meaning. Brick. Water. Wena’s hind legs ahead of him. The stranger’s breath behind. His wrapped palms darkened and loosened until the cloth dragged against the channel floor.

His ribs caught whenever the passage bent. At the first turn, he became certain he could not fit through it.

“I’m stuck.”

“Turn your left shoulder.”

Alaric tried. Pain cut beneath his ribs.

“I can’t.”

Urgency stripped the dryness from the stranger’s voice. “You can. Put the left shoulder down before the sluice reaches us.”

The second bell struck.

Something moved behind them.

Not footsteps.

Water.

It entered the lower channel with a distant rush, gathering sound as it approached.

Alaric forced his left shoulder down. Brick scraped along the messenger’s coat. His chest compressed until no breath remained inside it.

Then the corner released him.

He pulled himself forwards.

The first surge struck his boots.

Dirty water climbed his legs and drove through the narrow space around his body. Wena barked once ahead, the sound trapped and made enormous by the brick.

“Keep moving,” the stranger said.

Alaric did.

Not quickly.

Not well.

One elbow. One knee. One breath whenever the ceiling allowed it.

The water rose to his waist. It pushed at the coat and found every bruise beneath it. His hands slipped. The current carried him half an arm’s length before his knee struck the side of the channel.

Wena disappeared around another bend.

Then pale light touched the bricks ahead.

Alaric reached it upon shaking arms and found a broken grille opening into a stone settling chamber. Wena stood beyond it, soaked again and whining.

He dragged himself through. The floor dropped farther than he expected. His knees struck stone, and he rolled aside before the stranger emerged behind him.

Water poured around them and entered a deeper trench cut through the centre of the room.

Above, footsteps crossed wooden boards.

Wena’s head lifted.

The stranger caught her collar before she could move.

A trapdoor occupied the ceiling. Light entered around its edges. Someone paused directly upon it.

Alaric held his breath.

A man coughed above them, spat through a gap in the boards and continued walking.

The stranger released Wena and looked towards the trapdoor. Whatever relief entering Kelmend should have offered never reached his face.

---

They entered the town through a cellar that smelled worse than the channel.

The stranger waited until voices moved away from the tannery yard, then lifted the trapdoor and climbed into the morning. Alaric pushed Wena up before following.

Kelmend had begun working around them.

Carts groaned through narrow service lanes. Smoke from the first ovens mixed with the tannery stench and the damp air before sunrise. Labourers carried baskets between low stone buildings without looking closely at anyone who kept moving.

Alaric kept his collar high.

Twice, the stranger pulled him into an alley before armed men crossed the street ahead. Once, he placed a copper coin into the hand of a labourer who had opened his mouth at the sight of Wena emerging from a tannery yard.

The man looked at the coin.

Then at the mud covering all three of them.

He closed his mouth.

No one offered Alaric safety. No one asked whether he was hurt. The town accepted his presence only when it failed to notice him or was paid not to care.

Still, each street carried them farther from the gate.

The stranger finally entered a narrow lane between two inns. He passed the first without looking at it.

The second stood back from the street behind a small stable yard. Its lower walls were stone, its upper floor dark timber. A weathered wooden sign hung above the entrance, its painted cup almost erased by rain.

No light showed beneath the door.

The stranger stopped at the edge of the yard.

Alaric waited for him to continue.

He did not.

The man who had stepped from the dark behind an armed elf would not cross the remaining distance.

Alaric looked at the weathered door.

“You promised to bring me here.”

“And here you are.”

The stranger did not approach the entrance.

“Knock.”

“Why?”

His eyes remained upon the closed door.

“Because she won’t open it for me.”
