# Interlude: IV

Gurdred had five hundred and forty-one days left, and she spent the first four hours of this one standing in the rain beside a hold full of grain.

The rain had come in off the sea before dawn and hadn't found a reason to stop. It was summer rain, warm and straight. It fell on the fish stalls with their awnings down, on the sun basins brimming at the street corners, on the three Natharul hulls along the northern pier, and on her.

"Seal," said the quartermaster.

He was an elf, like all their quartermasters, with a waxed cloak and a face to match. He held the lantern low while she pressed the harbour's sun into the wax across the hatch. It was the ninth seal of the morning. He bent and studied it, as though she might have drawn it crooked on purpose.

"It will hold?"

"It's wax," said Gurdred. "It'll hold till somebody breaks it."

"No one will break it."

"Then it'll hold."

He glanced at the sky. "Your rain does not stop."

"It's not mine. You can have it. Put it in the hold with the rest."

He made a note in his own book, in his own writing, and didn't thank her. Elves didn't, as a rule. She'd stopped minding in her second year.

At the landward end of the pier, a cart waited in the rain with a horse that had given up and a man who hadn't. The cart was empty. It had come from Dalen, and it had been there yesterday as well.

"Anything spoiled?" the carter called as she passed. "Wet sacks? Split ones? We'll take wet."

"It's all counted."

"Counted for them."

"Go home, Tam."

"I've been home. There's nothing there. That's why I came." He tugged his hood lower against the rain. "How many days you got now?"

"Five hundred and forty-one."

"Lucky," said Tam. "Some of us have got the rest of our lives."

She didn't answer that. There wasn't an answer she was allowed to give, and he knew it, and she knew he knew it.

She walked on up the quay with water running off the rim of her helmet and down the back of her neck. She thought about the room she'd have after, one day, in some town where nobody had heard of a requisition.

Five hundred and forty-one days. It was a better number than it had been.

---

Cobb was standing outside the harbour office.

That was the first thing. There was a porch over the door with a bench under it, and Cobb had sat on that bench through every rain of the last two summers with his feet up and his helmet on his knee.

Now he was standing a yard out from the porch, in the open, with the rain beating on his helmet. He was staring at the warehouse opposite.

"What are you doing?"

"Standing," said Cobb.

"You're wet through."

"Yes."

"Is the harbourmaster in?"

Cobb's eyes came round to her, and went back to the warehouse.

"He went out."

"In this?"

"He went out, Gurdred."

"Who's inside?"

Cobb didn't answer. The rain ran off the end of his nose.

"Cobb. Who's inside?"

"Go in and see," said Cobb. "I'm not going back in."

She went up the step under the porch and shook the water off her cloak. The harbourmaster's cloak wasn't on its peg by the door. Whoever had taken it hadn't stopped for his hat.

She went in.

The office was one long room, low and brown, with the counter across the middle and the clerk's desk under the far window, where the light was best. A fire muttered in the grate. It smelled, as it always did, of ink and wet wool and the tallow they sealed the ledgers with.

Neldor and his sister were on the bench along the wall, where Gurdred had left them that morning and the three mornings before.

The Waluna report had come up the coast four days ago: two women carried aboard during the southern alarm, and no record of either. The harbour had held Neldor's ship ever since, because a report was a report. Neldor had spent the days being level about it. Nyana had spent them telling the clerk what she thought of his fire, his benches and his mother.

Neither of them was talking now. They sat side by side with their hands in their laps, and they weren't looking at the desk.

Someone was sitting at the clerk's desk.

He had the arrivals book open in front of him, the big one with the calfskin cover gone dark from forty years of harbour hands. He was turning its pages with one finger, reading each before he turned it.

He wore dark armour under a travelling cloak. The cloak was soaked through to the shoulders, and water had run off its hem into a pool round the legs of the clerk's chair.

His hair was wet. His ears came up through it, long and pointed. A sword hung at his hip in a plain black scabbard: single-edged, the kind she'd never seen on any of their quartermasters or couriers. He hadn't taken it off to sit.

He didn't look up when she came in.

Gurdred stood inside the door with the rain dripping off her, and felt the whole room lean towards her to see what she'd do.

"That's the clerk's desk," she said.

He turned another page. "Yes."

"You can't sit at it."

"The harbourmaster did not object."

"The harbourmaster isn't here."

"No," he agreed, and went on reading.

"What's your business in Meren?"

"This."

"The arrivals book's harbour property. If you want an entry, you ask at the counter, and the clerk reads it out, and you pay the fee."

He finished the page. Then he lifted his head and looked at her.

It wasn't a hard look. She'd had hard looks from quartermasters and captains and once, memorably, from a high priest on pilgrimage. This was only attention. It came to rest on her and it didn't leave.

He didn't get up. He turned the ledger round so that it faced her, and laid one hand flat beside it.

"Your clerk's hand is poor," he said. "I cannot read it. Read it to me."

His speech was good. Better than good. Every word was set down carefully on its own, as if he didn't want it on his hands.

Gurdred didn't move.

"Please," he said.

She'd never know afterwards why that was the word that did it.

She crossed the room. The boards were loud under her boots. She stood on the near side of the desk, the side the harbour stood on when it came to be written down, and bent over the page.

She knew the entry before she found it. She'd been standing at the gangplank when it was written.

"Sera," she read. Her voice came out rough. She cleared it. "Black hair. Very fair. Green eyes."

She looked up.

She didn't mean to. She'd said *green eyes*, and there were eyes in front of her. His were green. The same green, for all she knew. She hadn't looked that closely at the girl.

She was reading him a description of his own face.

He waited.

"Speech of a great household," Gurdred read.

Whatever household the girl had talked like, she thought, this was it.

"Nereth. Burn scarring, left side of the face, eye to temple. Taken aboard at Waluna during the southern alarm. Passage worked. Disembarking at Meren for inland travel."

That was the end of it. She stood with her finger on the last line.

"The attendant," he said. "Nereth. How badly was she burned?"

"I didn't look close."

"Someone did."

On the bench, Nyana made a small sound.

He didn't turn his head. "You are the ship's medic."

"Yes." Nyana was holding her treatment book to her chest in its waxed cloth, with both arms. She'd held it that way every morning for four days. "I treated her."

"Tell me."

Nyana glanced at her brother.

Neldor didn't look back. He'd gone grey. Gurdred had watched him take four days of the harbour's patience without once changing colour. Now he was grey from his collar to his hairline, and he was looking at the floor between his boots.

Nyana turned back to the man at the desk. Gurdred saw her decide.

"It wasn't a burn," Nyana said. "Not from a fire. It looked like one from across a room. Black and purple, from under her left eye to her hairline and down her neck, like veins. Like somebody had drawn roots on her. Poison, I thought. I still think it. Something got into her, and it didn't come out again."

"Could she walk?"

"By the end of the crossing. She shouldn't have been able to."

"Did she eat?"

"When I stood over her." Nyana's chin came up. "She was carrying crates she shouldn't have carried and lying to me about it. She had a fever three days. She came through it. I don't know how."

"And the medicine."

"A jar. For the pain. Five drops in water, no more than twice a day. It doesn't touch the rest." Her voice wobbled, and she put it back. "I told her —"

She stopped.

"Tell me what you told her," he said.

"I told her if the marks went hot, the jar wouldn't help. I told her to stop, and find a healer. Somebody who knows what it is." Nyana held the book tighter. "She's going to need one. Do you understand me? Not a jar. A healer. I don't know what's in her, but it isn't finished, and she's walking about on it as if it were a sprained foot."

The fire muttered. Rain ran down the window behind him in long crooked lines.

"Thank you," he said.

Nyana opened her mouth, closed it again, and sat back against the wall.

He turned to Gurdred.

"You were there when this was written."

"Yes."

"What else did she say? The first one." He didn't say *Sera*. He didn't give her a name at all. "Not what is in the book. What she said."

Gurdred tried to remember. It had been a long morning. She'd had a hold to look at, and a manifest, and grain in it for the northern camp, and Dalen on her mind.

"She told the clerk she spoke badly," she said slowly. "She said, ask what you need. That was after she'd put his back up. She talked —" She stopped.

"Go on."

"She talked like people are used to stopping when she stops."

Nothing moved in his face.

"She said they were going inland. She said she'd been told to start near the pilgrim roads."

Nothing.

"And she said they were looking for family."

"Family," he said.

He said it quietly, as if he were checking the word against something.

Then he breathed out.

It was a long breath, slow and even, the breath of a man setting something heavy down. Gurdred heard it all the way to the end.

And the room began to shake.

It came up through her boots first. It wasn't the floor. The floor was still. It was her.

Her teeth ached, all of them at once, at the roots, as they did in winter when she bit on ice. Her ears rang. Her hands on the edge of the desk wouldn't stay flat. She pressed them down, and they went on trembling by themselves, fine and quick.

Her heart had got into her mouth. She could feel it beating in her tongue.

At her hip, her sword began to shiver in its scabbard.

She heard it before she understood it: a thin steel whisper, the blade chattering against the wood inside the leather. Her belt buckle answered it. The nails in the counter answered it. Under the counter, in the box where the berth fees went, the coins began to hum against each other like bees.

Through the window behind him, the harbour had gone strange.

Rain still fell on it, but the rain wasn't what was moving it. The whole grey sheet of water was shivering, with no wind anywhere, in fine close ripples that ran from the pier to the harbour mouth and didn't break. Along the northern pier, the rigging of the Natharul hulls had begun to sing, every line on a different note.

Out at the end of the pier, in the watchtower, the harbour bell rang.

Once.

Nobody had touched it. The tower was locked till the evening watch. It rang anyway, one long note over the water, and the note didn't stop.

He sat at the clerk's desk with both hands flat on the wood, one on either side of the ledger. They were nowhere near his sword. Everything else in the room moved, and his hands didn't.

Gurdred had been a soldier for nine years. She'd seen a riot on the grain quay, and a man go into the harbour with a knife in his back, and a Fire-born sailor lose his temper in a hold full of lamp oil. She knew what it was like to stand in a room where something was about to happen.

This was a room waiting to find out whether it would.

She couldn't have said how long it went on. Long enough for the rain to run off her helmet into the collar of her mail, and for her to feel every drop. Long enough for Nyana's book to slip in her arms, and for Nyana to catch it and hold it against her like a child. Long enough for Neldor to shut his eyes.

Then it stopped.

It didn't fade. It stopped, like a held breath. Her teeth let go. Her hands were her own. The sword lay quiet at her hip, and the coins in the box were only coins.

Out in the watchtower, the bell was still humming.

He closed the ledger. He did it carefully, with both hands. Then he set it square on the desk, its edges in line with the edges of the wood, just as the clerk tried to leave it every night and never quite managed.

He stood.

He was taller standing than she'd thought. He looked down at the book. Then he turned to the bench.

"Thank you," he said to Neldor and Nyana. "You were kind to her."

Neldor didn't answer. He was looking up now, at the man's face. Gurdred understood that he couldn't answer.

He didn't wait for one. He crossed the office. The boards didn't creak under him. He opened the door, and the sound of the rain came in, and he went out into it, past Cobb, who was still standing in the open. Cobb didn't turn his head.

The door swung shut.

He'd taken nothing. The ledger sat square on the desk. The fee box hadn't been touched. The water from his cloak lay on the boards round the legs of the clerk's chair, a dark shape drying at the edges.

---

Nobody moved.

Gurdred stood by the desk with her hands on the edge of it. Nyana sat with her book. Neldor sat with his eyes open. The fire muttered, the rain ran down the window, and out in the locked tower on the northern pier, the harbour bell went on humming.

It hummed for a long time. It was a low, round, trembling sound, the sound a bell makes long after anyone has stopped listening, and she found she was listening to it with her whole body. None of them could move while it hummed. She didn't know why. She only knew that she couldn't.

Then it stopped.

Nyana let out a breath that seemed to have been in her the whole time. It came out with a shudder, and she bent over her book and stayed like that.

Gurdred took her hands off the desk. They left two wet prints on the wood.

"Who was that?" she said.

Neldor didn't answer.

"Captain."

"Don't," said Neldor.

"Don't what?"

"Don't write his name. When you write it up. Whatever you write. Don't put his name in it."

"I don't know his name."

Neldor met her eyes for the first time since she'd come in. He seemed older than he had that morning, and he'd looked old enough then.

"Good," he said.

---

The harbourmaster came back an hour later without his hat.

"Where've you been?" Gurdred asked him.

"Out." He hung the cloak on its peg. He didn't look at the clerk's desk. "Is it done?"

"Is what done?"

"Whatever it was." He went behind the counter and pulled the Waluna report out of its tray, and looked at it, and put it in a drawer. "Get me the captain's release. And the permit for the evening tide."

"We've held them four days for that report."

"And now we're not holding them," said the harbourmaster. "Get me the release, Gurdred."

He signed it standing up.

When the clerk came on shift and found his chair wet and his ledger squared, he stood looking at them with his pen case in his hand.

"Who's been at my desk?"

"Wipe the chair," said the harbourmaster.

"But who —"

"Wipe the chair, Aldo."

The clerk wiped the chair.

Nyana stopped at the counter on her way out. She'd put her book in her bag and held the bag with both hands, and for a heartbeat Gurdred thought she'd have a last word about the fire or the benches or the clerk's mother.

"She was a nice girl," Nyana said instead. "The other one. Sera. She lost a fight with a bucket on the second day and laughed about it for a week."

"I'll remember," said Gurdred. She didn't know why she said it, or what good it would do anyone.

"Will she be all right?" Nyana said. "You saw him. Will she?"

Gurdred thought about the hands flat on the desk, and the bell.

"He thanked you," she said at last. It was all she had.

Nyana nodded, as if that were an answer, and went out after her brother into the rain.

---

The Favale rider went at the end of the day.

In pilgrim season every harbour office on the coast reported to the capital at the end of each week, and this was the end of the week. There was a form for it. Gurdred sat at the counter with the form and a pen and stared at the part at the bottom that said *Other Matters*.

"What do I put?" she asked the clerk.

"What happened." He was drying the seat of his chair for the third time. "That's what the form's for."

"I don't know what happened."

"Then put what you saw."

She wrote: *An officer of the Natharul royal*

She crossed it out.

She wrote: *A Natharul officer came alone and examined the Waluna entry. While he sat at the desk the harbour shook and the bell*

She looked at that for a long time.

"Well?" said the clerk.

"It says the bell rang by itself."

"Did it?"

"You heard it."

"I heard a bell." He sat down in his dried chair. "I hear bells every day. Nobody in Favale is going to read a harbour form about a bell."

She crossed it out.

Underneath, in her plainest hand, she wrote: *A Natharul officer inspected the Waluna entry and continued inland.*

She read it over. It was true. Every word of it was true. She folded the form, sealed it with the harbour's sun, and gave it to the rider, and watched him go up the street towards the inland gate at a trot with water bouncing off his shoulders.

He'd be in Favale in a week, if the road held.

---

At the end of every shift, before she took off her mail, Gurdred opened her locker and crossed off a day.

She'd scratched the count into the inside of the door with the point of her knife two summers ago, in rows of five, and she crossed them off the same way, one short scratch through each. It was the last thing she did before she went home. It was the only part of the day that was hers.

She opened the locker and put the point of the knife to the door.

She stopped.

She knew the number. She'd known it that morning on the pier, in the rain. She'd said it to herself with the water running down her neck, and it had been a better number than it used to be. She had known it every morning for two years.

She stood in front of the open door with the knife in her hand, looking at the rows of little scratches, and couldn't remember how many she had left.
