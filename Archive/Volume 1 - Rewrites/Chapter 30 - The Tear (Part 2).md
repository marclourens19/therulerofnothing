# The Tear (Part 2)

They met the crowd on the river road, and the crowd stopped singing.

It was coming along the bank towards the hill, a whole street's worth of it. Families in their best, with the sick carried between them on doors and hurdles. Old men on sticks. Children on shoulders. A sun token at every throat.

They'd been singing since the bells. The front of them saw Thaeroval, and the hymn went ragged and stopped. The quiet went back through them row by row, until the ones at the rear were craning to see what had killed it.

Then they parted.

No one told them to. He didn't slow down, or look at them, or say a word. He walked up the middle of the road with Nereth in his arms and his cloak wrapped round her, and the crowd opened in front of him and closed again behind Seralune.

"Elf," someone said, low, when he'd passed. Someone else spat.

"That's him." A woman's voice, further back. "From the old quarter. That's the one."

"Hush."

"I won't hush. There was an old woman —"

"*Hush*, Marra. Not today. Not on the stair day."

Seralune walked a step behind her brother's shoulder with her hood up, and heard all of it, and kept her face exactly as his was.

It was easier this morning. That was the strange part. She'd sat in the ash all night wearing it, and somewhere towards dawn it had stopped being work and become only a thing she was. Her mouth, closed. Her eyes, far away.

Nereth's marks lay dark and still along her jaw, where the cloak had slipped. They hadn't stirred since the bells.

"Is she breathing?" said Seralune.

"Yes."

"You'd tell me. If she —"

"She is breathing," said Thaeroval.

Twenty paces back, two of the watch had fallen in behind them: the young one from the lane last night, and the older one with the grey moustache who'd taken him by the sleeve. When her brother stopped, they stopped. When he walked on, they walked on. They came no closer than that, and after a while Seralune stopped listening for them.

---

Favale had dressed itself for the day. There were white banners on every pole along the river road, and lanterns hung ready from every bracket, their glass washed and their wicks trimmed. Tonight, Bett had said, they'd light every one in the city at once, and it would all burn till morning.

Seralune looked at the lanterns, and at the word *Bett* in her head, and let both of them go past.

Every lane that ran down to the river road had its own stream of people coming out of it, and every stream met Thaeroval and went quiet.

"He's carrying someone," said an old man's voice, somewhere to the left. "Look there. He's carrying someone up to her."

"An elf carrying someone to *her*?"

"Well, where else would you carry them? Today?"

It went round the crowd like that, in murmurs. *Carrying someone. A woman. Burned, look, her face.* Seralune heard the sound of it change, a little, as it went.

"He killed a woman," said Marra's voice, somewhere behind, not low at all now. "In her bed. My sister's lodger saw them bring the sheet out."

"And now he's carrying one up to her. Maybe that's his penance."

"Elves don't do penance, Jory."

"Everybody does penance on the stair day."

No one came any closer. But fewer of them spat.

---

They passed the old palace, low and dark by the water, with its two banners over the gate: the sun, and a hand's breadth higher, her father's tree. She didn't look. She knew exactly where it was without looking, the way you know where a fire is in a room.

The aqueduct came striding in across the roofs from the west on its long legs of stone, and where it crossed the old river road, one arch stood higher than the others.

The night they came in, Nereth had seen it first and turned her away into a lane before she understood what was hanging there. She'd walked under it since, with a basket between her and Nereth. The last time, she'd stopped and made herself look up, on purpose, for as long as she could bear it, and found she could bear it as long as she liked.

This morning there was no basket, and no Nereth at her elbow.

Seralune looked up.

The woman was still there. They'd said she would be, until the Longest Light, until the pilgrims went home. Her grey shift had gone the colour of the stone. The black sun still hung round her neck on its cord. The birds had been at her. One of her bare feet had turned on the rope's twist so that it pointed at the river, and the other at the hill.

Nell, the kitchens had called her. Her own name, no one had ever asked.

Seralune looked at her for as long as it took to walk under her. She felt nothing. She made sure of it.

Beside her, Thaeroval glanced up once as they passed beneath, the way a man glances at the sky to see if it will rain. Then he looked ahead again.

Just past the arch stood the archivist's house, tall and narrow, with its shutters closed. There were two discs of red wax pressed across the crack of its door now, each with a sun stamped into it, and on the step lay a heap of flowers, most of them brown. Four men had died in there beside somebody's supper. People had come to stand here for a while, for them.

---

They were halfway to the market when the little girl dropped her lamp.

She was walking with her mother at the edge of the road, five or six years old, holding a clay lamp the size of her fist out in front of her in both hands, like something precious. She saw Thaeroval, and her eyes went round, and her hands forgot what they were doing.

The lamp hit the cobbles and burst.

The girl looked down at the pieces. Her face went from surprise into knowing, the whole of it at once, before anyone had said a word.

"What did I tell you?" Her mother had her by the arm. "That was your gran's, that was. That was your gran's lamp."

The girl began to cry.

Seralune watched her all the way past, until the crowd closed over the two of them.

"Do you remember Mother's glass bird?"

It was out before she'd decided to say it.

Her brother walked on. For a few steps she thought he wasn't going to answer.

"The blue one," he said. "From her sitting-room window."

"It had a crack in its wing already. Before I ever touched it."

"It did not."

"It did. A little one, just here." She touched the side of her own hand. "I only picked it up to see the light come through."

"You were six."

"I was six, and I dropped it, and it broke in four pieces, and I ran to you with them in my skirt." She could see it: the blue glass in her skirt, the long corridor, her own feet. "I didn't even cry until I got to you."

"You cried a great deal once you got to me."

"I cut my thumb on the wing."

"You bled on my sleeve," said Thaeroval. "I was dressed for council."

"You told Father you'd done it."

"Yes."

"Why?"

"It was easier."

"It wasn't. He made you —"

"It was easier than you crying."

"I cried anyway," said Seralune.

"Yes," said Thaeroval. "You did."

"Mother knew. That it was me."

"Mother knew everything." He was quiet for a few steps. "Nearly everything."

She looked at him sideways, under her hood.

"You made me count," she said. "That night. I couldn't stop crying, and you sat on the end of my bed and made me count."

"You could not breathe. You were crying too hard to breathe."

"I was afraid of everything."

"You were afraid of the thunder," said Thaeroval, "and the dark, and the gardener's dog, and the fish in the lower pool, which were the size of your hand. You were not afraid of anything that could actually hurt you."

"One breath first," she said.

"One breath first."

He almost smiled. She saw it. It was there at the corner of his mouth, and in his voice, a warmth so small and so old that she'd forgotten he had it. For the space of three steps he was only her brother, walking her home from somewhere with her hand in his.

Then Nereth's head rolled against his shoulder, and he shifted her higher in his arms, and it was gone.

"Keep up," he said.

---

The western market was shut for the feast, every stall boarded. They crossed it down the middle with the silent crowd round them, and came out at the top of the street she'd walked with Bett and a bundle of sheets.

Below them lay the square, and beyond the square, the stair.

She'd seen it scrubbed. She'd seen it lit all through the vigil, from the ash of the lily house: a long slope of lamps going up into the dark. She hadn't seen it like this.

It was full.

From the square at its foot to the doors at its top, it was covered in people. The sick lay on every step, on doors and hurdles and sheets and straw, row above row, so close that the white of the stone only showed in thin lines between them. Their families sat beside them, or knelt, holding up sun tokens on their cords. The vigil lamps were still burning among them, pale in the morning.

At the top, the temple's doors were shut. Above them the gold roofs climbed away up the hill one on another, and the sun hadn't cleared them yet. The whole stair lay in their shadow.

The square below was packed to the walls with people standing, waiting, singing.

"*The Light that keeps the faithful,*" sang the square, slow and huge, "*through the dark, through the dark.*"

Her brother started down into it.

---

The square opened for him too, more slowly.

At the foot of the stair, a little apart from everything, stood a carriage.

The priests had cleared a space for it and roped the space off, and put four of the watch at the rope. It was closed, with its curtains drawn, and it was white. On its door, in gold, stood the royal tree.

"Who's in it?" someone was asking, near the rope.

"The envoy."

"I know it's the envoy. Who's the envoy?"

"Nobody knows. Some lord of theirs. They say the king bowed to him first, in his own hall."

"They say it's the lad off the battlefield. Up north."

"They say a lot of things, Ferrin. They say she'll walk it."

She didn't need to count the stars. She hadn't needed to on the pilgrim road either, the day Nereth went down in the dirt beside her. There'd be nine.

Servants waited round the carriage in grey and green: a man holding the horses' heads, an older woman with her hands folded, and a girl with a basket of cherries on her arm.

The girl was Yseth.

She'd seen them come into the square. She saw the dark armour first, and went white, and dropped her eyes to the cobbles, as any servant of the palace would at the sight of the First Blade. Then her eyes came up again, as a servant's shouldn't, and found the burned face on his shoulder.

Her lips moved. *Nereth.*

Her eyes went past Nereth, to the person walking behind.

Seralune kept her hood forward and her face his. It made no difference. She saw Yseth know her, and not understand it, and decide what to do with it all the same.

Yseth set her basket down on the cobbles.

"Yseth," said the older woman sharply, in Elvish. "Stand still."

"It's her." Yseth's voice was thin. "Maris, it's her. Behind him. It's Her High —"

"Stand *still*, girl."

Yseth didn't stand still. She went to the carriage, and stood at its window, close against the curtain, and spoke.

She spoke for a long time.

The curtain didn't move.

---

"The saint's own water!"

A cart was jammed against the rope at the edge of the space, loaded with little clay bottles stopped with wax. A small man with a big voice stood on its wheel.

"Last day! Last chance! Drawn at dawn and prayed over! Two for a copper and a blessing thrown —"

He saw Thaeroval, and the rest of it dried up in his mouth.

Old Hobb. Bett had pointed him out in the market, with his crowd three deep. He stood on his wheel with a bottle in each hand and looked at the dark armour, and the sword, and what the tall elf was carrying.

Then he got down off his wheel.

"For the lady," he said.

He held out a bottle. He held it out to Thaeroval, and when Thaeroval didn't stop, he turned and held it out to Seralune instead.

"For her lips," he said. "When the saint comes. You wet her lips with it, miss, so she's ready." He said the next part quickly. "It's only river. It's only out of the river. But you wet her lips."

Seralune looked at the little bottle.

"I don't have a copper," she said.

"No. Not today." He pushed it into her hand and folded her fingers over it with both of his. His hands were rough and warm. "Not for her. Light keep her, miss."

"Thank you."

He'd already turned away. She heard him climb back up onto his wheel, and his voice go up again behind her, a little thinner than before. "The saint's own water! Two for a copper!"

The bottle was warm from his hands. She held it, and followed her brother to the foot of the stair.

---

A priest stood at the bottom step.

He was young and fair, in white, with a gold sun on his breast. Two older priests stood behind him, and a line of the watch behind them. He stepped out into the way.

"My lord." His voice cracked on it. He tried again. "My lord, the stair is —"

Thaeroval stopped.

He didn't say anything. He only stood on the cobbles with Nereth in his arms, a step below the priest, and looked at him.

The young priest looked round at the older priests.

"Father Anselm," he said. "The watch said — last night, in the old quarter, the watch said he —"

"I heard what the watch said," said the grey-haired priest.

"Then we can't let him —"

"Can't we?" The grey-haired priest opened his hands a little, the smallest movement. "Tell me how we don't, Tobyn. Tell me that, and I'll do it."

The young priest had nothing to tell him.

He stepped aside.

Thaeroval went up.

---

The stair was the worst of it.

On the road, the crowd had been able to part. Here it couldn't. The sick lay where they lay, and there was no room to move them anywhere. Thaeroval went up between them, stepping where there was stone. The people on either side pulled in their hands, and their feet, and their children, and held their breath while he went by.

On the stair, they didn't say *elf*. Mostly they didn't say anything.

"Mind the baby, my lord —" A woman's voice, a step up, sharp with fright. Then, as he stepped over and not on: "Thank you. Thank you, my lord."

Seralune climbed behind him.

She had to watch where she put her feet. There were babies. There were a great many babies. The lamps burned between them all, going out one by one as the oil ran dry, and no one filled them again.

*Eight*, she counted, to keep her face. *Nine. Ten.*

People watched her pass. They didn't watch her brother. It was easier to watch her.

She counted, and climbed, and let none of it in.

*Forty-one. Forty-two.*

*Forty-three.*

"*Nereth!*"

Bett was on her feet.

She'd come up off the step so fast she'd knocked over a lamp, and she stood with her hands out, staring at the burned face on Thaeroval's shoulder. Behind her, Tilde was half up too, with one hand closed on her sister's skirt.

Agna sat between them.

She'd put on the dark blue shawl, the one with the darn in the corner. She had Kit's paper in both hands in her lap, and she'd been holding it there, Seralune knew, for hours.

"Nereth!" said Bett. "Mam, it's Nereth, it's — what's *wrong* with her? What's he doing with her? Sera! Sera, what happened?"

"Sit down," said Tilde.

"But it's *Nereth* —"

"Sit *down*, Bett." Tilde hadn't let go of her skirt. She was looking at Thaeroval, and she'd gone the colour of the step. "That's him," she said. Not to anyone. "Isn't it. The one they're all saying." Her eyes came to Seralune. "What did he do to her? Sera. What did he *do*?"

Thaeroval didn't stop. He was already on the forty-fifth step, the forty-sixth.

"We came for Kit!" Bett called after him, as though it would make him turn round. "Mam's brought his name, for her shadow! So she'll know him!"

Agna hadn't moved.

She was looking at Seralune.

She didn't look at the elf lord, or at the burned girl in his arms. She looked at the face under the hood, and she knew it. Seralune watched her know it. She watched that hard, fair face take in the hood, and the ears under it, and the brother, all of it at once, the way the little girl in the road had taken in her broken lamp.

"Sera," said Agna.

Seralune looked back at her with her brother's face on.

"Is she alive?" said Agna.

Seralune didn't answer. She didn't nod. She let her eyes go flat, and far off, and none of their business, and she climbed.

Behind her, Agna said to her girls, steadily, "She's alive. He wouldn't carry her if she wasn't. Sit down, Bett. Sit down, now."

*Forty-four.*

Seralune felt the place where they were sitting all the way up the stair behind her, like the place where a tooth has been.

She didn't look back.

---

He stopped near the top.

There were perhaps twenty steps left above them, then a broad landing, then the shut gold doors. The people up here had the look of people who'd fought for their places. They'd come the night before, and the night before that, and slept on the stone. These were the places where the saint's shadow fell first.

Thaeroval stood among them with Nereth in his arms and looked at the step in front of him.

A woman was sitting on it.

She had a little girl across her lap, four or five years old, asleep or nearly, with her lips gone grey and her breath coming in small quick sips. There was a nest of straw under them, and a folded blanket, and a lamp, and a cup of water, and a sun token laid on the child's chest. They'd been here all night. Longer. The straw was flat with them.

The woman looked up at the dark armour, and the sword, and the face.

Thaeroval didn't say anything.

He didn't need to. Seralune watched the woman work it out, fast and plain: who he was, what he was, what he could do, and what it would cost her to make him ask. She'd have done the same sum on any street in the world, with any man who had a sword and that face.

"Liss," said the woman on the next step, low. "Liss, you don't have to. You were here first."

"Hush, Ada."

"You've been here *three nights*."

"I said hush."

She gathered up her daughter. She did it carefully, so as not to wake her. She got the blanket round her, and got her up against her shoulder, and stood. She couldn't carry the lamp, or the cup, or the straw. She left them.

"Here, my lord," she said. "Here."

She went down the steps with the child in her arms. Six steps down, eight, she found a finger's width of stone at the edge of somebody else's sheet and sat down on it, and settled the little girl in her lap again, and looked up.

She didn't look at Thaeroval. She looked at the step she'd left.

Thaeroval knelt and laid Nereth down on it, in the flattened straw, in his cloak.

Seralune knelt on the other side.

The straw was still warm. She could feel it through her skirt: the little girl's warmth, and her mother's, pressed into it all night.

She knelt in it, and her face didn't move.

---

The sun climbed.

It was still behind the temple, burning at the edges of the gold roofs. All down the stair, the temple's shadow lay long and blue across the sick and reached down into the square. *Da said the saint's shadow's very long in the morning*, Bett had said. *It reaches right down.*

Thaeroval stood one step above them, between Nereth and the doors. He'd put his back to the temple and his face to the city, and he stood with his hands at his sides and looked out over everybody's heads.

Seralune broke the wax on Hobb's bottle.

She wet the corner of her sleeve and touched it to Nereth's lips. They were cracked, and there was dried blood in the cracks. Nereth didn't wake. Under the cloak her breath went in, caught on something, dragged past it and went out.

"Burns?"

Seralune looked up.

The voice came from the door beside them. An old man was lying on it, propped on a rolled coat, with a grown son sitting at his feet. He was thin to the bone and grey-bearded, and his chest went up and down in quick, shallow heaves. He was looking at Nereth with frank interest.

"Burns," said Seralune.

"Thought so. The face." He nodded at his own chest. "Mine's the lungs. Forty years at the lime kilns. It gets in."

"Da," said the son. "You're not to talk. You're to breathe."

"I can breathe and talk. I've been doing both since before you were thought of." The old man turned his head on the coat to see Seralune better. "Twelfth year, me." He coughed, a wet, rattling cough, and his son put a hand on his shin and left it there. "My wife came up with me every year. She carried me the last four. First year without her."

"I'm sorry."

"Don't be. She's gone where there's no stairs." He grinned. He had four teeth. "She hated the stairs."

"She never hated the stairs," said the son. "She hated carrying you up them."

"Same thing." The old man wheezed, pleased with himself. "When the saint's done with me, I'm walking down this stair on my own two legs. And I'm going to the Crooked Bell, and I'm drinking till they carry me home, and I'll owe you the carrying, Denny."

"You'll owe me a lot more than that."

His son looked away down the hill.

The old man's eyes went past Seralune to her brother, standing above them. He considered him for a while with the same frank interest.

"She your wife, my lord?"

Thaeroval didn't answer. He didn't look down.

The old man shrugged, as far as a man lying on a door can shrug, and looked back at Seralune.

"Your sister, then?"

"My friend," said Seralune.

She'd never said it aloud. She'd never had anyone to say it to. It came out plain and ordinary, and it didn't crack anything.

Above her, her brother turned his head and looked down at her.

"Your friend," he said.

"Yes."

He looked away again, out over the city, and didn't say anything else.

The old man shut his eyes. "Well. You've a good place, the pair of you. You'll be seen first. She doesn't mind elves, you know, the saint. She doesn't mind anything. That's what makes her a saint."

Down the stair, eight steps, Liss was still sitting on her finger's width of stone with her daughter in her lap. She hadn't taken her eyes off the step she'd given up.

On the step below, Ada had seen Seralune look.

"Three nights," said Ada. "Her girl's had the sweating fever since the thaw. They've a room over a tannery in Cobb's Yard." Her voice didn't rise. "You know what that's like, for a chest? The tannery?"

"No."

"No," said Ada. "I don't suppose you would."

She turned her back, and didn't turn round again.

Seralune took the edge of the cloak where it lay over Nereth and drew it up, and tucked it under Nereth's chin, against the morning cool.

*One breath first.*

She waited.

---

The first of the sun came over the temple roof.

It came all at once, the way it does over the top of a hill: a line of fire along the gold, and then the whole sky behind the temple burning white. Every face on the stair turned up to it.

And the bells began.

It started with the great bell over the doors, the deep one that rang curfew. One note, and the stair went so quiet she heard the lamp beside her hiss.

Then another note.

Then the bells in the towers on either side took it up, and the bells on the lower roofs, and then the bells of every temple and shrine and tower in Favale, near and far, until the whole morning was ringing.

The gold doors opened.

A breath went up the stair from the square. It went up through thousands of people at once, long and shaking, and at the top of it the hymn broke out again. It wasn't slow now, as it had been at the vigil. It was every voice on the hill.

"*The Light that keeps the faithful! Through the dark! Through the dark!*"

She came out.

She stood in the doorway with the sun behind her, lit round every edge, the way Seralune had seen her from the laundry roof: small and white and burning at the rim. Priests came out behind her and stood on either side. She stood there and looked down the stair, all the way down, at all of them.

Then she started to walk.

"She's *walking* it, Mam, the whole stair —"

She came down on her own feet, step by step, into the shadow of her own temple. As she came out of the glare, Seralune saw her for the first time as a person and not a shape.

She was small. She was smaller than Seralune, and slighter, and so pale that the white of her robe looked warm beside her skin. Her hair was hidden under a white hood. Her feet were bare on the scrubbed stone. Her face wasn't beautiful. It wasn't much of anything: a narrow girl's face, tired, with bruised shadows under the eyes, as though she hadn't slept either.

She was no older than Seralune.

At the first step below the landing, she stopped and knelt beside a man on a door, and laid her hands on him.

Seralune couldn't see what she did. She saw the man's family hold each other. She saw the saint bow her head and stay like that for the length of a few breaths, with her hands on the sick man's chest and the bells going over her.

Then she got up, and moved to the next.

The man on the door sat up.

He sat up by himself.

"*Da!*" screamed a girl beside him. "Da, you — Da —"

The scream went down the stair like fire through dry grass, and the hymn roared up behind it. People were on their feet everywhere, reaching out towards the small white figure with both hands.

"Here! Ruler, here, my boy —"

"*Here!*"

She didn't look back at the man she'd left. She was already kneeling at the next.

Step by step, she came down.

---

She came down to them.

It didn't take long. They were near the top, in the good places. Seralune knelt with the straw under her knees and watched her come, kneeling, rising, kneeling, with the priests a pace behind her and the whole hill singing round her, and the bells.

The old man on his door had his eyes open again. His son was holding his hand.

"Here she comes, Da," the son said. His voice wasn't steady. "Here she comes."

"I can see her. I'm not blind. I'm only dying." The old man's hand found his son's wrist. "Denny. If it's not my turn this year, you go home. Don't you sit here all day on the stone."

"Da."

"Promise me."

"I promise."

The saint came down the last step above them, and found Seralune's brother in her way.

He was standing where he'd stood all morning, one step above Nereth, with his back to the temple. When she was a step above him, he turned round. That was all he did. He didn't step aside for her, as everyone on the stair would have stepped aside, as the woman called Liss had stepped aside for him.

The saint stopped.

They were almost of a height, standing like that, with her a step above him. She looked at him. He looked back.

The singing near them faltered, and then it stopped. The stopping spread out from the two of them until there was a ring of silence round that part of the stair, with the hymn still roaring beyond it.

The priests behind her had gone rigid. One of them put out his hand towards her arm.

"Ruler —"

She didn't look at him. He took his hand back.

"No blade is carried on this stair on the Longest Light," she said.

Her voice was low and clear, and it carried. It had been taught to carry. There was nothing in it she didn't mean.

"First Blade," she said. "Lay it down."

Seralune knelt below them with Hobb's bottle in her hand and watched her brother.

He looked at the saint for a long time. There was nothing in his face. His hands stayed at his sides. The sword stayed in its plain black scabbard at his hip, and he didn't touch it.

"No," he said.

It was only the one word. He said it as he said everything.

The saint looked at him a moment longer.

Then she stepped down past him.

She didn't argue. She didn't back away. She went round him on the step as though he were a pillar somebody had built in the wrong place, and came down onto Nereth's step, and knelt in the flattened straw across Nereth from Seralune. She didn't look up at him again.

Behind her, the priests stayed where they were, a step above the man in dark armour. Neither of them tried to go round him.

---

Close to, the saint smelled of candle smoke and cold stone.

She looked down at Nereth: at the cloak, at the burned side of the face, at the blood dried in the cracks of her lips. She looked at the splint on her arm, the two lengths of lath and the strip of curtain. She looked at all of it without any hurry.

Then she looked up at Seralune.

Her eyes were a pale, clear grey. They were steady, and tired, and they didn't go past Seralune to anyone else.

"What is her name?" the saint said.

"Nereth."

"Nereth," said the saint.

She said it as if she were putting it somewhere safe. As if no one had ever said it before, and it needed saying properly, once, by someone who meant it.

"And yours?"

Seralune opened her mouth.

She could feel her brother standing on the step above her, the way you feel a wall at your back in the dark. She knew her true name, and what it would cost to say it here, on this stair, in front of all these people. She knew he'd want her to say nothing at all.

"Sera," she said.

She heard it go out of her. It came out plain. It was the name she'd given Nyana, the one the harbour had written down, the one Nereth said a hundred times a day. It was the only thing on the whole stair that was hers.

Above her, her brother didn't move.

The saint nodded.

"This will hurt her," she said.

"Will it help?"

"I don't know yet." She didn't look away when she said it. "I'll know when I'm in it. Hold her, Sera."

Seralune set Hobb's bottle down in the straw. She slid her arms under Nereth's shoulders and lifted her, cloak and splint and all, and gathered her up against her own chest, with Nereth's head under her chin. Nereth weighed almost nothing. Her breath caught and dragged against Seralune's neck.

"Like that," said the saint. "Don't let go."

She drew the cloak down from Nereth's throat.

The lines lay under the skin, black and purple and swollen. They crawled down from under her jaw, down her neck, past her collarbone and under the edge of her shift. The saint looked at them. Her face didn't change. Whatever she thought of them, she kept it.

She laid her hand on Nereth's throat.

---

It happened all at once.

The Light went into Nereth.

Seralune didn't see it. She felt it, through Nereth's body against her own: a heat that wasn't heat, clean and enormous, pouring in under the saint's small hand. And the lines on Nereth's throat woke up.

They flared white under the skin from end to end, every one of them, and Nereth's whole body went hard in Seralune's arms. Her eyes flew open.

They were open. For the first time since the leaning house they were open, and they were looking straight up at Seralune from a hand's breadth away, and there was no one in them.

Nereth's mouth opened. Her breath went in.

It didn't come out again.

Seralune looked up from them. She looked for anyone.

The bells were ringing. Every bell in Favale was ringing.

The hymn came up the stair like a sea: *through the dark, through the dark*.

At the edge of her eye, her brother's hand had moved at last. It had gone to the hilt of his sword.

Far below, at the foot of the stair, the white carriage stood in its roped square with a servant girl at its curtain.

On the forty-third step, a woman in a blue shawl had got up onto her feet. She was holding a square of paper over her head, as high as her arms would go, for the saint's shadow to fall on.

*Sera*, said her own voice, somewhere, still.

Nereth wasn't breathing.

The saint's hand was on her throat, and under it the light was so white it had no edges. Seralune had seen it before. She had run towards this white through the mud and the smoke, and she had been late —

Her face cracked.

She felt it go. She felt her brother's face come off her like a skin of ice, from the jaw up.

The field came.

All of it.
