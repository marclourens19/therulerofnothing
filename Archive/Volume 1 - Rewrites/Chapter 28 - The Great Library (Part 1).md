# The Great Library (Part 1)

The Keeper's door had no step. It opened onto the river.

It sat low in the Library's wall, a hand's breadth above the black water, an iron-bound door no taller than a man, with a single lamp burning in a niche beside it.

The punt bumped against the stones beneath it. Silas caught an iron ring set into the wall before the current could carry them past.

Nobody spoke.

Helmi had found the punt among the last piers of the aqueduct, where the eel-catchers tied up for the night.

He'd poled it across the river himself, standing in the stern with Darcy's coat buttoned inside his own, and kept them under the far bank in the shadow of the wall, where the lanterns on the river road couldn't reach.

Twice they'd heard boots on the bridge upstream. Twice he'd stopped poling and let the river hold them, and they'd sat in the dark and listened to the watch complain about their feet.

Now he stood in the stern and looked at the door.

Alaric watched him. He'd watched Helmi since breakfast, and he'd learned the old man's face by its stillness. It was stiller than ever now.

"It's past midnight," Redd whispered.

"I'm aware of the hour," said Helmi.

"I'm only saying. If she's asleep —"

"She won't be." Helmi handed the pole to Silas. "She never slept before the second bell. Not once in fifteen years."

"People change."

"Not Idony."

He stepped up onto the thwart, steadied himself against the wall, and knocked.

Three times. Exactly spaced. Then he stood with his knuckles against the iron and waited.

Wena pressed against Alaric's legs in the bottom of the punt. She didn't like the boat. She hadn't liked it since Helmi pushed off from the pier, and she'd spent the crossing with her whole body jammed against Alaric's shins, shivering, watching the black water slide past a hand's breadth from her nose.

Alaric put a hand on her head. His other hand, the right one, he kept against his chest inside his coat, where Redd's knot was.

The lamp in the niche flickered.

Behind the door, something moved: the scrape of a bolt, then another, then a bar lifting out of its brackets. The door opened inwards on a narrow stone passage lit by a candle.

A woman stood in it.

She was small and very straight, with white hair pulled back hard from her face, and a plain dark robe, and a sun token on a cord round her neck, worn so thin with handling that the light came through it.

There was ink on her cuffs. She held the candle in one hand and a book in the other, with her finger still marking the page.

She looked at Helmi. She looked at him for a long time.

"You're late," she said.

"I know," said Helmi.

Alaric's hand closed on Wena's fur.

He'd heard those words before. He'd heard them in lamplight, in the one room he remembered from before the wheat field, in a woman's voice with a laugh underneath it. *You're late again.* And his own voice, answering. *I know.*

He didn't know why it should hurt to hear two old people say them to each other at a door in the dark. It did.

The woman's eyes went past Helmi to the rest of them. Silas, at the pole, with a cut along his cheekbone. Redd, with his father's sword across his knees and his sleeve torn off at the elbow. Alaric, holding his hand against his chest. The dog.

"You've brought friends," she said.

"I've brought something older than you are."

"Nothing's older than I am. Ask the novices." She closed the book on her finger.

Her eyes went over them once more, the blood and the sword and the dog, and came back to Helmi's face. She didn't ask about any of it.

"What do you need?" she said.

"The pool," said Helmi. "Tonight. Before the —"

"Yes," said Idony.

The river slapped at the stones under the door. She stood back against the wall of the passage, holding the candle up, to let them by.

"Get the dog out of the boat," she said, "before it drowns us all."

Silas was the last out. He stood in the doorway with one hand on the frame and pushed the punt off with his boot, and the river took it away into the dark.

"Good," said Idony, and shot the bolts behind him.

---

Her rooms were built into the thickness of the wall.

There were two of them, low and narrow, and every inch that wasn't door or window was books. They were stacked on the floor and along the sills and under the narrow bed.

A small fire burned in a grate no bigger than a bread oven. In a niche above it, a gilt sun caught the candlelight, with a dish of oil and a dead wick in front of it.

Idony set her candle beside the sun. She touched two fingers to her forehead, briefly, without seeming to know she did it.

Then she turned round.

"You said yes before I'd finished," said Helmi.

"I've had a long time to decide."

"Then let me finish anyway." He didn't sit down. "The watch change at the second bell. Four of them won't be there to be changed."

The room went quiet.

"They came to my house after curfew," Helmi said. He said it in the same careful voice he'd used over the core, weighing every word before he set it down. "For the dog. They're dead on my kitchen floor, all four, with the supper still on the table. You should know that before this goes any further. You should know what you're letting in."

Idony turned to the dog, sitting against Redd's legs. Then to Silas.

"Two," said Silas.

She turned to Redd.

Redd opened his mouth, and shut it, and looked at the floor. "One."

Her eyes came to Alaric last.

"One," said Alaric.

Idony stood straight-backed in the middle of her small room with the fire behind her. Her hand had gone to the sun token at her throat. Her lips moved. Alaric couldn't hear the words, but he knew the shape of a prayer when he saw one. He'd seen enough of them on the pilgrim road.

When she'd finished, she let go of the token.

"What do you need from the pool?"

Helmi unslung Alaric's satchel from his shoulder and set it on her table, between two piles of books. He opened it and folded back the cloth.

The core lay in the candlelight: a clear shell as big as two cupped hands, thick as a thumb, with its black centre hanging in the middle like a seed in a grape.

Idony didn't touch it. She bent over it, very close, with her hands behind her back, the way Helmi had bent over it that morning in his own house. For a breath the two of them might have been the same person.

"Where did you get this?"

"He carried it," said Helmi, and nodded at Alaric. "Across the Expanse."

"Nobody carries anything across the Expanse."

"He did."

Idony straightened. She looked at Alaric properly for the first time: his face, his torn coat, the hand held against his chest. He made himself look back.

"What is it?" she asked him.

"I don't know," said Alaric. "That's why we came."

She nodded. It seemed to be the only answer she'd have accepted.

"The night office runs till the third bell," she said. "Every priest in the building is in the chapel or supposed to be. After that, the whole place wakes up again." She was already moving: to a chest under the window, to a hook on the door. "Copyists work through the night. Nobody looks twice at a copyist. You'll be mine."

She pulled out a stack of long brown smocks and dropped them on the bed.

"Over everything. Especially the blood." She glanced at Redd. "And the sword stays under it."

"It's my father's," said Redd.

"Then keep it under your father's smock." She held one out to him. "Copyists don't talk. If anyone speaks to you, you look at your feet and you think about ink. Can you do that?"

"I've never thought about ink in my life."

"Then it'll be a new experience for you."

Redd took the smock. For the first time since Helmi's kitchen, the corner of his mouth moved.

Idony held the next one out to Silas, and didn't let go of it straight away.

"You've done this before," she said.

"Worn a smock?"

"Walked into a place you'd no business in, dressed as somebody else."

Silas took the smock. "Once or twice."

Idony turned to Helmi. She didn't say anything. She held out the last smock, and he took it, and their hands were both on it for a breath longer than they needed to be.

"You haven't asked me why," said Helmi.

"I've had thirty years to ask you why," said Idony. "I'll ask you afterwards."

"There may not be an afterwards."

"There's always an afterwards." She picked up her candle. "That's what this building is for."

---

The Library was never dark.

Alaric had thought it would be. Yesterday afternoon, from the last rise above the plain, it had looked like a place built to keep things and not give them back: a grey square mass on the far side of the river, with no gold on it anywhere.

He'd imagined it inside as black and cold and shut, like a vault in a story.

Instead there was light everywhere.

Lamps burned along every passage, in iron brackets high on the walls, with no one tending them that Alaric could see. They came out of Idony's stair into a long hall where the lamps hung on chains from a ceiling so high the lamplight hardly reached it.

Tables ran down the middle of the hall in rows. At one of them, a young woman in a brown smock was asleep with her cheek on the page she'd been copying and her pen still in her hand.

The young woman stirred as they passed, and lifted her head with the ink printed backwards on her cheek. "Keeper? Is it the office?"

"Not yet, Maren. Go back to sleep."

She put her head down on the page again. Idony walked on without slowing. The four of them followed in their borrowed smocks, heads down, with Wena at Redd's heel on the end of her rope.

"Don't look up," Idony murmured.

Alaric looked up.

The ceiling was painted from end to end. It went on and on over their heads, pale and faded and cracked, lit faintly from below by the swinging lamps. He saw armies on it. He saw burning towns, and a great river full of the dead, and people with their arms raised against a sky full of fire.

At the far end, over the doors, everything ended in a sun: an enormous gold disc that filled the ceiling from wall to wall. Its rays reached down into all the burning and the dead, and the fires went out wherever they touched.

"The Remaking," Helmi said quietly, beside him. "And the Light that ended it. It's the first thing the pilgrims see."

"Is that what happened?"

"That's what's painted."

"I said don't look up," said Idony, without turning round.

They passed benches along the wall where the pilgrims must sit on feast days. Every inch of the old wood was scratched with names. Hundreds of them, cut in with knives and pins and fingernails, one on top of another, in every size of letter.

Some of them were so old they'd worn back into the grain.

Redd trailed a finger along one as he passed. "Should've brought a knife."

"You did," said Silas.

"A little one, I mean. For names."

"Copyists," said Idony, "don't talk."

Near the end of the hall, a man was kneeling in an alcove before another painted sun. He was old, much older than Idony, in white that had gone the colour of cream, and he got up by stages as they came near, one hand on the wall.

He had a lamp beside him on the floor and a face like a walnut.

"Keeper," he said.

"Aubin."

"You keep late hours."

"The Light keeps later ones."

The old priest smiled at that. It had the sound of something they'd said to each other many times. His eyes went over the copyists, one after another, not very interested. Then they came down to the end of Redd's rope.

"That," he said, "is a dog."

"It's the Vaults' new ratter."

"Is it?"

"The cats have grown lazy."

The old priest studied Wena for a while. Wena gazed back at him and thumped her tail once on the floor.

"The cats," said Aubin, "will have something to say about that."

"The cats can take it up with me."

He laughed, a dry small sound like paper tearing, and lowered himself back down onto his knees. "Light keep you, Keeper."

"And you."

They went on. Alaric felt the old man's eyes on the back of his neck all the way to the stair, and then he didn't.

The cats did have something to say.

It was waiting on the top step of the stair that went down, a big grey tom with a torn ear and a tail like a bottlebrush, and it saw Wena at the same moment Wena saw it. Wena's whole body went rigid. Her lip lifted. A sound started somewhere deep in her chest.

Redd dropped to one knee and got both arms round her neck.

"No," he breathed into her ear. "No, no, no. Not tonight. Tomorrow you can have him. Tomorrow you can have every cat in Favale."

The cat considered them. Then it turned its back, with enormous contempt, and walked off down the hall with its tail in the air.

Wena whined through her nose, heartbroken.

"That's Tollan," said Idony. "He was here before me. He'll be here after."

---

The stair went down a long way.

It turned and turned inside the thickness of the wall, and every turn it got colder, and it went on long after Alaric had stopped expecting it to end.

The lamps grew fewer. Then there were none, and Idony took one down from the last bracket and carried it, and the walls went from painted plaster to bare stone, and then to a stone that was older and darker and sweated in the cold.

"We're under the river," Helmi said.

Alaric didn't ask how he knew. He could hear it. It wasn't a sound so much as a weight: the feeling of a great deal of water moving somewhere above his head.

Redd must have felt it too. It made him talk.

"How far down does it go?" he whispered.

"Far enough," said Idony.

"Is it true there's a giant's skull down here? My da said there was a giant's skull in the Library the size of a cart, and the priests keep it under a —"

"Redd," said Silas.

"I'm only asking."

"It's a whale's," said Helmi.

Redd twisted round on the stair. "What's a whale?"

"A fish the size of a street."

"You're having me on."

"He isn't," said Idony, without turning round. "East range, ninth shelf. He catalogued it himself."

"Badly," said Helmi.

"Very badly. You put it under fish."

"It is a fish."

"It isn't a fish, Helmi. We had this out in the year of the flood."

Redd looked back at Alaric with his mouth open. Alaric found, to his own surprise, that he wanted to laugh.

"Copyists," said Silas, "don't talk."

The stair ended in a door, and Idony unlocked it with a key from a ring at her belt, and beyond the door were the Vaults.

They were rooms. Long, low rooms, one opening into the next, with shelves on either side from floor to ceiling and a narrow aisle between. The air was so dry it pulled at the inside of Alaric's nose.

Idony's lamp went along the shelves and showed him books, and boxes, and rolled things in leather cases, and bundles tied with tape, and more books, and more.

On the end of every shelf was a label. Small, square, yellowed, pasted to the wood. Each one had a few words on it in a close, careful, backward-leaning hand.

Helmi stopped.

He'd been walking steadily behind Idony since the river door, and now he stopped dead at the end of a row. He was looking at a label.

He lifted a hand and touched it lightly, with two fingers.

"That's my hand," he said.

Idony had stopped too, a few steps on. She didn't turn round.

"I never changed them," she said.

"Your hand was always better."

"Yes." Now she looked back at him. "I didn't change them anyway."

"What does it say?" Redd whispered.

"'Rolls of arms.'" Helmi's fingers didn't leave the label. "'The northern marches. Before the Remaking.'" He touched the next one down. "'The same, continued.'" And the next. "'The same, continued. Damp.'"

"Damp?"

"It got damp," said Helmi. "In the year of the flood."

He went on down the row with his eyes, label by label, into the dark. Alaric watched him and understood that the old man was reading fifteen years of his own life off the ends of shelves.

He must have come down here every day of the thirty since, in his head. He'd told a little girl about these rooms at bedtime instead of stories.

Redd shifted on his feet. Wena's nails ticked on the stone.

"Helmi," said Silas quietly.

"Yes." Helmi took his hand away. "Yes. I'm coming."

---

The last room of the Vaults ended in water.

There was no door. The shelves stopped, and the floor went on a few paces past them and then went down, in three broad steps, into a pool that filled the far end of the room from wall to wall.

The water was black and perfectly flat. Idony's lamp lay on it without a tremor, a second lamp, upside down.

At the far side, the wall came down into the water in a low arch, and the arch was drowned. Its curve showed above the surface for a hand's breadth, no more. The rest of it went down into the dark.

"That's the way," said Idony.

Redd looked at the arch. He looked at Idony. "That's a wall."

"It's an arch. The reading chamber's on the other side of it."

"Under the water."

"Through the water. The room itself is dry." She set the lamp down on the top step. "The first Keepers made it so. Nobody reaches the pool by accident, and nobody reaches it in a hurry, and nobody reaches it with anything they aren't prepared to get wet."

"How far?" asked Silas.

"Six pulls. You keep one hand on the roof of the arch and pull yourself along it. When the roof goes up, you come up." Idony was unbuttoning her robe at the neck, briskly, without any fuss. She hung it on a peg on the wall behind her and looped the ring of keys over her head. "I've done it twice a week for thirty years. I'm sixty-six. None of you has any excuse."

Alaric looked at the black water.

The last time he'd gone under water, it had been a river in flood, in the dark, and the current had turned him over and over until he didn't know which way was up.

He could still feel it if he let himself: the cold closing over his head, and the stone slamming his shoulder, and his torn palm splitting open again on a root.

He didn't let himself.

"I can't swim," he said.

Everybody looked at him.

"I don't think I can. I don't remember doing it." He kept his eyes on the arch. "I went into a river once, and it didn't go well."

"You won't need to swim," said Idony. "You need to hold your breath and pull. Can you hold your breath?"

"Yes."

"Then you can do this."

"What about Wena?" said Redd.

Idony eyed the dog. Wena was pressed against Alaric's legs, staring at the water with her ears flat. "Can she hold her breath?"

"She's a dog."

"Dogs can. Most things can, if they have to." Idony considered her. "Carry her. Hold her head against your chest and her mouth shut, and don't let go whatever she does."

"She'll kill me."

"Probably," said Idony.

Silas was watching Alaric. "I'll go first."

"No," said Alaric.

It came out before he'd decided to say it. He heard it in the room, and so did the others. Redd's eyebrows went up.

"Someone has to take the lamp through," Alaric said, "and light it on the other side, so the rest of you can see where you're coming up. Helmi can't. Idony shouldn't have to. You're needed on this side, for Helmi." He looked at the water, and not at Silas. "I carried the core here. I'll go first."

Silas looked at him. Then he nodded once, and that was all.

Idony took a second lamp from a niche by the steps, wrapped it in oilcloth with a flint box tied to it, and handed it to him. "There's a ledge on your right when you come up. A niche above it. Light the lamp in the niche and keep it there. Don't put it near the water."

"Why not?"

"Because the water has to be still," said Helmi, "and a lamp's heat will stir it, if you're careless."

Alaric took off his sword and gave it to Redd. He took off the smock and his coat, and stood in his shirt with the wrapped lamp in his left hand. His right hand, with Redd's knot on it, he held against his side.

Silas's eyes went to it. "That was a bad cut."

"It's tied."

"That's not what I asked."

"I can pull with it," said Alaric, and turned away to the steps before Silas could look any harder.

Helmi was folding his coat on the top step. He did it carefully, corner by corner, and he took out the thing he'd buttoned inside it all night, and folded that too, and laid it on top.

It was a child's coat. Small, and blue once, faded almost to grey.

Idony saw it. Alaric watched her see it. Her hand went to the sun token at her throat, and stayed there, and her face did something he'd never seen a face do: it held perfectly still while everything behind it moved.

She didn't ask.

Helmi didn't tell her.

He smoothed the blue coat flat with the side of his hand, once. Then he straightened up and faced Alaric.

"Six pulls," he said. "Don't hurry them."

Alaric went down the steps into the water.

It was colder than the river had been. It came up his legs and over his waist and took his breath away, and the bottom went on down under his feet, step after step, until the water was at his chest and the curve of the arch was right in front of his face, with only a hand's breadth of air under it.

He could see where it went down. He couldn't see where it came up.

He put his right hand flat on the underside of the stone. The cut under Redd's knot pulled.

He took a breath.

He went under.

The cold closed over his head, and everything went black and loud, and his body remembered the river before his mind could stop it: the roaring, the turning, the stone. His chest locked. His hand scrabbled on the roof.

*Pull,* he told himself, and pulled.

The stone was smooth under his palm, smoother than any stone had a right to be. He hauled himself along it.

*Again.*

The lamp dragged at his other hand. His shoulder scraped the side of the arch.

*Again. Again.*

His chest was burning. The dark was total. He had no idea whether he was going forward or down.

*Again.*

His hand came off the end of the stone into nothing. He kicked, badly, and came up.

Air. He dragged it in, and it was cold and dry and tasted of stone, and it was the best thing he'd ever breathed.

He was in the dark. The water was up to his chest. He turned in it, blind, and his hip struck a ledge, and he got his arm onto it, and then the rest of himself, and lay on cold stone with the water running off him, breathing.

After a while he sat up and found the flint box.

Four tries failed, with his hands shaking. On the fifth, the tinder caught, and he lit the lamp, and set it in the niche above the ledge, where Idony had said it would be.

The light went up the walls.

He was in a round room, not much bigger than Idony's rooms together. Its walls went up and curved over into a dome, and the whole of it was one pale grey stone without a single join that he could see.

It hadn't been built. It might have been poured, or grown. Or someone had reached into the rock and lifted out a room-shaped piece, and left the rest.

Nothing in Favale was made like this. Nothing he'd seen in the whole of Mydea was.

The water he'd come through filled the middle of the floor: a round pool, as black as the one on the other side. The ledge he sat on ran all the way round it. On the far side, set into the wall, were an iron chest and a reading desk and a stone bench.

He sat on the ledge in his wet shirt and looked at it all, and had the feeling you get in a house where someone has just left the room. He waited for it to mean something. It didn't.

The water heaved.

Silas came up out of it with a gasp, and found the ledge, and hauled himself out beside Alaric. He looked round the dome once, very fast, as he did every new place. Then his gaze settled on Alaric.

"Six," he said.

"Was it?" Alaric's teeth were chattering. "It felt like a hundred."

Redd came next, with Wena. He'd carried her through. He came up spluttering and swearing with his arms locked round her, and the dog came up with him in a flailing, furious, dripping heap, and got her front paws on the ledge and scrabbled out, and shook herself all over all of them.

"She bit me," said Redd, from the water. "Under there. She bit my ear."

"You held her under a wall," said Silas.

"For six pulls! She could have waited six pulls."

Idony came through as neatly as a seal. Helmi came last, and took his time. Silas and Redd had to reach down and take an arm each and lift him out, and he sat on the ledge with his thin shoulders heaving and his white hair plastered to his head, and for a while he couldn't speak at all.

When he could, he looked round the dome.

"I haven't seen this room," he said, "in thirty years."

"It hasn't changed," said Idony.

"No. It never does. That's the point of it."

"Who made it?" said Redd. He was turning round and round on the ledge with his mouth open, dripping.

"No one knows," said Idony.

"The first Keepers found it when they dug the Vaults," said Helmi. "They built the Library on top of it, and then they spent six hundred years writing down everything in Mydea except where this came from."

"It was old when Favale was a village," said Idony. "That's all anyone knows."

Helmi wiped the water out of his eyes. "Has the core got wet?"

"It's glass," said Silas.

"It's not glass."

"It's wet, then."

Helmi opened the satchel with his shaking hands and unwrapped the core, and held it up to the lamp. Water ran off the shell. The black centre hung in it, unchanged.

"Good," he said. "Now we wait."

"For what?" said Redd.

"For the water to forget us," said Idony.

She'd gone round the ledge to the stone bench, where a folded robe lay waiting, and she was wringing out her hair. "Five people and a dog have just swum through that pool. It's the stillest water in Mydea, and we've churned it like a mill race. It takes an hour to forget you. Sometimes more. Until it has, the core will show you nothing but your own faces."

"An hour," said Redd. "Sitting here."

"Sitting here."

"Wet."

"Sitting perfectly still," said Idony, "and wet, and silent. No talking. No moving. The water hears you."

Redd opened his mouth.

"Not even that," said Idony.

---

It was the longest hour of Alaric's life.

They sat round the ledge, wet through, with their backs against the curved stone, and didn't move. The lamp burned in its niche without a flicker. The cold rose out of the stone and into Alaric's bones, and his teeth wanted to chatter, and he held them still.

Across the pool, Redd sat with Wena lying across his boots, and his hand buried in the fur of her neck. The dog had her chin on her paws and her eyes on the water. She didn't move either.

Alaric didn't know how Redd had managed it. He thought perhaps Wena understood, as she seemed to understand most things that mattered.

Silas sat with his eyes closed. He might have been asleep. He wasn't.

Helmi and Idony sat side by side on the stone bench, not touching. Once, when Alaric looked, Helmi's lips were moving, and he realised the old man was reading the room: counting something, or naming something, over and over, silently, the way other men prayed.

The water went still.

It happened so slowly that he couldn't have said when. The rings from their coming spread out and out and flattened against the walls, and grew fainter, and then there were no rings. There was only a black disc, flat as glass, with the lamp lying on it upside down and the dome curving over it.

He could see his own face in it, from where he sat. A pale shape. A wet head.

Helmi stood up.

He did it in stages, so slowly that Alaric could hardly see him move. He knelt at the edge of the pool with the core in both hands.

Idony knelt beside him and tied a silk cord round the shell, in a knot Alaric had never seen, and Helmi lowered the core down over the water, hand under hand, a finger's width at a time, until it touched the surface.

It went under without a ripple.

Helmi let the cord out until the core hung just beneath the surface, a hand's depth down, an arm's length out from the edge. Its black centre was darker than the black water round it.

Then he sat back on his heels and let the cord lie slack across his fingers, and didn't breathe.

Nobody breathed.

Alaric looked at the water.

He saw the water.

He saw the lamp lying on it. He saw the dome, curving. He saw the core, faintly, a hand's depth down: a glimmer of clear shell with a darkness in the middle of it. He saw his own face, pale, looking up at him out of the black.

That was all.

He looked up.

Redd's mouth had fallen open.

He was staring at the water with his whole face, and his eyes were moving, fast, from one side of the pool to the other, following something.

Beside him, Wena had lifted her head off her paws. Her lips had drawn back from her teeth. Her hackles were up, all along her spine, and a sound was starting in her chest that she didn't let out.

Silas had opened his eyes. He was leaning back against the stone, very slightly, the way a man leans away from a fire that's too close.

Helmi's lips were moving again. He was counting, fast.

Idony had both hands flat on the stone of the bench, pressing down.

Alaric looked back down at the water.

Water. The lamp. His own face.

He looked at the others again. They were all watching the same place, the middle of the pool, and all of their eyes were moving the same way at the same time, from left to right, and back, and up. Something was happening in the water. Something big enough to make Silas lean away from it.

He couldn't see it.

He sat with his back against the cold stone and his wet shirt stuck to him, and watched four faces and a dog watch something that wasn't there, and felt the cold go all the way into him, deeper than the stone could reach.

Then all of them flinched at once.

Redd's arm went up over his eyes. Silas turned his head sharply aside. Idony gasped, a small sound, and Helmi rocked back on his heels. Even Wena ducked her head and shut her eyes tight. All of them, in the same breath, as if a door had been flung open onto the sun.

There was no light.

The lamp burned in its niche, exactly as it had burned all along. The dome was grey. The water was black.

Slowly, one by one, they lowered their arms and opened their eyes, and looked at the water again, and saw what he saw.

Nothing.
