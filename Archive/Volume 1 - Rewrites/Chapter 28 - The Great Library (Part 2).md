# The Great Library (Part 2)

Helmi drew the cord up, hand over hand.

The core came out of the pool streaming water. He held it to the lamp. It was what it had been all along: a clear shell with a black centre, cold and wet and quiet.

For a while the only sound in the dome was the water dripping off it.

Redd broke first.

"There were machines," he said.

His voice came out hoarse.

"Walking." He swallowed. "Silver ones. Big as a house, and not a join in them anywhere, and they were walking. Like men made out of bells." He stared round at all of them. "They were alive."

"Not alive," said Helmi.

"They were moving."

"Moving isn't alive."

"It was to me," said Redd.

Alaric sat very still against the curved stone.

He knew those machines. He'd woken up among them, in the ash of a wheat field, with his face in the mud. He'd looked up at them with the crows sitting on them. He'd walked between them with Gerolt's hand on his arm.

He'd never seen one move.

"There were beastfolk in the line," said Silas.

He said it quietly, to the water. "Horned. Big. Shoulder to shoulder with men, and on the same side. And elves in the rank behind."

"That isn't possible," said Idony.

"No," said Silas. "I know what I saw."

*Men don't die shoulder to shoulder with beastfolk.* Gerolt had said that at his cabin window, with his back turned. *Not in any war I've ever heard of.*

"The banners," said Helmi.

He'd set the core down on the ledge. His hands had started to shake again, and not from the cold. "I didn't know them. I've read every roll of arms in this building. I've catalogued every banner that was ever painted or stitched or sung about in Mydea, and I didn't know one of them." He looked up. "Not one."

*I didn't know a single banner on that field.*

Alaric pressed his back into the stone until it hurt.

It was the field. His field. The pool had shown them the field whole, with the dead still on their feet and the machines still walking and the banners flying over all of it.

The core he'd carried out of the ash had kept every bit of it, and shown it to everyone in this room except him.

"And then the light," said Idony.

The silence after that was worse than the one before.

Redd's head turned.

"Al," he said. "Al, did you —"

He stopped. His eyes went over Alaric's face, and whatever they found there made up his mind for him.

"Course you did." He turned back to the water. "Course you did. You're white as milk."

Alaric said nothing.

He waited for someone to ask. Silas, who asked about everything. Helmi, who'd want the colour of every banner and the number of legs on every machine. He waited, with his heart knocking at the bottom of his throat, for one of them to turn round and say, *Alaric. What did you see?*

None of them did.

That was the worst part. It was so easy.

---

Idony got up.

She did it stiffly, like a woman rising off her knees after a long vigil. She went round the ledge to the reading desk and put both hands flat on it, and stood with her back to them.

"I've read that," she said.

Helmi's head came up.

"Not seen it. Read it." She didn't turn round. "Silver engines that walked like men. The banners of beasts in the line of men. And then a light, and after the light —"

"— there was no field," said Helmi slowly, "nor any who stood upon it."

They said the last words together. Alaric didn't think either of them had meant to.

"What's that?" said Redd.

"The end of a book." Helmi was staring at Idony's back. "The last line of a chronicle of the Remaking. Every novice who's let into the Vaults learns it, because it's the strangest ending in the Library. Three hundred leaves of armies, and kings, and who burned what, and who married whom. And then that." He rubbed his face with a wet hand. "I catalogued it. I was twenty-six. I wrote the label myself."

"What did it say?" asked Silas.

"'The Remaking. Author unknown. Incomplete. One leaf wanting.'" Helmi's mouth twisted. "The last gathering's short by a leaf, just before the end. Someone cut it out a very long time ago."

"No one cut it out," said Idony.

She turned round.

"It isn't wanting," she said. "It's here."

Helmi got to his feet. It took him two tries. "Here."

"In the chest."

"Idony —"

"There's a list," she said. "You know there's a list."

"I know there's a list. I was never sworn to it."

"Seven things." She didn't look away from him. "The Keeper swears on the sun she was given at her first office never to show them to anyone. Not to a priest. Not to the high priest. Not to the Ruler herself, if she came down those stairs in her own person and asked. The Keeper keeps them here, where nobody comes in a hurry. And if the Library burns, she burns them first."

"And you swore it," said Helmi.

"The morning they gave me your keys."

"What's on the list?" said Silas.

"If I told you that," said Idony, "I'd have broken it."

Redd looked from one old face to the other. "Then don't," he said. "You don't have to. We don't need —"

"Don't," said Helmi.

He said it to Idony, not to Redd. Alaric stared at him. Helmi, who'd spent a whole morning bent over the core and forgotten there was anyone else in the room. Helmi, who'd told a little girl about these rooms every night instead of stories.

"I didn't come here to make you break an oath," Helmi said. "I came for the pool. We've had the pool."

"You came thirty years late." Idony's voice didn't rise. "You came up out of the river with blood on you and a thing in a bag that's just shown me the last line of a book I've read every year of my life. And now you'd like me to wrap it up in its cloth and say goodnight."

"I'd like you to keep your oath."

"I've kept it. I've kept it for thirty years." She lifted the ring of keys off over her head. "Do you know what it's like, being the only one who's seen a thing? Every year, alone, down here. Nobody to say, *Yes. I see it too.*"

Helmi said nothing.

"I'm tired of being the only one," said Idony.

She touched two fingers to her forehead and said something under her breath, too low for anyone to hear. Then she knelt in front of the iron chest and put the key in the lock.

---

It was a single leaf of old skin, brown at the edges and soft as cloth.

She took it out of a flat wooden case, from between two sheets of yellowed silk, and laid it on the reading desk. Helmi moved the lamp from its niche to stand beside it.

The rest of them came round the ledge, dripping, and stood in a half-circle behind the two old people, with Wena pressed against Alaric's knees.

The whole of one side was painted.

It was a battle. It was painted small and busy, the way old books painted things: rows of tiny men with tiny spears, and banners like fingernails, and among them silver shapes on jointed legs, done in real silver that had gone black with age.

The armies came in from both edges of the page. They filled the brown field from corner to corner.

In the middle of the field stood a woman.

She was painted larger than everything round her, as old painters made kings bigger than their soldiers. She was an elf. The ears under her crown were long and fine.

The crown was gold leaf, and so was her gown, and the gold had kept its shine through everything, so that she stood in the dark field like a lamp. One of her hands was held out to her side.

Her face was gone.

It hadn't been scraped away. The paint was all still there. Alaric could see the colours in it, the pale of the skin and the dark of the eyes and the red of the mouth.

But it had moved. Every line of her face had been dragged sideways, the way wet paint drags under a thumb, until the eyes were long smears and the mouth was a streak of red. All of it pulled the same way.

Towards the place beside her.

Beside her there was nothing.

There was a shape where the paint stopped. It was the height of a man. The field ran up to it on every side, and the sky came down to it, and the gold of the woman's outstretched sleeve ran up to its edge and stopped there.

Inside the shape the skin of the page was bare. It wasn't scraped or stained. It was clean and pale, as clean as the day it had been stretched.

Alaric looked at it.

It hurt.

It was like pressing on a bruise: a dull, deep ache behind his eyes and under his breastbone that came when he looked at the empty place and went when he looked away. He looked away. He looked back, like a tongue going back to a broken tooth. It came again.

It wasn't his bruise. He didn't know how he knew that. It was like pressing on somebody else's and feeling it in his own body.

He didn't say anything. He put his hand on Wena's head and kept looking.

"Someone really didn't like her," said Redd.

"Or him," said Silas.

Redd turned.

Silas nodded at the empty place. "That's a man. Look at the shoulders."

Redd looked. His face changed.

"Who is she?" he said.

"There's no name." Idony was holding her sun token so hard her knuckles were white. "There are no words on the leaf at all. The scribe wrote three hundred leaves, and he never wrote a word on this one."

"The church did this?" said Silas.

"That's what every Keeper before me believed. After the Remaking, the church went through the old books and made them decent. Zealots with knives. They scraped out a heretic and spoiled a queen's face, and the leaf was too holy to burn, so they hid it." She glanced at Helmi. "I believed it too, for about a month."

Helmi had found the drawer in the desk without being told where it was.

Alaric watched him open it. Everything inside had its own place. Cotton gloves, folded. Brass rings with lenses in them. A thin silver rod. It was the same as the chest under Helmi's stair, laid out the same way, down to the fold of the gloves.

Helmi pulled on the gloves. He bent over the leaf with a lens to his eye, and he was quiet for so long that Redd shifted his feet.

"Nothing scraped this," he said.

"I know," said Idony.

"A scraper lifts the nap. Pumice leaves the skin furred. You can wash a page, and the ink will ghost back through it in a hundred years. There's nothing here." He moved the lens. "No nap. No fur. No ghost. It's cleaner than the margins."

"Then what did it?"

"Nothing did it. It was never painted."

"It was painted. Look at the edge."

Helmi looked. Idony leaned in beside him, not quite touching, and pointed with her little finger, a hair's breadth above the skin.

"Her sleeve," she said. "The gold runs right up to the edge and stops in the middle of a stroke. And the sky's the same. You can see the brush go into it. Nobody paints a sleeve into nothing."

"So it was painted —"

"And then it wasn't."

"And nothing touched it in between," said Helmi.

He took up the silver rod.

He drew its tip across the field first, across the rows of tiny soldiers, lightly, with his head on one side. It made a sound. Alaric could only just hear it: a faint dry whisper, like a fingertip over cloth.

He drew it across the woman's gold gown. The whisper went higher and thinner.

He drew it across the empty place.

It made no sound at all.

"Do that again," said Redd.

Helmi did it again. The rod went across the field, whispering, and into the empty shape, and the whisper stopped as if someone had put a hand over its mouth. It came out the other side and began again.

Redd let his breath out, slowly, through his teeth.

Then Helmi moved the rod up to the woman's face.

It caught. Alaric saw it catch. It dragged in the smeared paint like a comb going against the grain of hair. Helmi drew it back the other way, towards the empty place, and it went smooth.

"Every grain of it," said Idony softly. "Like iron filings, when you hold a lodestone near them. I've looked at it a hundred times. Every grain of her leans the same way."

"Towards him," said Helmi.

Alaric's chest hurt.

"How old?" said Silas.

The two old people looked at each other across the leaf. It was the look of two people who'd worked across one desk for fifteen years.

"The hand on the leaves either side of it is the old court hand," said Idony. "No one's written it since before this city had a name."

"The gold's laid on gesso, not size."

"The skin's been limed the old way. We stopped doing that eight hundred years ago."

"The blue's ground stone. Not glass."

"Nine hundred years, at the least," said Helmi.

"A thousand," said Idony.

Helmi looked down at the leaf. "A thousand," he agreed.

Then he turned and picked up the core.

He held it in his gloved hands beside the painted field, the black centre next to the tiny silver machines, and Alaric saw everyone in the half-circle look from one to the other and back.

"The last thing this saw," Helmi said, "is the last thing in that book. The same engines. The same banners. The same light. Whoever poured the first mana into this core stood on that field, a thousand years ago." His voice cracked on it, just once. "Darcy was right. She was right about all of it."

At the name, Idony's head came round. She studied Helmi's face, and then looked down at the leaf, and didn't ask.

"A thousand years," said Redd. He tried the words out like words in somebody else's language. "It came out of a machine on a farm. In Mydea. This summer."

Silas wasn't looking at the core. He was looking at Alaric.

"So what does that make him?" he said.

Nobody answered.

Idony looked at Alaric, and then at Helmi, and said nothing. Helmi looked at the core. Redd looked at his wet boots. Alaric looked at the empty place on the leaf, where a man should have been standing, and felt it ache.

---

A voice came out of the roof.

It was so small and so far off that at first Alaric couldn't tell where it came from. Then it came again, dry and thin, falling out of the top of the dome.

"Idony."

Every head went up.

At the very top of the dome, where the curve closed, there was a hole he hadn't noticed: round and black, no wider than a hand. A faint breath of air came down it.

So did the voice.

"Keeper. Are you below?"

Idony had gone white. "Aubin," she said.

"The watch are at the great door. A sergeant and six men, with a list." There was a pause. The old voice had to rest between sentences. "They want the Keeper and the Vault keys. I've told them the Keeper is at her prayers." Another pause. "I'd be quick about them."

Then nothing.

"It comes up behind the sun in my rooms," Idony said. "The first Keepers found it. It's how the Keeper hears the bell for the office when she's below." She was already moving, pulling the borrowed robe off over her head, folding it onto the bench. "Helmi. The leaf."

"I'll put it away."

She met his eyes. For a heartbeat she seemed about to say something else. Then she dropped the ring of keys into his gloved hand, and closed his fingers over it, and let go.

"When I go up, I'll leave the shaft open," she said to the rest of them. "You'll hear everything that's said in my rooms. If you can hear them, they can hear you. Not a word. Not a breath." She pointed at Wena. "Not a sound out of her, either."

"She'll be quiet," said Redd.

"She'd better be."

Idony went into the pool. The black water opened and closed over her head. Then there was only the ripple, spreading out towards the walls, and the lamp shaking on it in pieces.

---

Nothing came down the shaft.

They sat round the ledge in the lamplight, wet and shivering, and looked up at the hole in the roof. Redd had both arms round Wena.

Helmi had put the leaf back in its silk and its case and the chest, and locked it, and now he sat on the stone bench with the keys in his lap and his eyes closed.

"Will she tell them we're here?" Redd whispered.

Helmi didn't open his eyes. "Idony has never told a lie in her life," he said. "Not once. Not even to the examiners, the morning after the cup." His thumb moved on the keys. "She didn't lie to them. She just didn't say anything."

"Then what's she going to say?"

"I don't know," said Helmi.

Silas pointed at the hole in the roof, and they were quiet.

Then, far up, a door.

Boots on boards. A clink of metal. Voices.

They came down the shaft small and hollow and strange, like voices at the bottom of a well, if the well were over your head.

"— sorry for the hour, Keeper." A man, tired and flat. A voice that had said the same thing at a great many doors tonight.

"The Light doesn't keep hours, Sergeant." Idony. She sounded exactly like herself. "Nor do I. What is it?"

"Four of ours dead. Down by the hanging arch, at the archivist's house." A rustle, like the pages of a book. "Helmi. You'll have known him, Keeper."

On the bench, Helmi's eyes stayed closed.

"Thirty years ago," said Idony's voice.

"He's gone. Him and three men and a dog, out into the dark after curfew, with our lads on his kitchen floor. Orders are, every house of the Light's to be turned out before morning. Every temple door."

"This isn't a temple."

"It's the temple's, Keeper." He didn't sound happy about it. "Anyone come in tonight? Anyone you didn't know?"

"No one comes through the great door after curfew, Sergeant. Ask the doorkeeper."

"And your copyists?"

"Eleven, the same as every night. You're welcome to count them. They're in the long hall, and they'll be glad of the interruption."

Someone up there laughed and swallowed it.

"And below?" said the sergeant. "The Vaults?"

"The Vaults are kept, Sergeant. They always are."

Every word of it was true. On the bench, Helmi's face didn't change.

"Is there anyone below, Keeper?"

The silence went on and on.

Alaric saw Helmi's lips move on the bench. He couldn't hear what Helmi was saying. He thought it might be her name.

Then, clearly, in the voice she must have used for thirty years to read the office:

"By the Light the Ruler carries, there is no one below."

In the dome, no one moved.

Up in the shaft, someone let out a breath. One of the watchmen said something, low and quick. "She swore it, Sarge."

"I heard her." The sergeant was quieter now. "I'm sorry, Keeper. I didn't mean to make you swear."

"You didn't make me do anything."

"No." Boots shifted. "All the same, I've orders. Nobody in or out of this building till the priest comes with the seal. He'll do the Vaults at first light, for form's sake, and the halls after. I'll have a man on every door. The river door too."

"And my rooms?"

"A Keeper's rooms are sworn ground. I know that much." A pause. "Goodnight, Keeper. Light keep you."

"And you, Sergeant."

The door. The boots, going away. Then nothing.

They waited.

The quiet went on until Alaric's legs ached with keeping still. Then something came down the shaft again. It was faint. It was Idony's voice, alone in her room over their heads, saying words he half knew. He'd heard a whole road sing them under a hanging woman. *The Light that keeps the faithful through the dark.*

She got that far, and stopped.

She didn't start again.

---

The pool heaved, and Idony came up out of it for the third time that night.

She didn't need lifting. She hauled herself onto the ledge and sat there in her wet shift with the water running off her, and didn't wring out her hair, and didn't reach for the robe on the bench. Her hands lay open in her lap.

"There's a man on the river door," she said. "Two on the great door. One on the pilgrims' door, and one at each of the little doors on the lane, and they've a fire going in a brazier in the forecourt, so they mean to stay. The priest comes with the seal at first light."

"We heard," said Helmi.

"I know you heard. I left it open." She tipped her head back towards the black hole in the dome. "I said it in front of the sun."

Helmi opened his mouth, and shut it again.

"I stood in front of it and said it, and I waited." Her voice was level. "I've been told all my life what happens to people who swear falsely by the Light. I was taught it before I could read. So I waited."

"Idony," said Helmi.

"Nothing happened." She turned her hands over and looked at them, as though they belonged to somebody else. "The lamp didn't even flicker."

Helmi got up off the bench. He went round the ledge, steadying himself on the wall, and sat down beside her on the wet stone. He didn't touch her. He sat close enough that their shoulders nearly did.

"Thank you," said Silas, from across the ledge.

"Don't," said Idony. "I don't know yet what it's cost."

After a while, she straightened her back.

"My rooms will hide two," she said. "And the dog, if the dog can hold her tongue. The watch won't search them, and the priest won't either. A Keeper's rooms are sworn ground." Something bitter touched her mouth. "I've a good deal of sworn ground left, it seems, even if I haven't much else."

"Two," said Silas.

"I can explain one stranger at my table. Two, if they're dull. Not four. Four is what they're looking for."

"And the other two?"

Idony looked at the pool.

"The pool has to be fed," she said. "If it weren't, it would have sunk into the rock before the first Keeper was born. The river comes into it through a conduit. It runs under the river and comes up under the old quarter, below the Lily Steps, where the women do their washing."

She wiped her face. "The Keeper before me showed me the mouth of it the morning she gave me your keys. The Keeper's last door. For fire, or war, or anything else that comes down those stairs."

"How far?" said Silas.

"Six rungs down the wall to the mouth. It's the width of a barrel. Forty pulls, and then the roof lifts, and there's air under it. A hand's depth. Then it bends to the left and runs forty more to a grate." She paused. "The grate's rusted through at the bottom, on the left. The old Keeper swam it once, for a wager, when she was a girl, and that's what she told me."

"When was that?" asked Redd.

"About seventy years ago."

"Oh, good," said Redd.

Helmi hadn't looked at the pool once.

"I can't," he said.

He said it simply, the way he'd said *It's real* over the core. "I did six tonight, and you had to lift me out at the end of them. I can't do eighty."

There was nothing to argue with.

"Then I'm staying," said Redd.

Every head turned.

He was sitting with his back to the wall and Wena lying across his boots, and he didn't look up from the dog. His face had gone red. It always did when he expected to be laughed at.

"I'm not a parcel," said Helmi.

"No," said Redd. "Parcels don't argue."

"Redd —"

"I'm not leaving you in a cupboard on your own." Redd's hand had gone still in Wena's fur. "Not after last night. I didn't do that in your kitchen so you could get caught in a cupboard with nobody but her." He jerked his chin at Idony. "No offence."

"None taken," said Idony.

"I'm staying," said Redd, "and that's the end of it. Al can look after himself. Al's got Silas." He looked up at last, and his eyes were wet, and he glared at Alaric, daring him to say so. "Haven't you?"

"Yes," said Alaric.

"Well, then."

Helmi hadn't said anything more. He was looking at Redd like a man who'd found something on a shelf that he'd never catalogued.

"You'll have to feed him," he said to Idony, without turning his head.

"I've fed novices for thirty years. He can't eat more than a novice."

"I can," said Redd.

Silas was already looking at the water. He'd been looking at it since Idony said *the width of a barrel.*

"I'll go first," he said.

"No," said Alaric.

It came out the same way it had at the arch. He hadn't decided to say it. He only knew, when he heard it, that it was right.

"You can't swim," said Silas.

"It isn't swimming. It's pulling." He made himself hold Silas's eyes. "If I go first and get stuck, you can pull me back out by the feet. If you go first and get stuck, I can't pull you anywhere. You're heavier than me. You're stronger than me."

Silas said nothing.

"If I don't come back," said Alaric, "don't come."

"Then I'm right behind you," said Silas. "I'll have hold of your ankle the whole way. If you stop, I'll know."

It wasn't agreement. Alaric took it anyway.

---

There wasn't much to settle.

The swords stayed. Silas unbuckled his and laid it on the stone bench beside Redd's father's, and Alaric's, which Redd had carried through the arch on his back.

"Three," said Redd, looking at them. "I'm not carrying three swords round Favale for the rest of my life. You come back for yours."

"We'll come back," said Silas.

"Both of you. And keep your head down."

"I always keep my head down."

"You've never kept your head down in your life. You only look like you have."

Silas kept his knife. He took the sergeant's little book out of his coat, where it had got damp at the edges in the arch, and wrapped it in the oilcloth the lamp had come through in. He tied the parcel flat against his chest under his shirt.

"What's in it?" asked Idony.

"Names," said Silas. "Every door they've knocked on this week."

Idony's gaze rested on the parcel. She didn't ask anything else.

The coats stayed. They'd only drag. Alaric's was still on the step on the far side of the arch, folded with Helmi's, and the small blue coat on top.

The core stayed too.

Helmi wrapped it in its cloth again and put it back in Alaric's satchel. Then he stood holding the satchel against his chest with both arms, as a man holds a sleeping child, and looked at Alaric over the top of it.

He didn't say anything. It was a long look. Alaric had seen Helmi look like that at a core, and at a label on the end of a shelf, and at a leaf of painted skin. He'd never had it turned on him before.

"It's safer in a chest than in a river," said Silas.

"Yes," said Helmi, still looking at Alaric. "It is."

Then there was only the dog.

Wena knew. Alaric didn't know how, but she did. She'd been lying across Redd's boots with her chin on her paws, and when Alaric knelt beside her she got up and pressed her whole weight against him, and pushed her head under his arm, and stayed there.

*Take Wena.*

Gerolt had said it lying in his own blood, with his thumb moving once between her eyes. Alaric had taken her. He'd taken her out of a flooded river, and across the Expanse, and all the way to Favale, and she'd hardly let him out of her sight since.

Eighty pulls in the dark. She'd hated six.

He put his thumb between her eyes, the way Gerolt had, and moved it once.

"Stay," he said. "Stay with Redd."

Wena whined through her nose.

He looked up at Redd.

"Take Wena," he said.

Redd's face twisted up. He knelt, and got his arms round the dog's neck, and held on.

"I've got her," he said. "I've got her, Al. Go on."

"Redd —"

"Go on, I said. Before she bites me again."

Silas caught Helmi's eye over the dog's back. "Don't let him talk."

"I'll do my best," said Helmi. "I raised a daughter who wouldn't ask for the salt. I've no idea what to do with one who never stops."

---

They went, in the end, in the order Idony gave them.

She went first, to see the stair was clear. Then Redd, with Wena clamped against his chest and his eyes screwed shut, and the three swords tied across his back. Then Helmi, last, with the satchel strapped to him and Idony waiting on the other side to take his arms.

Before he went under, Helmi turned round in the water, with one hand still on the ledge.

"When you see Darcy —" he said, and stopped.

Alaric waited.

"No," said Helmi. "I'll tell her myself."

He took a breath, and went under, and the pool closed over his white head.

The ripples spread out across the water and slapped against the walls, and grew smaller, and stopped.

Then it was the two of them, and the lamp, and the dome.

---

They didn't talk much while they waited.

Silas sat on the ledge above the place Idony had shown them, where the rungs went down. He'd found the first of them with his foot and gone still. Alaric sat beside him with his arms round his knees, shivering.

"You were right," Silas said, after a while. "About going first."

"I know."

They listened to the drip of water somewhere in the dark.

"Will Redd be all right?" Alaric asked.

"Redd's got an old man to look after and a dog to keep quiet, in a room full of books he isn't allowed to touch." Silas's mouth moved. "He'll be too busy to be anything else."

Silas glanced sideways at him. His eyes went to Alaric's right hand, where Redd's knot was, and rested there, and came away again.

He didn't ask anything.

Alaric was very glad of the dark in the corners of the dome.

Some time later, Idony's voice came down out of the roof. It was very tired.

"The bell's gone for the dawn office. Go now." Then, after a pause: "Light keep —"

She stopped. They waited. The voice didn't finish.

"Keep your right hand on the wall," it said instead.

---

The first rung was under the lip of the ledge. Alaric went down it into the black water, and the cold climbed him rung by rung until it closed over his head.

He found the next rung by feel, and the next. At the last of them, his feet went down into nothing, and when he groped below him with one hand there was an edge of stone, round, like the rim of a well turned on its side.

A hand closed on his ankle.

He went in.

It was black at once. The lamp behind him turned the water brown for a pull or two, and then there was nothing, not even the memory of light. He put his right hand on the wall and his left on the roof, and pulled.

*Forty,* Idony had said. He'd meant to count them. The cold took the count out of his head with the first pull, and it didn't come back.

The roof was close. His knuckles scraped it. The walls were close. When he pulled, his shoulders brushed them on both sides.

*Pull.*

Silas's grip on his ankle was the only thing in the world that wasn't stone or water.

*Pull.*

He'd thought the cold would be the worst of it. It wasn't. The worst of it was that there was nowhere to go but on. At the arch, the other side had been in reach from the start. Here there was only the next pull, and the one after that, and the stone closing round him like a sleeve.

*Pull.*

His chest began to ache.

The ache became a pressure. The pressure became a fist. He kept pulling. His hand slipped on something soft and slimy on the wall, and for a heartbeat he didn't know which way was forward.

*Pull. Pull.*

His body wanted to breathe. Not a little: more than it had ever wanted anything. It was going to, whether he let it or not, and he pulled and pulled, and his throat jumped, and his belly jumped, and he kept his mouth shut on the jumping.

His hand went up and hit no roof.

He went up after it, and his face broke into air.

The conduit swelled there, a little, like a throat. The air under its roof was a hand's depth, as Idony had said, no more. He had to tip his head right back and press his face up against the stone to get his mouth and nose into it. The air was cold and stale and tasted of rust. He sucked it in, and he'd never been so glad of anything.

Beside him in the black, water heaved, and Silas came up too, and dragged at the air with a sound like tearing cloth.

"Slow," Silas said. His voice was right against Alaric's ear, flattened by the stone. "It's old air. Slow."

Alaric breathed slow. He couldn't see anything. He could feel Silas's shoulder against his, and the roof against his nose, and his own heart slamming away at his ribs.

"Again," said Silas.

He breathed again.

"Ready?"

"No."

"Go," said Silas.

He went.

The conduit bent to the left. He felt it bend under his right hand. After the bend, the water changed: it was colder, and it had begun to move. It pushed against him, gently at first, as if the river at the far end were breathing out through the pipe.

*Pull.*

His chest had filled with the old air, and the old air wasn't as good as it had tasted. It ran out faster.

*Pull.*

He was going too slowly. He knew it, and he couldn't go faster. The water shoved at his face. His arms had turned to rope.

*Pull.*

The fist was back in his chest. It had brought friends.

*Pull —*

His hand struck iron.

Bars. He felt along them, blind. They ran from roof to floor, a hand's width apart, furred with weed, and they didn't move when he shook them. Beyond them the water moved faster. He could feel the river on the other side, pulling.

Bottom left. Rusted through at the bottom left.

He went down along the bars. His hands found the floor, and the corner, and there, at the bottom of the left wall, a gap where a bar should have been. He got his head into it. His right arm. He pushed.

His shoulders stopped.

The gap was too narrow. The broken stub of the bar dug into his back, and the next bar dug into his chest, and he was wedged between them with his head and one arm in the river and the rest of him in the pipe.

He pulled.

He pulled with everything he had. The iron ground into him. He didn't move.

**THOOM.**

His heart hit the inside of his ribs so hard that the whole world jumped. His throat opened. He clamped it shut. Water came in at the corners of his mouth anyway, and he swallowed it, and his chest heaved, and the grip on his ankle tightened until he could feel every finger of it.

He pulled again. Nothing. His heart hit him again, and again, each beat a little further from the last. He was going to die here, in a drain, in the dark, with his head through a door.

*Don't pull.*

It was Silas's voice, in the grey light under the colossi, with Redd playing the villain in the sand.

*Pulling's a fight. Turning's a door.*

He stopped pulling.

He let his body go slack against the bars, and it was the hardest thing he'd ever done. Then he turned. He dropped his left shoulder and lifted his right, and twisted slantwise through the gap, as you'd turn a table to get it through a doorway.

The iron scraped his back. Something caught his right hand and tore away, and he felt Redd's knot go.

Then he was through, and the river had him.

The grip on his ankle was gone.

He turned in the current, blind, with his lungs on fire, and grabbed the bars before the river could take him. He felt along them to the gap. An arm was coming through it. A hand, reaching, closing on water.

He took the wrist.

He didn't pull. He felt the shape of Silas wedged in the gap, shoulders square to it, as his own had been, and he twisted the wrist, turning it, turning the whole arm and the shoulder behind it slantwise, and Silas turned with it, and slid, and came through the bars into the river like a cork out of a bottle.

Up.

There was grey above them. Alaric kicked for it, badly, with one hand locked in Silas's collar, and the river rolled them over, and his shoulder hit stone, and then his face was in the air.

He breathed river and air together and choked on both.

The current was dragging him along a wall of rock. A flat, pale edge went past above him. He threw his free hand at it and caught its edge, and held, and the river hauled the rest of him downstream until his arm nearly came out of its socket.

His other hand was still knotted in Silas's collar.

He'd done this before. He'd done it with his fingers locked in a dog's collar, in the dark, in a flood. He knew how to do this part.

He held on.

Silas's head broke the surface beside him. His mouth was open. He wasn't breathing.

"Silas!"

Silas's eyes were open, and fixed on nothing. The river pulled at him. Alaric pulled back, and got his knee onto the flat rock, and then his hip, and dragged, and dragged, until Silas's chest was on the stone and his face was turned sideways on it.

Then Silas's whole body heaved, and water came out of him, a great deal of it, and he coughed, and retched, and coughed, and dragged in one long, whooping, terrible breath.

And another.

Alaric lay on the rock beside him in the grey light and listened to him breathe.

It was a while before Silas could speak. When he did, it came out in pieces, between coughs.

"The grate," he said. "You turned me."

"You taught me."

"I taught you on a wrist." Silas coughed up more of the river and lay with his cheek on the wet stone. "Not a whole man."

"It worked on a whole man."

Silas shut his eyes. The corner of his mouth moved in a way that on anyone else would have been a laugh.

---

There was no one at the washing rocks yet.

The river went by, brown and fast, with a thin mist lying on it. Up above them a flight of pale stone steps climbed from the rocks between two leaning houses. The middle of every step was worn hollow by feet.

Silas got up onto his hands and knees and stayed there for a while.

"Up," he said at last. His voice was wrecked. "Off the rocks. Before the women come."

They went up the steps on their hands as much as their feet. At the top, where a house should have been, there was a gap full of weeds and black stone.

The walls had burned down to stumps, none higher than a man's shoulder. A doorway still stood on its own with a charred lintel across it and nothing on either side. A young elder tree was growing out of the old hearth.

In one corner, where two stumps of wall met, somebody had built a little shrine: a stone basin on a plinth, with a sun painted above it and a jar of flowers in front.

Silas pulled Alaric down behind the wall beside it, out of sight of the lane, and they sat with their backs against the stone.

Alaric was so cold he couldn't feel his hands. He put his head down on his knees and shook.

Above his head the wall went on up a little way, rough and black with soot, and he didn't look at it. He didn't look at anything. He sat in the ruin with his eyes shut and water running out of his hair and shook, and shook, and waited for it to stop.

Then every sound in Favale stopped at once.

The carts, the voices, a dog somewhere, the shutters going up: all of it, all together. Alaric knew that silence. He'd stood in it yesterday morning, in a square full of people, while a small white figure came out at the top of the temple stair and looked out over the city.

He didn't lift his head to see.

Then the noise came back, and up on the hill a bell began to ring. One deep note, and then again.

The sun came up over the roofs and reached into the ruin. It lay across the black stones and across the flowers in the jar, and across Alaric's knees, and he felt it on the backs of his hands before he felt anything else.

He lifted his head.

Silas was sitting beside him, with his head back against the wall and his eyes open. The cut on his cheekbone had opened again in the water. A thin pink line of blood ran from it down to his jaw, and he didn't wipe it away.

Then Silas turned his head and looked at Alaric's hand.

It was lying on Alaric's knee in the sun, palm down. Redd's knot was gone, torn off on the grate.

Silas reached over and took Alaric's wrist.

He did it slowly, the way he'd have taken hold of something that might bite. He turned the hand over, palm up, into the light.

Last night a frightened boy's blade had gone across that palm and up the inside of the forearm. Alaric had seen the bone. Silas had seen the blood. There'd been enough of it on Helmi's floor.

There was a line across the palm now. It was thin and pale and perfectly clean, like a crease in paper.

Silas pushed the wet sleeve back with his thumb. The line went on up the inside of the forearm, just as thin, just as clean, and faded out before the elbow. That was all.

Silas looked at it for a long time.

He didn't let go. He looked up from the palm to Alaric's face, and his own face was very still, the way it was before he decided anything.

"How long?" he said.

Then he sat with Alaric's wrist in his hand, in the first of the sun, with the river running off both of them onto the stones, and waited for the answer.
