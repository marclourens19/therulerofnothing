# Chapter 1 - A War Without Sound

"…hear me, lad?"

The words reached him without their beginning. A man's voice drifted somewhere above him, blurred by the pounding inside his skull.

He tried to answer. His tongue found grit between his teeth, and the breath he forced through his throat carried no voice with it. Worse, he could not remember what his own voice was supposed to sound like.

Wet earth pressed cold against his cheek. Something heavy pinned him from his left shoulder to his hip. An arm lay across his ribs, its fingers brushing his jaw whenever he breathed.

The skin was cold. Whoever lay on top of him was dead.

The air smelled of iron, ash and burnt meat. Crows called nearby, sharp and impatient.

*Where am I?*

He reached for whatever had come before the mud. No fall returned to him. No road, no room, not even the fading edge of a dream. There was only the ground beneath his face and the dead weight holding him against it.

His heartbeat struck the inside of his skull.

**THOOM.**

His body jerked a fraction after the sound.

He tried to lift his right hand. Nothing happened. When the effort left him, his fingers dragged through the mud as though the command had reached them late.

"There. I saw that."

The voice was closer now. A warm hand touched the side of his neck, two fingers finding his pulse.

"Don't try the rest of you yet. Can you open your eyes?"

Mud had dried along his lashes. He forced them apart and caught a blade of white sky before the glare drove them shut again.

"Slowly," the man said. "The sun isn't going anywhere."

The boy tried again.

A dead man's hand lay inches from his face, its fingers still curled around a spear shaft snapped off halfway up. The arm crossed the boy's chest and disappeared beneath the body weighing down his back.

He shoved at it before he knew he meant to move. Armour ground against his shoulder, and what little air he had vanished.

"Stop. He's caught across you." The living hand closed around the boy's shoulder. "I'm going to pull him clear. Hold still or his armour will cut you."

The boy fixed his eyes on the broken spear and stopped fighting.

The man grunted. Weight shifted across the boy's back, slowly at first and then all at once. A metal buckle dragged along his side. The dead hand carved a furrow through the mud as the body rolled away and struck the ground beside him.

Air rushed into the space it had left. The boy coughed until the taste of iron filled his mouth again.

"That's him off." The man's hand returned to his shoulder. "I'm turning you onto your back now. Slowly."

One hand took his shoulder and the other his hip. The movement drove pain through the centre of his skull, and the cry that left him sounded nothing like a voice he knew.

**THOOM.**

"Aye. I know. I'm sorry. That's the last of the moving for now."

The hands stopped trying to shift him and held him steady instead. The boy squeezed his eyes shut until the pain loosened enough for him to hear the crows again.

When he looked up, a broad man with grey in his beard was leaning over him, blocking the worst of the sun. His clothes were coarse and dirt-stained, and he kept glancing beyond the boy as though something there might move if he looked away.

"Still with me?"

The boy managed a small nod.

"Good enough. Don't force your voice. Show me where it hurts most."

He pressed a hand to his head.

"Your head. Aye. Hold still. I need to know whether the rest of you is put together."

Rough, calloused hands worked carefully over his shoulders, arms and ribs before moving down his legs. Wherever the blood lay thickest, the man checked twice, searching the skin beneath it with growing care.

The dark cloth covering the boy was smooth beneath the mud and blood. A buckle had just scraped hard across his side, yet the fabric had not torn. The man's thumb paused over the fine seam at one cuff, but only for a moment before he returned to searching for a wound.

He found none.

"Not a mark on you," he muttered. "All this blood, and not one mark."

His hands rested on his knees while he looked past the boy once more.

The boy followed his gaze as far as he could from the ground. He saw boots, a broken shield and another body lying face-down beyond the man. There was more farther out. He could tell from the crows.

He planted one hand in the mud.

"No. Stay down."

The boy pushed anyway. His arm began to shake before it had lifted him an inch.

"I need to see." The words scraped their way out, but they were his.

The man looked towards whatever lay beyond them, then back at the boy.

"Aye," he said. He slid one arm behind the boy's shoulders. "I suppose you do. Slowly, then. Put your weight on me."

Together they raised him. The world smeared into brown, red and white, and he caught a fistful of the man's sleeve before he could fall sideways.

"Steady. Let it settle."

The arm behind him did not move until the colours separated and the ground stayed beneath him. Only then did the boy look past the man's shoulder.

He forgot to breathe.

Scorched earth stretched towards the horizon, and the dead covered almost every part of it.

The boy tried to count the nearest bodies. The number fell apart before he reached twenty. Behind every corpse lay another, and beyond those were hundreds more, scattered among broken shields, abandoned weapons and armour split open far enough to show what remained inside.

Some had died fighting, their swords still buried in shields or held above their heads halfway through a final blow. Others had simply fallen where they stood. One soldier remained on his knees with both hands wrapped around a spear, though there was no wound on him that the boy could see. Nearby, a horse lay with its forelegs stretched forward as if it had collapsed in the middle of a stride. Its rider was trapped beneath it, reaching towards another man whose fingers rested inches away.

Neither man had reached the other. The battle had not ended so much as been *interrupted*.

Seamless silver structures stood above the bodies. One had collapsed on jointed legs taller than a man. Another lay on its side, its smooth front broken only by a narrow opening blackened around the edges.

The boy knew carts and wheels. He knew what hammers, fire and ordinary hands could shape in a forge. None of that explained these things, yet the word *machine* came to him before he could ask for it. He could not have said how they worked. He knew what they had been built to do.

Pale soldiers with pointed ears lay among the men. Farther out were creatures with horns, claws and shoulders broad enough to strain the armour wrapped around them. One horned warrior had died beside a human soldier, close enough that their shoulders touched. Their weapons pointed in the same direction.

*Were they fighting together?*

Banners lay trampled among the dead. Their colours should have divided the field into armies, but he knew none of them. The faces offered no more: human, pale, horned; young enough to have been frightened and old enough to have learned how to hide it. Every one was a stranger.

*Was I here with them?*

The question met a blank so complete that, for a moment, the bodies frightened him less.

A crow landed on a broken shield and hopped onto the man beneath it. The bird buried its beak below the edge of his breastplate and pulled. The wet tearing sound carried across the field because there was nothing else to cover it.

The boy turned away—and saw where the destruction ended.

The dead did not thin out towards the edge. Burned earth did not soften into trampled grass. Ruin simply stopped, and beyond the final corpse, rows of wheat stood beneath the wind, their golden heads brushing together.

He searched for the path the army had taken. Thousands of soldiers should have crushed the crop beneath their boots. Horses, carts and those towering machines should have left roads of torn earth behind them. There were no wheel ruts, no abandoned supplies, no trail of broken wheat leading towards the horizon.

Nothing had entered through the crop. The entire battlefield had appeared in its midst without bending a single stalk beyond it.

Behind him, the old man released a breath.

"All of it," he said. "Poor wheat, but mine."

The boy turned. The farmer stared beyond the bodies at the untouched rows, his jaw tight enough to move the muscles beneath his beard.

"I go to bed with a field I've walked every furrow of since I was your age, and I wake up to—this." He gestured once at the corpses without looking at them directly. "Hip-high. Nearly ready for the sickle. I could've told you the exact day I'd cut it."

"You were here?" The boy's throat scraped around the words. "Did you hear what happened?"

"Hear it? Lad, a fox puts one paw wrong near my henhouse and I'm awake before the other three follow. Fifty winters on this land, and I know every sound it's ever going to make at me."

He crouched beside the nearest corpse. His hand moved towards the ruined breastplate, then stopped before touching it.

"Last night I heard wind. Two owls arguing over the same tree, same as always." His hand returned to his knee. "No shouting. No horns. No steel on steel. You cannot put this many armed fools in one place and have them kill each other silently. It doesn't happen. It has never once happened."

"Then when did they come?"

"I went to sleep with a field, not a graveyard. That's the whole of what I know, and I'd give a great deal to know more."

The boy looked across the bodies again. "Have you found anyone else alive?"

The farmer did not answer straight away. His eyes moved over the nearest dead as though another survivor might rise simply because he had been asked to look for one.

"I found you."

The wheat hissed in the wind. Somewhere behind them, a crow's beak clicked against armour. The boy looked from one body to the next and waited for a chest to rise.

"And the others?"

"No." The farmer looked towards the wheat as soon as he said it.

A violet spark moved between two blackened stones near the boy's knee. It crawled over one, stretched across the gap and struck the other with a dry crack. He flinched.

"Don't touch it," the farmer said, pulling him back by the collar before the boy had even leaned closer.

"What is it?"

"If I knew that, I'd have given you a warning worth a damn instead of just grabbing you." He watched the light strain between the stones. "Whatever in the Last Dark it is."

The spark divided into thin branches before disappearing into the soil. Others moved farther across the field, flashing between armour and patches of burned earth. In several places, frost remained beneath the midday sun. The frost swallowed the light instead of throwing it back, leaving the ground beneath it dim.

Then the boy noticed the circle a short distance from where he had awakened.

A perfect boundary divided it from the destruction. Outside, the earth had burned, frozen or split apart. Inside, green grass survived beneath a clear layer of frost, every blade pressed flat in a spiral that tightened towards the centre.

There were no bodies inside. No blood, armour, bone or metal. Not even ash carried by the wind had crossed the boundary.

Only a single white flower stood near its edge, its folded petals untouched.

The boy stared at the empty centre. Some part of him insisted it should not have been empty.

*Someone was there.*

His left hand closed around nothing.

The movement came before thought. For one heartbeat, a wrist filled his grip, warm beneath his thumb. Then his fingers struck his own palm hard enough to hurt.

He tore his hand open. A word had risen with the sensation, whole and certain.

『Magic』

The boy's breath caught. He knew what it meant.

*How do I know that?*

The remembered warmth vanished. The taste of blood and ash filled the back of his throat, thickened by the stench of opened bodies, and his stomach clenched hard enough to fold him forward. He covered his mouth too late. Bile and bloody spit struck the scorched earth between his hands.

"Easy—by the Four, lad!"

The farmer dropped beside him, one hand at his back and the other clamped around his arm. The first words reached the boy clearly. Everything after them sounded farther away.

The ground shifted beneath his knees. Crows lifted from the bodies, their wings becoming black streaks across his failing sight. The silver machines leaned together as their edges blurred.

"Look at me. Come on now, boy, stay with me."

The boy tried, but the farmer's face would not hold still long enough for him to find it. His arms gave way.

The old man caught him before he struck the ground, swore under his breath and hauled him over one shoulder like a sack of feed.

"Ten paces," the farmer muttered, rising with a grunt he did not bother to hide. "You couldn't have fallen ten paces nearer the house. Heavy little shite, and half-starved besides. Where do you even keep it—"

He talked through the whole walk back, low and constant, the way a man talks when he needs to keep his own hands steady.

Past the farmer's back, at the edge of the boy's narrowing vision, a violet spark stretched between two stones. It thinned until it was no wider than a strand of hair.

Then it snapped.

---

When he opened his eyes again, the smell of death was gone.

Stewing vegetables had replaced it, earthy and sweet, along with the sharp tang of burning pine.

For a while, the boy lay still and breathed. Someone had wiped the mud and dried blood from his face. A heavy wool blanket covered him, and a straw mattress crackled softly whenever he moved.

A stone hearth popped and hissed nearby, throwing shadows up rough wooden walls and across thick ceiling beams. One of those shadows seemed to linger after the fire shifted. He blinked, and it behaved itself.

There was a small table with a candle burning low, iron pots hanging from hooks, and a shuttered window black with night. By every measure, it was an ordinary cabin.

The boy knew what the table was for. He understood why the pots hung beside the hearth and why the shutters had been closed against the night. He knew the purpose of everything he saw.

He could not remember where he had learned any of it.

The old man from the battlefield sat on a low stool beside the hearth. A dented metal mug rested between his hands, and his shoulders sagged as he stared into the fire.

The blankets rustled, and the man looked up. When the boy tried to push himself upright, he raised one broad hand.

"Easy now. You've been senseless since morning. If you mean to sit up, do it slowly. I'm too old to catch you twice in one day."

The boy stopped halfway and waited for the room to settle.

"…Where?"

"Didn't catch that. Try using more than one syllable. You've got a whole mouth back there."

"Where am I?"

"My farm. In Mydea." His eyes moved towards the shuttered window before returning to the boy. "Gerolt, by the way. Seeing as it's my roof you've woken up under, seemed only polite to introduce myself before you started asking it questions."

"…Mydea?"

"Aye, Mydea. This kingdom we're in." Gerolt gestured vaguely with his mug. "My house, my farm, and what used to be my wheat field are all Mydean land. So no, I haven't moved you that far. Carrying you as far as this bed nearly finished me, and I'll be feeling it in my back for a week."

A small laugh escaped him, though it didn't reach far.

*Mydea.*

It was the name of an entire kingdom, and the boy had never heard it before. His fingers closed around the blanket as he waited for the name to stir something inside him. Nothing did.

"The name should mean something, shouldn't it?" His breath shortened. "But it doesn't. I've never heard it before."

Gerolt's laughter stopped.

"Never heard of it. Lad, you're currently lying in it." He leaned forward, the mug forgotten between his hands. "You must remember a road, a town, whose colours you marched under. Something. People don't usually turn up on my land from nowhere in particular."

"I remember opening my eyes in your field." The boy pressed a palm against his forehead. "That is the first thing I have."

Gerolt stared at him.

"By the Four." He rubbed a hand over his jaw. "Well, shit. That's inconvenient."

His gaze moved over the boy again and settled on his clothes.

"All right. Let's start with what we can actually see, since apparently that's all either of us has to work with." He pointed towards the boy's chest. "You're not dressed for soldiering. No armour, no weapon—and what in the Last Dark are you wearing?"

The boy looked down at himself.

"Are they strange?"

"Strange is generous." Gerolt reached towards one cuff, then stopped before touching it. "No iron. No leather. Cloth soft enough for a lord's bed, and I found you in that field without a tear in it. Thirty years of traders on this road and I've never seen stitching like that. Not from any hand I know."

The boy rubbed the dark material between his fingers.

"Who made it?" Gerolt asked.

The boy looked down at the fabric. "I wish I could tell you. I keep looking for something in them—a mark, a colour, anything that might explain who made them or why I was wearing them. There's nothing."

"Yet they fit you."

"That only makes it worse."

The answer left him more sharply than he intended. Gerolt drew his hand back and studied him for a moment.

"Fair enough," he said, and for once, nothing followed it.

The boy ran his thumb over the seam and waited for a memory to come. When nothing did, his nail slipped beneath one of the stitches.

"Leave the cloth alone before you unpick the poor thing entirely," Gerolt said. "We'll start simpler. What do people call you?"

The boy opened his mouth.

The answer should have come without thought. Instead, he sat there with his lips parted while Gerolt waited.

Nothing came.

"Well?" Gerolt prompted.

"I…" The boy's face tightened. "I can't remember."

"Your own name."

"I know I should." His voice cracked as he looked down at his hands. "Why can't I remember it?"

Whatever suspicion Gerolt had been holding left his face.

"All right," he said, quieter now. "What about home? A village, a city, a river. Anything you recognise."

"I know what those things are. I can picture them when you say the words." The boy gripped the blanket harder. "None of them feel familiar."

"Nothing before this morning?"

"There has to be something." The boy heard his voice rising and could not stop it. "I can't have *nothing*."

"Easy. You're shaking badly enough without tearing your clothes to pieces on top of it."

The boy barely heard him. His hands returned to his collar, then his cuffs, then every strip of stitching he could reach.

*Someone made these. Someone will recognise them.*

There was nothing hidden in the lining. He checked every seam twice anyway, then stopped himself before beginning a third search.

"Wait." Gerolt caught his wrist as his nail found another stitch. "Leave the coat alone. There's one more thing worth trying before you pull it apart. What's your 『Affinity』?"

The boy's hand went still.

"My what?"

"Your element. Fire, water, earth, wind. Whichever one decided it liked you best." Gerolt released his wrist and tapped his own chest. "Everybody's born with one, same as they're born with a face."

The boy looked towards the hearth. Fire meant heat, light and the pain of touching it. None of that told him why it should know him in return.

"So it isn't something I learned," he said slowly. "Not like knowing what a spoon is for without remembering who put one in my hand. It's more like my height or the colour of my eyes. Something that's there whether I remember it or not."

"Aye. Close enough."

"Then how could I lose it?" His voice caught somewhere in the question, and he had to push through it. "How could there be nothing?"

"There isn't supposed to be." Gerolt's mug sat forgotten between his hands. "That's my whole point."

"Then try telling me how to find it, instead of just telling me it's there."

"Close your eyes. Don't chase after it—that only scares it off. Sit still and feel for whichever one already belongs to you."

"That explains nothing."

"I know. Nobody's ever had to explain it to me before. It's just—there. The way a man doesn't need someone to point out he's got hands."

The boy closed his eyes anyway. Gerolt had carried him home, cleaned him and given him the bed. The least he could do was try.

The fire ticked and settled somewhere to his right. The blanket was rough beneath his thumb. His pulse beat once, twice, past ten, behind the ache in his temple, and the wind pushed at the shutters like it wanted in.

That was all of it. Fire beside him. Wind beyond the shutters. Nothing inside him answered either.

He opened his eyes. Gerolt was already watching him.

"Well?"

"There's a fire. I can feel it because I'm sitting three feet from it." The boy heard his own voice go flat, reciting the room back like directions to a stranger. "There's wind, because your shutters don't sit right in the frame. Is that what you meant, or am I only telling you about your own house?"

"You're only telling me about my house."

"Then there's nothing."

Gerolt's fingers tightened around the mug. One leg of the stool scraped across the floor as his weight shifted back, scarcely an inch, but the sound cut through the room. His gaze dropped to the boy's empty hands and then flicked towards the door.

For one breath, he looked at the boy the way he had looked at the violet sparks.

Then Gerolt set the mug on the floor, planted both boots and leaned towards him again.

"Give it another moment. Sometimes it's shy the first—"

"You said I would know it." The words came faster now, and once they started he found he couldn't slow them back down. "You said I'd feel it before I could even put words to it. I don't feel fire. I don't feel water. I don't feel earth or wind or some fifth thing you haven't mentioned because you're hoping I won't need it. I feel a fire that isn't mine, and wind that isn't mine, and a headache that's at least probably my own, and that is the whole list, Gerolt. That is every single thing I have."

Gerolt said nothing.

"So either I'm doing this wrong," the boy said, quieter now that the anger had burned through as fast as it had come, "or there's actually nothing there. Tell me which. Because ever since I woke, I've been finding out I'm missing things one piece at a time, and I would rather hear it plainly than keep discovering it the way I discovered I don't have a name."

Gerolt was quiet long enough that the boy almost took it back.

"I don't know which," he said finally. "That's the truth of it. I've seen men forget their wives. I've seen men forget the war. I've never seen one come up this empty." He rubbed both palms against his knees. "I keep hoping if I say the right thing, something in you will just—answer. Like calling a dog that's wandered off. Maybe it didn't hear you the first time."

"And if it doesn't come the second time either?"

"Then you call again. Louder." Something almost like a smile crossed his face, though there wasn't much behind it. "I'm a farmer, not a scholar. Calling louder is the whole of my strategy."

The boy didn't answer that. He was too tired to find it funny, and too frightened to let it comfort him, and he sat there with both feelings pressing on him at once until neither one won.

Gerolt rose and crossed to the window instead of pressing further, opening the shutter a finger's width to stare into the night, one hand braced against the frame.

"I keep going back to the banners," he said, without turning around. "Not one of them meant a thing to me, and I could name every flag that's ever marched through Mydea in my sleep. Same with the armour. Same with those silver devils lying in among the rest." He shook his head slowly. "A man spends enough years at war, he thinks he's seen every way there is for people to kill each other. Apparently not."

"You were a soldier."

"Long enough." His jaw worked once. "And in all those years, I never saw men fight beside beastfolk."

The boy saw the horned warrior again, his shoulder against the human soldier's and both weapons aimed the same way. "But they were."

"Aye. By the thousands." Gerolt did not turn around. "That's what won't sit right in me."

He kept one eye to the opening in the shutter.

"So either I've been wrong about how the world works for sixty years, or something out there made it true just long enough to kill every one of them."

Something beneath the bed gave a thin whine.

The boy looked down.

"Wena?"

"That's Wena." Gerolt nodded towards the darkness beneath the frame. "Barks at owls, carts, the wind changing its mind about which direction to blow. Give her half an excuse and she'll use it. Last night, not a sound out of her."

Two frightened eyes caught the firelight beneath the bed.

"Found her pressed against my door this morning like she was trying to become part of it. Now she won't come out. Not even for me, and she'd usually trade her own tail for a scratch behind the ears."

The eyes vanished again as Gerolt closed the shutter and turned towards the boy.

"I'm not blaming you. Understand that." Gerolt kept one hand on the shutter as he faced him. "But something happened on my land, and you're the only living soul I found in it. Is there anything you remember that I don't? Anyone you heard speak, before I found you?"

"Only what was there when I woke. Crows. Bodies. The machines. Those violet sparks." The boy lowered his eyes. "You saw all of it before I did."

"Anything before you opened your eyes?"

"The first thing I remember is waking in that field."

Gerolt's shoulders lowered.

"I wish I could tell you more," the boy said. "You carried me here. You cleaned me up. I can see that you're afraid, and I can't explain a single piece of it."

"Aye. I know." Gerolt scrubbed a hand over his face. "Wanting an answer badly enough has never once made one show up."

The boy's stomach growled into the silence. He froze, then pulled the blanket higher when Gerolt looked at him.

"I'm sorry."

"For what? Having a stomach? That's the first normal thing about you all night."

An unsteady breath escaped Gerolt, almost becoming a laugh.

He crossed to the hearth, took a wooden bowl from the counter, and filled it from the pot. The boy pushed the blanket down from his lap as Gerolt returned.

"It's hot. Take it with both hands, there. And if you spill it, you're the one explaining the stain to my floor."

Gerolt kept hold of the bowl until the boy's grip was secure.

The first spoonful stung his cracked lips. He swallowed it anyway. Root vegetables, salted meat and thick broth—he recognised every taste.

"Thank you."

"Eat first. Thank me after."

The boy lowered the next spoonful and blew across it before eating.

Gerolt retrieved his mug and returned to his stool. He did not ask another question for a while. He sat with both hands around the metal and watched the fire instead.

A dark nose appeared beneath the bed and sniffed towards the bowl.

"Oh, now you're brave," Gerolt muttered. Wena's nose disappeared.

The boy ate another spoonful. By the fourth, the shaking in his hands had begun to ease.

Then the fire went white.

Broken earth filled his sight, cut through with red mud. Before he could find a horizon, the cabin returned: the bowl's weight in his lap, the spoon between his fingers, the fire warming one side of his face.

White took it again, but the field did not return whole. Gold flashed at a wrist. A mouth moved without a face around it. Red mud struck his shins from a bootfall he neither saw nor heard.

Then the pieces joined, and someone was already running towards him.

Mud blackened their sleeve to the elbow, but a narrow line of gold still showed at the cuff. The reaching hand opened, closed on empty air and reached again.

His left hand opened in answer.

Whenever he tried to look beyond the hand, white cut across the figure's face and erased it. They were close enough to reach him in three strides.

They took all three. The distance did not change.

The spoon tilted in his other hand, and broth spread across his fingers. The heat never reached him. Cold moved through his open left hand instead, from his fingertips into his wrist.

The figure's mouth formed a word he could not hear.

The spoon struck the rim of the bowl.

"Something wrong with it?"

Gerolt's voice reached him through the white.

The boy tried to answer. His throat closed before a sound came.

The image broke apart again: gold thread, an open hand, a mouth shaping the same word. This time, he almost heard it.

"Boy?"

The stool scraped against the floorboards.

White light crossed the space between them. The mouth disappeared first. The hand remained for one moment longer, fingers spread towards his, and then that vanished too.

The bowl slipped from his lap, struck the boards, and spun away. Hot stew splashed across his knees, and the burn reached him this time as he folded forward, both hands flying to his throat.

"Damn the bowl."

Gerolt caught him beneath the arms before his head struck the floor.

"Here, lad. Look at me if you can. I've got you."

The boy's nails dug beneath his jaw. He could feel the skin gathering under them, but he could not make his hands stop.

"Leave your throat alone or you'll have it bleeding." Gerolt closed one hand around both of the boy's wrists and pulled them down against his chest. His other arm stayed firm around the boy's shoulders. "You're in my house. The fire's beside you. Feel my arm around you and stay here with me."

Fear roughened Gerolt's voice. His grip did not shake.

The broken field vanished. The cabin returned—the fire, Gerolt's face, the overturned bowl—but his chest still would not draw breath.

Then a thread of air scraped into him. He coughed it out and dragged in another.

"There. That one came," Gerolt said. "Let the next be small as well. I've got you."

The boy obeyed because there was nothing else he could do. One shallow breath followed another until his chest finally loosened. Only then did Gerolt ease his grip.

"There was someone," he managed.

Gerolt bent closer. "In the field?"

"Running towards me."

"Who?"

"I couldn't see their face." The boy caught at Gerolt's sleeve. "They were close enough that they should have reached me, but they kept running and never came any nearer. Their mouth kept moving. I almost heard—"

His breath caught again.

"I should know who they were."

Gerolt gave him a moment before asking, "Was it someone from that battle?"

The boy's fingers went still around Gerolt's sleeve.

"How could I know?" His voice came out level and far too quiet. "I woke surrounded by bodies. Maybe they were trying to save me. Or maybe I was what they came to stop."

Gerolt drew breath to answer, then shut his mouth.

The boy looked down at the hand clutching his sleeve and tried to let go. His fingers did not move. Gerolt followed his gaze. Instead of pulling away, he covered the boy's hand with his own.

Gerolt shook his head. "No. Bad question. You saw a hand, and I'm asking you for a name. Leave it for tonight. The questions can wait until you can stand without falling over."

The boy nodded and flexed his left hand once. There was no warmth beneath his thumb now.

Gerolt stayed crouched beside him, one hand steady on his shoulder.

Then his fingers dug in.

His head turned towards the road, and every part of him went still.

At first, the boy heard nothing. Then a faint rhythm passed through the floorboards and into his knees.

*Hoofbeats.*

Far off, but coming fast.

Under the bed, Wena's whimpering cut off, as if a hand had closed around it.

Gerolt was at the window before the boy could ask what was wrong. He pressed one eye to the gap in the shutters, and whatever he saw out there wiped the exhaustion from his face and left only fear behind.

"Shit." It came out under his breath, barely a word at all.

He moved at once.

The iron poker scraped through the hearth. Ash fell over the coals, and their orange glow died, ember by ember. He crossed to the table and pinched out the candle between finger and thumb. The flame burned him. He did not pull away.

Darkness swallowed the cabin.

Outside, a torch flared to life. Orange light cut through the shutters and laid long bars across the floor. A shape passed behind the lowest gap—dark metal crossed by a curling line of gold—and vanished with the moving flame.

One bar of light swept straight across the boy.

He froze.

*They saw me.*

Then he found Gerolt's face in the dark, and understood.

He flattened himself against the floorboards, dragging the blanket with him. Gerolt crossed the room in two strides, eased the bench onto its side, and pulled the boy in behind it.

"Not a sound," Gerolt breathed. "Not one. Do you understand me?"

"What's—"

"Riding openly across Mydean fields…" His voice was barely there. "They shouldn't be here. Not ever."

His mouth started to shape a name. Whatever it was, he swallowed it.

The boy's lips parted.

The riders might have crossed the field. They might know what had happened there, or recognise his clothes, his face. Before he could stop it, hope rose in him.

One of them might even know *him*.

He drew breath to call out—

—and Gerolt's hand tightened on his shoulder.

The boy looked at him. At the man who had walked through thousands of corpses that morning without breaking. Who had carried a stranger home across his own ruined harvest, and put food in his hands, and asked for nothing.

Now that same man crouched in the dark beside a smothered fire, hiding from whoever stood outside rather than let them know he was home.

Calling out wouldn't be the boy's choice alone. It would make it for both of them.

He looked once towards the door.

Then he closed his mouth.

He wanted answers. He chose silence.

Outside, the hoofbeats slowed. Harness creaked. Metal chinked against metal.

Then they stopped.

The boy held his breath. His heart hammered so hard against his ribs he was certain it would give them away.

A voice murmured beyond the wall.

Not a language he knew. The syllables overlapped, as if several throats were shaping the same sounds without keeping time with one another.

Gerolt's fingers dug harder into his shoulder. His eyes never left the door.

The boy gave up on the voice and listened beneath it instead. A buckle shifting. A hoof scraping dirt. Torchlight sliding between the shutters, vanishing, returning from a new angle.

None of it made a picture. He didn't know how many were out there, where they stood, or what they wanted. So he watched Gerolt instead.

If the farmer understood that tongue, his face didn't show it. But he knew enough to be afraid.

For now, that was enough for the boy too.

A stirrup rang softly. A mount released a long breath as weight lifted off its back.

Someone dismounted.

The boy waited for the ground to answer—the thud of boots, the crunch of dirt.

It never came.

*Tock.*

*Tock.*

*Tock.*

Knuckles on the door.

Gentle. Patient. As if whoever stood outside was willing to wait forever.

Gerolt's eyes went wide. He looked down at the boy, and the boy looked back. His heart battered his ribs and every breath shook on the way out, but he did not turn towards the door.

*Don't move,* Gerolt mouthed. No sound came with it at all.

No hoof scraped the dirt. No harness shifted. No one spoke.

Then the fourth knock came, no louder than the first three, no less patient.

*Tock.*

The boy's breath stopped halfway in. A thought surfaced from the same empty place that had given him the word 『Magic』.

*Opening that door would be the last thing he ever did.*
