# Refuge

Alaric didn't knock.

The weathered door stood a few paces away. No light showed under it. The painted cup above the entrance swung on its chain whenever the wind found the yard, answering with a small wooden creak.

Behind him, Kelmend was waking. Cart wheels rattled over the street at the end of the lane. Near the southern wall, a bell kept ringing at measured intervals, and after every note came the distant tramp of armed men moving somewhere new.

He looked back at the edge of the yard.

The stranger was still standing there with his cloak pulled tight, as if the last few steps to that door were more dangerous than anything they'd crossed outside the walls.

"Well?" he said. "What are you waiting for?"

Alaric looked at the door again.

Gerolt had sent him here. That should have made lifting his hand easier. Instead, knocking meant arriving — arriving at the place Gerolt had sent him, where Gerolt would never follow.

Wena stood beside him with her head hanging. River water had pooled under her paws. A small wet cough went through her, and she swallowed it.

Alaric knocked.

Nothing.

He knocked again, harder.

Somewhere inside, a floorboard creaked.

"If you think this is noon, the ale's left you with piss for brains," a woman called through the door. "Find a wall to sleep against and come back when the sun's up properly."

The lock turned. The door opened only far enough for one sharp eye to appear in the gap.

It passed over Alaric.

The gap widened.

The woman behind it held herself with a quiet sort of authority that didn't need raising. Dark red hair hung in a loose braid over one shoulder. She wore a long black robe fastened neatly at the throat, and even this early, with sleep still soft under her eyes, there was something striking about her that the plain doorway couldn't do anything to diminish.

Her eyes went over his wet hair, the mud dried down one side of his face, the charcoal messenger's coat hanging heavy off his shoulders.

Suspicion got there before concern did.

"The inn's closed."

Wena lifted her head.

For one heartbeat she only stared.

Then she ran.

She didn't run well. Her back legs slid on the wet stone, and another cough broke out of her before she'd covered half the distance. She ran anyway.

The woman's face opened.

"—Wena?"

She dropped to one knee as the dog reached her. Wena shoved her head under the woman's arm and made a sound Alaric had never heard from her — too low to be a bark, too broken to be a whine.

Both of the woman's hands disappeared into the wet fur at her neck.

"Easy, girl. Easy. I've got you."

Wena leaned into her so hard the woman had to brace a hand on the doorframe.

When she looked up at Alaric again, the suspicion had gone. Alarm had taken its place. It had not turned into welcome.

"Where's Gerolt?"

Alaric opened his mouth — and her eyes moved past him, to the edge of the yard.

Something in her face went cold.

"Silas."

The name crossed the yard and settled there.

The stranger inclined his head, once.

"Marta."

The hand in Wena's fur tightened.

"I told you to stay out of Kelmend."

Silas didn't answer.

"Get out."

He still said nothing.

Her eyes came back to Alaric, and whatever Wena had won for him vanished under the weight of Silas's name.

"And if he brought you here, you can leave with him."

She stood, one hand still on Wena's neck, and began to close the door.

Alaric pulled the iron token out from under his coat.

"Gerolt sent me."

The door stopped.

Marta looked at the split shield stamped into the metal. She didn't reach for it straight away. Her eyes went from the token to his wrapped hands, and back to his face.

"Your name."

"Alaric."

She held out her hand. He laid the token in it. Her thumb passed once across the worn line dividing the shield.

"Where is my uncle?"

She'd left no room for a kinder answer.

"He —" Alaric swallowed. "He's dead."

Wena pressed harder into Marta's side.

"There were two riders at the farm." His voice had started to shake. He kept going. "Gerolt killed one. The other got away and came back with more. He made Wena and me run, and he —"

The burning cabin opened behind his eyes.

"He stayed behind. He died protecting me."

Marta's fingers closed around the token.

Her eyes shut.

"You old fool," she whispered.

The last word wavered, barely. Then her eyes opened, and every trace of it was gone.

She looked at Silas.

"Gerolt sent the boy. He didn't send you." Her voice rose until it cracked across the yard. "Get out of Kelmend. *Now.*"

Under her hand, Wena's ears went flat.

"Wena and I wouldn't have got past the wall without him," Alaric said.

Her eyes cut back to him. The force of it almost made him step back. He stayed where he was.

"Then you owe him," Marta said. "I don't."

Bootsteps came into the lane.

Silas turned his head first. Marta a breath after. Metal scraped stone somewhere beyond the stable wall — several men, moving fast.

Marta's grip tightened on the edge of the door.

"Alaric. Take Wena inside."

He crossed the threshold with Wena pressed against his leg.

Silas took one step forward.

Marta moved into the doorway. "Not you."

A soldier's shout came from the lane. "Search the next yard!"

The boots changed direction.

Marta didn't move. Silas stood in front of her and didn't try for another step. He waited, the way a man waits for a sentence.

Her jaw tightened.

"Damn you."

She moved aside barely far enough to let him pass.

"Inside. Now. And the moment they're gone, so are you."

"I know."

Silas went in without touching her. Marta shut the door hard behind him.

The dark inside took a moment to become a room. Cups upturned and drying beside a washbasin. Chairs stacked upside down on the tables. A soot-blackened hearth under a stone chimney. It smelled of yesterday's bread, cold ash, and something sweet left in an open bottle.

Marta pointed at a low door beside the hearth.

"Cellar."

Silas crossed the room without a word.

"If that latch moves before I open it," Marta said, "I'll put you back in the yard myself."

He paused with his hand on the door. "Understood."

He went down. Marta waited until the latch settled, then turned to Alaric.

"You and Wena. Pantry."

She opened the first door under the stairs. Flour sacks filled most of the cramped space, with bundles of dried herbs and strings of onions hanging above them.

"Touch nothing. Keep her close, and if she coughs, muffle it. Neither of you comes out till I open this door."

Alaric crouched and pulled Wena into him. Marta pushed the door nearly shut.

The knocking began before she'd crossed the room.

"Marta! Open up. Captain's orders."

"Your captain knows what time this inn opens."

The lock turned. Marta opened the door and planted herself squarely in the frame.

Cold air slid in under the pantry door. Through the narrow gap, Alaric could see four Mydean guards crowded outside. The oldest stood in front with a thumb hooked in his belt. The one behind him was barely more than a boy, and when Marta's eyes landed on him, he straightened so fast his shoulder plates clacked together.

"Morning, Marta," the young one said.

The older guard gave him a look. The boy went red to the ears.

Marta folded her arms. "What does your captain want?"

"We need to search the inn." It came out with a good deal less force now that Marta was standing in front of him. "Boy came through the southern wall. Young, dark hair, strange black clothes. Big farm dog with him."

In the pantry, Wena started to lift her head. Alaric closed a hand gently round her muzzle and drew her tighter against his chest. She trembled, but she let him.

"And why are four armed men hunting a wet boy and his dog?" Marta asked.

The sergeant glanced back up the lane and dropped his voice. "He came from Gerolt's farm. Captain doesn't want that repeated."

Marta's eyes went to the neighbouring shutters. "Then lower your voice."

"I shouted your name, not his business." His mouth tightened. "Natharul wants him found by the next bell."

Her mouth flattened at the name. "And your captain's decided to obey them."

"We're trying to stop a bigger problem."

"You already let him through your wall."

The young guard choked back a laugh. His sergeant silenced it with a glance.

"Have you seen them?"

"No. You woke me."

"Then we need to look inside."

"No."

Marta didn't move from the middle of the doorway.

"Every property near the southern gate is being searched."

"Every market night, I throw drunks twice his size out of this room." She nodded at the youngest guard. "A frightened boy and a farm dog won't get past my door. If they try, I'll hold them here and send for your captain."

The young guard looked like he couldn't decide whether he'd been insulted or complimented. His eyes went back to Marta's face and then dropped very quickly to his boots.

"We still have our orders," the sergeant said, though there was less certainty in them now.

"Then use your men where they'll do some good. A boy who's just climbed through a wall will pick an empty building over an occupied inn." She pointed west. "Search the old grain sheds. Half of them have broken doors, and none of them have owners awake enough to complain."

"Why west?"

"The barracks are east, the market's already waking to the north, and you've filled every southern road with armour. Where would *you* hide?"

The young guard cleared his throat. "She's right, Sergeant."

"I didn't ask you, boy."

The colour climbed his face again.

The sergeant looked back at Marta. She hadn't moved an inch.

"If they turn up here —"

"You'll find them waiting for you."

He held her eye a moment longer, then stepped back.

"Grain sheds first," he ordered. "Then the western wall."

The young guard hung back until Marta raised one eyebrow at him.

"*Move*," the sergeant snapped. The boy hurried after the others.

"We may come back," the sergeant warned.

"After noon." Marta began closing the door. "Perhaps by then you'll have remembered how one of these works."

The soldiers left.

She turned the lock and left Alaric in the dark while the patrol crossed the stable yard, reached the lane, and thinned away towards the western wall. Only when the last of them had faded did she open the pantry.

Light fell across Wena's wet coat.

"Bring her to the hearth," Marta said. "I won't have either of you freezing to death among my onions."

---

She laid two pieces of wood across the last of the coals and held out her fingers. Air gathered into a thin current, so controlled it didn't disturb the ash around it. The coals brightened. Flame ran along the bark and closed round the wood.

Wind『Affinity』.

Alaric knew it before he decided to.

Marta hung a pot over the fire. She didn't ask whether he'd noticed.

Then she crossed to the cellar and lifted the latch.

Silas was standing on the first step down, waiting. He hadn't touched the door. Water still dripped from the hem of his cloak and puddled round his boots.

Her lip curled.

"Far end of the table. Cloak on the chair. Hands where I can see them."

He came up with his eyes lowered, went to the chair she'd chosen, took off his cloak and sat without a word. He laid both hands flat on the table.

Only then did she turn her back on him.

Wena lay by the hearth. Marta spread three rough towels under her, knelt, and worked another through the soaked fur of her head and chest. Wena leaned against her knees with her whole weight. Another cough shook her, and Marta waited it out before setting a shallow bowl of water where she could reach it.

"There you are," she murmured. "There's my girl. Drink."

Alaric sat nearer the fire with a blanket round his shoulders. Marta put bread and a bowl of broth in front of him.

Hunger had the first spoonful in his mouth before he'd even tasted it. His hand was already going back to the bowl when she spoke.

"Slowly."

He stopped. "I'm trying."

"You'll make yourself sick, eating like that."

He lowered the spoon and did as he was told.

Marta cut cooked meat into a second bowl and carried it to Wena. The dog sniffed it without lifting her head, so Marta held a piece against her mouth.

"Come on, girl. You know me. Come on."

Wena took it. Marta waited until she'd taken another before coming back to the table.

"She knows you," Alaric said.

"Gerolt brought produce for the inn. Wena came along often enough to decide the yard belonged to her." A corner of Marta's mouth moved, and stilled. "She used to chase my chickens. He'd swear she was only herding them."

She took Gerolt's token from a pocket in her robe and set it between them, shield side up.

"Do you know what he gave you?"

"No. He only told me to bring it to Marta."

"You didn't know who Marta was?"

"No."

"And yet you came."

"It was the last thing he asked me to do."

She watched him a moment longer, then looked down at the split shield.

"This belongs to the Broken Shield."

"What's that?"

"A promise." She sat across from him. "It tells me Gerolt trusted you enough to send you here. That earns you food, warmth and one night under my roof while I hear what happened."

She tapped the broken line with one fingernail.

"It doesn't buy trust. And it doesn't make this inn yours."

"I understand," Alaric whispered.

He understood only that the token had bought him one night. Everything past that was still out of reach.

Marta's eyes moved to Silas.

"It buys you nothing."

Silas kept both hands flat on the wood. "I know."

She turned back to Alaric. "You said he got you through the wall."

"One of the riders found us by the river. Silas killed him before he found where Wena and I were hiding. Then he brought us here."

Marta never took her eyes off Silas.

"What were you hoping to take from him?"

"Nothing from him," Silas said.

"Then why bring him at all?"

Silas glanced once at Wena. His fingers pressed flatter on the table.

"The riders had reached the river. If I'd left them there, Natharul would've had both of them before morning."

"That explains the river." Her voice didn't soften at all. "It doesn't explain why you used them as an excuse to ignore me and come here."

He had no answer for that.

She turned the token once on the table and pushed it back towards Alaric.

"Start with where Gerolt found you."

Alaric closed his hand around the iron.

"On his farm."

Marta waited.

"I woke up under a dead man." His voice thinned. His throat was already closing round the words. The bowl was warm in his hands, but mud pressed cold against his cheek again, and a dead hand lay inches from his face with its fingers still curled round a broken spear. "I was covered in blood."

"Was it yours?"

He shook his head. "Gerolt searched me. He couldn't find a wound."

"What do you remember before you woke?"

"Nothing."

Her eyes sharpened. "Nothing at all?"

"I remember how to talk. I know what things are when I see them." He stared into the broth. "I just don't remember learning any of it. I don't remember where I lived. I don't remember anyone."

It was harder to say in a warm room. Nothing here let him pretend his lost life would come back once the danger passed.

For the first time since she'd sat down, Marta forgot to look at Silas.

"How many bodies?"

"I couldn't count them. They covered the farm. Thousands, Gerolt thought."

"Mydean?"

"The banners were wrong. He didn't know any of them."

He told her about the beastfolk, because leaving them out felt too much like lying. Horns snapped beneath armour. Clawed hands locked round weapons. Bodies too big to belong to any man.

Marta's face tightened. "They can't come into Mydea."

"They were there."

He told her about the silver machines next, though describing them felt impossible. Metal with no rivets or seams. Great hollow bodies built to carry people into war, though he couldn't remember ever seeing one move.

"How did you know what they were for?"

"I don't know."

"You must know something."

"The same way I knew what a sword was." His fingers tightened round the spoon. "The knowing is there. Nothing around it is."

Marta sat back, but she didn't interrupt again.

He tried to tell the rest in order. The untouched wheat all round the edges. No road, no camp, nothing to explain how an army got there. Gerolt going to bed with a crop outside his window and waking up to thousands of dead.

"He heard nothing in the night. No horses. No fighting. It was just there in the morning."

The fire cracked softly beside them.

"There was a circle," he said.

Marta leaned forward. "What kind?"

"Perfectly round. The grass inside was still green, with frost over it. Everything outside was burned or broken, and nothing inside had been touched." He could see it, waiting among the dead. "All the grass pointed in towards the middle. And there was nothing there."

Marta looked at Silas. "You saw this?"

"I saw enough bodies to keep my distance. I didn't see a circle."

"Gerolt thought the riders had," Alaric said. "He said that might be why they came to the cabin."

Marta's attention came back to him. "And the one who escaped saw you alive inside his cabin."

He nodded. "She heard me shout Gerolt's name. She knew he wasn't alone. By the time we got to Kelmend, the guards were already looking for me."

Marta went quiet.

"Do you know what happened there?" Alaric asked.

"No."

She said it too fast for there to be any comfort in it.

"But I know someone who might recognise where the questions lead."

She stood before he could ask who.

"Coat and shirt."

He stared at her.

"You can be embarrassed once I know whether that river broke anything."

Silas stood up.

Marta turned on him immediately. "Stable store. Second door across the yard."

"The patrol might circle back."

"Then keep your head below the windows and do as you're told. You're already blackening my floorboards with whatever the river left on you."

He collected his cloak and left without a word to either of them.

Alaric set the token down by his bowl and peeled off the messenger's coat. It clung where the filth had dried under the collar, then let go all at once. Marta hung it by the hearth.

The black clothes underneath were whole.

Mud stained them. Gerolt's blood had dried almost brown down one side. The river and the tannery had pressed their smell into every fold. Not one seam had opened.

Marta caught the edge of a sleeve and turned it to the firelight.

"Where did this come from?"

"I woke up in it."

"Of course you did," she muttered, with a flicker of real irritation, as if the clothes had personally offended her.

She let it go. He pulled the shirt over his head, and pain caught under his ribs and shoulder and held one arm halfway up until she guided the cloth the rest of the way off.

Bruises crossed his side in dark bands. A shallow tear ran along his ribs where the stone had caught him under the water. His right shoulder had started to swell. Both knees were marked — one by the riverbed, one by the settling chamber floor.

She pressed carefully along his ribs. He jerked away when her fingers found the darkest place.

"Breathe."

He did. The breath hurt at the end but didn't grind the way he'd been afraid it would.

"Again."

He did it again.

"Nothing moves where it shouldn't," she said. "That's not the same as nothing being hurt. If your breathing gets worse, you wake me."

She cleaned the cut and bound it, then checked his shoulder, his ankle and his knees. The ankle had swollen round the place it had turned in the drainage ditch, but it held his weight when she made him stand. She wrapped it without once pretending the cloth would make walking pleasant.

His wet shoes came off last. The skin had rubbed raw along both heels.

Marta looked at them. Then at him.

"You walked from the river on these?"

He glanced towards the stable yard. "He said he'd leave me if I stopped."

"And he would have."

She said it with a certainty that came from somewhere older. She didn't explain it.

She washed the blood and tannery filth from his palms. The loosened wrappings had dried into the torn skin, and taking them off hurt more than tearing them had. He kept his hands flat on the table while she picked grit out of each cut. Her touch was firm and exact. She talked to him while she did it — not about anything that mattered, only about the chickens, and how Gerolt had once tried to trade her a goat for a winter's worth of ale and been genuinely offended when she refused — and it wasn't until she'd finished the left hand that he realised she'd been doing it so he'd stop watching.

She bound the left palm. The last strip of clean cloth lay by the bowl.

Then she stopped.

Her eyes lifted from his hands, but not to his face. She seemed to be looking at the air around him.

"What's your『Affinity』?"

"I don't have one."

"How weak?"

"Not weak. None."

Her eyes narrowed. "Those aren't the same thing."

"I know." He looked at Gerolt's token. "He made me reach for it. Nothing was there."

Marta stepped back from the table. Distance didn't change whatever she was searching for.

"What did he say?"

"He called me Empty."

She stood still for several breaths. Then she turned towards the passage to the yard.

"Silas."

The stable store door opened a little while later. Silas appeared in the inner doorway and stopped there without crossing it.

"On the road," Marta said, "did you feel his mana?"

Silas looked at Alaric. "I thought he was spent."

"That wasn't the question."

A long pause.

"No," Silas said at last. "I felt nothing."

"And you said nothing."

"He'd crossed a flooded river and could barely stand. I had better reasons to explain it than impossible ones."

Marta looked back at Alaric, and the caution in her face had become something other than plain suspicion.

"My Wind is ranked Eminent," she said. "Strong enough that I can feel the mana other people give off. I've spent most of my life around people who lie about how strong they are. Some have so little the law calls them Faint. Exhausted mages swear they've nothing left." She shook her head once. "They're always wrong. Weak or spent, something's there. A pressure. A direction. Enough to know what answers when they reach inside."

Alaric's throat tightened. "And me?"

She held his eyes.

"My uncle was right," she said quietly. "You really are Empty."

It hit harder than it had in Gerolt's cabin.

Gerolt had said it like a question. Marta said it like a verdict.

Empty.

He looked down at his hurt hands. The name he'd fought so hard to remember suddenly felt far too small to fill the hole she'd found inside him.

"Is that what I am?"

"I don't know what you are."

She came back to the table and picked up the last strip of cloth.

"There's never been any need for a word for someone like you. Everyone has an『Affinity』. Even the Faint have something."

She wound it round his right palm. The first pull was too tight. He flinched, and she loosened it without apologising.

"The law knows exactly what to do with the Faint," she went on. "It calls them dependants, or servants, or property, and puts them under someone stronger. You don't even fit into that cruelty."

"What would they do to me?"

She tied off the bandage and sat back.

"Men don't leave impossible things alone. They'd lock you up first and decide what to call you afterwards. Priests would examine you. The court would want to know if you were dangerous. And if Natharul asked for you, Mydea might hand you over and call it peace."

He looked towards Silas. "Would any of them help me understand?"

"No." Her face hardened. "They'd hate you. The Faint are treated as property for having too little. You have nothing at all. They'd put you beneath even them — and call every cruelty that followed your rightful place."

The fire was warm. Alaric couldn't feel it anymore.

"No one can know," she said. "Not a guard. Not a priest. Not anyone who'd sell your secret for coin or favour."

"Silas knows."

Her eyes went to the doorway.

"For his own sake, he'll remember how to keep quiet."

Silas didn't react to the threat. "I'd already planned to."

"Back to the store."

He went. The door closed behind him, and there was only Alaric, Marta, and the sound of Wena breathing by the hearth.

"Are you sending me away?" Alaric asked.

Marta looked at the split shield.

"Gerolt bought you a night, and I said I'd honour it. Tonight, you stay."

She carried the empty meat bowl over to Wena and crouched beside her, laying one palm against the dog's ribs, feeling the uneven rise and fall under the drying coat.

"She needs warmth and sleep. If that cough gets worse, you wake me."

Wena pushed her nose against Marta's wrist.

And for a moment, grief came back into Marta's face. She bent her head and hid it in the dog's fur.

---

The room she gave him was under the slope of the roof: a narrow bed, a washstand, one chair with a crack through the seat. No one had slept there in a while. Dust lay on the window latch, and the blanket smelled faintly of dried lavender.

Wena circled the bed once and lay down beside it.

Alaric sat on the mattress. Pain found him differently now that he'd stopped moving. His ankle throbbed under its wrapping. Both heels burned. The bruises round his ribs pulled tight every time he bent towards her.

He put Gerolt's token on the washstand.

Then he lowered himself onto the floor beside Wena instead of lying on the bed. Her coat had started to warm. When he laid his hand between her eyes, she opened them and looked at the door, as if she expected someone else to come through it.

No one did.

He stayed beside her until her breathing slowed. He meant to get up afterwards.

The dark took him first.

---

When he woke, night had filled the room.

Laughter was coming up through the floorboards. Tankards banging on tables, chairs scraping, several voices fighting their way through the same song in several different keys. For one lurching moment the noise turned into Gerolt's cabin breaking apart around him, and he sat up far too fast.

Pain clamped round his ribs and shoulder. His breath stopped halfway in. The room only steadied once he'd leaned back against the bed and waited for the dark at the edges of his sight to go.

Wena lifted her head. Her coat had dried in stiff ridges. She coughed once, swallowed, and stood up when he did.

"Stay."

She followed him to the door.

The dark coat Silas had given him, a clean linen shirt and a pair of plain trousers were waiting on the chair. He didn't remember anyone bringing them up. He'd managed the shirt and trousers when someone knocked.

Marta came in with a cup of water. She'd changed into a fitted dark-green dress with long sleeves and pinned her hair up off her face. The noise followed her in until she shut the door on it.

"Keep Wena away from the passage," she said. "Half of Kelmend seems to have decided thirst is an emergency tonight."

She set the cup down next to Gerolt's token.

"Silas stays in the stable store till I close up."

"You let him stay."

"I kept him from being seen outside my inn. Don't mistake one for the other."

She looked at him properly.

"What are you going to do tomorrow?"

His eyes went to the iron shield. "I don't know."

"Then decide before morning."

"How long can I stay?"

"Until first light, whether you do anything for me or not. That's what Gerolt's token buys."

"And after?"

"After that, I can't keep risking this inn for someone who's got no intention of leaving it."

She turned for the door.

"You said you knew someone who might know where to start," he said.

Her hand stopped on the latch.

"I do."

"Can you bring them here?"

Her fingers tightened on the latch. "No. Her name's Darcy. The garrison keeps her under the fort, and at dawn they mean to move her out of Kelmend."

A roar of shouting came up from the room below, followed by the crash of something wooden hitting the floor. Marta shut her eyes for one brief moment, like a woman counting the cost of a chair.

"Drink that. Stay here till I come for you. And don't let Wena announce you to every drunk under this roof."

The door closed before he could ask what she meant.

---

She didn't come back until the inn had gone quiet.

By then the lamps in the passage had burned low. Alaric took the token off the washstand and followed her downstairs with Wena at his heel.

The inn bore the wreckage of a busy night — rings of foam on the tables, chairs knocked crooked, the air thick with smoke, spilled ale and damp wool.

Silas sat at the far end of the longest table. Three sheets of paper lay in front of him beside a rough map of Kelmend, each corner weighted down with a cup.

He watched Alaric come down the stairs one careful step at a time.

"You look worse for wear." One corner of his mouth tugged up.

"He crossed a flooded river and walked here on torn feet," Marta said. "Keep your amusement to yourself."

Wena went to her first. Marta rested a hand on the dog's neck, then pointed Alaric at a chair where a bowl of broth was waiting.

"Eat."

He sat. "How long has the garrison had Darcy?"

Marta stayed standing across from him.

"Since she was ten."

"Why?"

Her fingers pressed against the table.

"We grew up together. When Mydea tested our『Affinities』, the examiner put a marked silver cup under Darcy's hands. She held them over it until her arms shook. Three drops fell. They didn't reach the lowest line."

Alaric looked at her hand on the wood. "What happened to her?"

"The examiner emptied the cup and made her try again. Then a third time, with every child behind her in the queue watching."

"That made her Faint."

Marta pulled out the chair opposite him, but didn't sit.

"It gave the law the word it needed." Her fingers stayed wrapped round the chair back. "By the end of that week, men she'd never met were deciding where she'd sleep, what work she'd do, and whether she was allowed to see her own family. The law calls it protective stewardship." Her mouth tightened round the words. "The garrison calls her property. Neither of them thinks there's a difference."

Silas kept his eyes on the map. The stillness in him wasn't indifference.

"If she's so weak," Alaric asked, "why does the garrison want her?"

"Because her mind has won battles her owners couldn't begin to understand."

She pushed one of the papers across the table. Lines crossed it in tight columns — roads and formations he didn't recognise.

"She sees how armies move before they've started moving. She can look at stores and weather and distance and tell a commander exactly where his men will starve. She's ended sieges without breaching a wall. She's won battles without ever letting the enemy choose where to fight them."

"Then why is she still treated like property?"

"They value what she gives them," Marta said. "They don't value her."

It came out fast. Too fast.

She drew the paper back and smoothed a corner that was already flat.

"Officers put their seals under her plans. When the plans work, they get promoted. When she corrects them in front of other men, or refuses to clean up the mess their stupidity made, the captain reminds her that being clever never lifted her above her『Affinity』."

Alaric put his spoon down. "How?"

Her fingers tightened on the table.

"They keep her awake until she can't remember what day it is. They hold back food until her hand shakes too hard to hold a quill. If she still refuses, they beat her where her clothes will hide it." Her voice dropped. "Never the hands. They need those to write the victories they mean to steal."

Alaric forgot the broth entirely.

"Last time they let me visit, she'd been awake four days. They only let me in because she'd stopped answering them." Marta's eyes fell to the map. "She was sitting on the floor next to the bed. She couldn't lift the cup they'd left in reach. The captain asked me to talk her into finishing a supply route before morning. He stood right over her while he said it. Talked as if the bruises under her clothes had turned up because she'd been difficult."

The room was completely silent.

"I held the water for her," Marta said. "She apologised for spilling it."

She took her hand off the map, crossed to the nearest table and gathered up two abandoned cups. Their rims knocked together in her hands. She stilled them. When she came back, she didn't look at either man.

Alaric stared at the roads and formations spread across the table. The world had called her weak, and then taken everything her weakness couldn't defend.

"What happens tomorrow?"

Marta finally sat.

"She leaves Kelmend at dawn under military guard. Natharul's moving against Kozmagar's beastfolk, and Mydea's sending troops to the frontier. The court thinks helping the elves is safer than refusing them."

"They're giving Darcy to Natharul?"

"They're sending her to Mydean officers serving in Natharul's campaign. Whatever flag flies over their heads, they'll use her mind and call it loyalty." She pulled the map closer. "The transfer order came this afternoon. Once she reaches the frontier, she'll be surrounded by soldiers who don't know me, on roads I can't reach, under commanders who know exactly what she's worth."

"And you want to stop the transfer."

"I want to give her a choice before it happens."

Silas tapped one finger on the edge of the map. "The order gives us the hour."

"Then find a way to reach her before dawn." Marta's eyes cut to him. "I want Alaric close enough to speak to Darcy. You'll get him there."

Silas looked across the table at Alaric. "He can barely walk."

"Then for once in your life, match your pace to somebody other than yourself." She turned back to Alaric before Silas could answer.

"Why me?" Alaric asked.

"The people I trust are known to the garrison. My movements get noticed, and Silas has given enough guards reason to remember his face. Nobody there knows yours."

"They know what I look like."

"They've got a description. Dark hair, black clothes, farm dog. Wena won't be with you, and we can cover the clothes." Her eyes went over his bandaged hands and swollen ankle. "That doesn't make you ready. You carry my offer. You don't fight your way to her. If Darcy says yes, Silas gets you both over the wall."

Silas looked at Marta. "You've already decided I'm doing this."

"You wanted to stay in Kelmend. Make yourself useful." She held his eye until he looked down at the map.

"Where do you want them taken?"

She put one finger past Kelmend's outer wall. "The broken well, on the outer edge of the Faint quarter. I'll be waiting there with Wena and provisions."

Alaric looked at the dog. "How will you get her through the wall?"

"In my provision cart. The eastern workers' postern lets supply carts through before dawn, and the watch knows my seal better than they know half their own guards."

Wena rested her head against his knee.

"Has Darcy asked you to do this?"

Marta's hand stopped on the map. "She's asked what freedom feels like. What's out past the roads she's not allowed to use. I think she wants to leave. But I haven't been able to ask her outright without putting her under suspicion."

"Then she might say no."

"She might." Marta looked at Silas. "If she does, you bring Alaric back without her."

Silas's jaw tightened. "We might not get another chance."

"Then don't waste this one arguing with her. You will not drag her away from one lot of armed men just because you think where you're taking her is kinder."

He held her eye for a long moment. Then he gave a short nod. "Understood."

Alaric looked at the map. He could still see the silver cup under Darcy's hands. Three drops, and strangers had started deciding the rest of her life.

That was what weakness bought you here. And he had nothing at all.

The thought frightened him. It also shamed him. Darcy was under that fort right now. She wasn't a warning about what might happen to him.

"You haven't answered," Marta said, more quietly.

He rubbed his thumb along Wena's ear. "I know."

"What I said upstairs still stands. You and Wena have until first light, whether you say yes or not. I'll show you a way out of Kelmend."

Silas was watching him across the table. The last of the humour had gone out of his face.

"Think before you answer. The men in that fort will kill you the moment you get inconvenient."

Marta's eyes moved to him. "Warn him without making your fear sound like a certainty."

"It's kept me alive."

Her voice dropped. "It kept *you* alive."

Whatever was left behind Silas's expression took that like a blow. He looked away.

Wena's fur had gone warm under Alaric's hand. He remembered it cold and slick in the river, her body going under because she'd followed him in. Gerolt had asked him to keep her alive. Dying under that fort wouldn't repay either of them.

But Marta wasn't asking him to beat the people holding Darcy. She was asking him to reach her. And Silas would be there. For once, Alaric could choose what he was willing to try before somebody else chose for him.

"I can't promise I'll get her out," he said.

"I didn't ask you to."

"I can carry the offer. And whatever she says, I'll listen."

Marta's hands went still. She studied him for a long moment, then nodded.

"There's something else you should know."

Silas looked up at her. Marta kept her eyes on Alaric.

"Darcy might be able to help you understand what happened on Gerolt's farm."

His hand stopped on Wena's neck. "How?"

"She knows history. Old wars. Describe the bodies, the machines, that circle. She might recognise something the rest of us can't. That's all I can promise."

He looked at her. "You knew that before you asked me."

"Yes."

"Why didn't you say?"

"Because I needed you to understand that she can refuse you too." Marta didn't look away. "I won't promise you anything she hasn't agreed to give."

Alaric took Gerolt's token out of his pocket and set it down beside the papers, shield side up. He wanted to ask whether she really believed Darcy could help him. But she'd already given him the only answer she had.

Silas gathered the pages into one stack.

"I need to look at the garrison before the watch changes."

"Then go," Marta said. "Come back with a route he can manage."

She turned back to Alaric.

"You listen when he tells you to stop, or run. If anyone draws steel, get out of sight. Wena and I will be waiting at the well."

He nodded.

There was no real plan. No strength inside him he even knew how to offer. By dawn he'd be walking towards the garrison while the people hunting him held every gate in Kelmend.

His hands shook when he put them on the table.

He left them there anyway.

"Then tell me what Darcy needs to hear."
