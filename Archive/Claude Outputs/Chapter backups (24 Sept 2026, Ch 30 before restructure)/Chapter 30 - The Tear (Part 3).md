# The Tear (Part 3)

The bells started while it was still grey.

Alaric was sitting on the bottom step of the water stair, with his boots a hand's breadth above the river, watching the Keeper's door across the water. The first note came down over the roofs from the hill behind him and went into him like cold.

Then another. Then all of them.

He turned to look. Above the roofs behind him, the whole hill was ringing. The stair was a long slope of little lights going up it, the vigil lamps, pale now in the dawn, thousands of them. He could hear the singing from here, far off and huge, like the sea heard from inland.

"That's the morning bell," said Hessa, behind him. "The saint's comes later. When the sun's over the roof."

She was sitting in the stern of her punt under the last arch of the aqueduct, gutting eels into a bucket with a knife as long as her forearm. She was old and brown and small, and she'd been doing it since before the light, and she didn't look up.

"Gran," said Dab. "Gran, can we go across? Can we go and see her close —"

"We've eels."

"The eels'll keep."

"The eels won't keep. That's the whole trouble with eels." She slit another one open and flicked its insides into the river. "Fifty Longest Lights, I've seen. The sick go up the stair and the sick come down it. My man went up the year he drowned. Had a cough. Came down with the same cough, and drowned in the spring, cough and all."

"*Gran.*"

"What? It's true. The Light keeps who it keeps." She dropped the eel in the bucket. "You want to see a miracle, watch me sell this lot on a feast day."

Dab looked at Alaric for help. He was ten, or near it, all elbows and knees, and he'd attached himself to Alaric from the first morning for no reason Alaric could understand.

"She'll be out soon," said Alaric. "You'll hear it. The whole city will."

"Hearing's not seeing."

"No," said Alaric. "It isn't."

Silas was awake. He'd been awake when Alaric woke, sitting with his back against the pier and the oilcloth packet of the sergeant's book on his knees. He was watching the Keeper's door too.

"Today," said Silas.

"Today."

And under the bells, faint, from further off than the hill, someone was there.

It wasn't words. It had the shape of something said close to him, quietly, the way you'd speak to someone in the same room. It belonged somewhere with lamplight in it.

It hadn't come since the Expanse. Not on the road, not in Helmi's house, not in the Library. Four days by the river, four nights under Hessa's boats, and nothing. He'd begun to think it had gone for good. He'd begun, some nights, to be glad.

It was back.

He kept his face to the water.

---

He'd answered Silas on the second night.

Not in the ruin. In the ruin, with the sun just up and the river still running out of their clothes, he'd only said, "Later," and Silas had looked at him a while longer and let go of his wrist.

"Later," Silas had said. "Up. Before the women come."

They'd gone down off the top of the steps the other way, by the lanes, keeping to the river. The washerwomen were coming down to the rocks with their baskets as they went, and nobody looked twice at two wet men. Half the old quarter was wet at that hour.

They'd followed the river road down past the palace, where the banners hung from the gate, to the aqueduct. In the shade of its last arches, where it came down to the bank and the water ran slow, the eel-catchers tied up.

That was where Helmi had found his punt. It was the only place on the river Alaric knew.

Hessa had been gutting eels then, too.

She'd looked at them standing on the stones above her, soaked and filthy, and her knife hadn't stopped.

"You're the archivist's lot," she'd said.

Silas hadn't answered.

"Four of you and a dog. The watch have been up and down the bank since the bell, asking after you." She'd flicked guts into the water. "You look like you've been in the river."

"We have," said Silas.

"Then I'd get off my stones, if I were you."

Silas had got down off her stones. He'd crouched on the edge of the punt, in front of her, and opened his hand, and there'd been silver in it. Alaric hadn't known he had any left.

Hessa had looked at the silver without touching it.

"Wanted men don't pay," she'd said.

"These do."

"By the Four." She'd taken it. It went somewhere inside her apron and didn't come out again. "Then you can drown under my boats. Mind the eels. They bite."

---

So they'd drowned under her boats, and watched the Keeper's door.

There was nothing else to do. The door sat low in the Library's river wall, across the water from the piers, a hand's breadth above the river, with its little lamp burning in the niche beside it. A pair of the watch stood on the wall above it the whole of the first day, and the whole of the second. Nobody came out. Nobody went in.

"Books, and a cat," Hessa's son reported, that evening, back from the fish market. "That's all they found under there. The priest with the seal had the whole place turned out, top to bottom, and the old Keeper stood at the stair like a post and never said a word. Books, and a cat."

"And the archivist?" said Silas.

"Drowned. Them and the dog. Went into the river off the Keeper's door, the watch reckon, trying to swim for it." He'd shrugged. "Serves them right, some say."

"Some say," said Silas.

Alaric had said nothing. He'd sat with his arms round his knees and looked at the door across the water, and thought about Wena in the dark of the drowned arch, and Redd's arms round her, and the priest with the seal at the Keeper's door, and whether a priest cared what ground was sworn.

*Books, and a cat.*

No dog.

---

Dab asked him about the sand on the second morning.

"Gran says you came out of the Expanse."

"Your gran hears a lot."

"She hears everything. She says you're the drowned men and you came out of the Expanse, and nobody comes out of the Expanse, so you must be lying about one of them." Dab sat down beside him on the stones with his knees up under his chin. "Which is it?"

"We came out of the Expanse."

"So you're lying about drowning."

"We never said we'd drowned. The watch did."

Dab thought about that for a while.

"What's it like?"

"The Expanse?" Alaric looked at the river. "Sand. Then more sand. Then sand that falls."

"Falls where?"

"Nowhere. It just goes on falling."

"That's stupid."

"Yes," said Alaric. "It is."

"Were there monsters?"

"Yes."

"Big ones?"

"Big enough."

Dab looked at him sideways with enormous satisfaction. "I knew it," he said. "Gran said you'd only say sand."

---

On the second night, Silas built a fire in a clay pot in the bottom of the empty punt, as the eel-catchers did, and sat across it from him.

The river went by in the dark. The piers stood over them, black. Hessa and her son and Dab were asleep under their own boats, further along, and there was no one to hear.

"Well," said Silas.

Alaric looked at the fire.

"It's later," said Silas.

"I know."

He held out his right hand, palm up, into the light of the pot. The line across it was thinner than it had been in the ruin. It was so thin now that you had to know where to look.

"Since the field," he said.

Silas didn't say anything.

"I woke up there with blood all over me. I didn't know whose it was. I thought it must be mine, but there was nothing under it." He turned the hand over. "Then at Marta's I was up in days. She said it was her salves. I let her."

"The limp," said Silas.

"Five days. It should have been a month. You saw."

"I saw you walking. I thought you were being stupid on it."

"And Redd's joke. In Foramen. *He heals quickly.*" Alaric closed the hand. "Everyone laughed."

"And the kitchen."

"The kitchen." He looked at the pale line. "It went to the bone. I saw the bone."

"I saw the blood," said Silas. "Enough of it."

"An hour later it was closed. By the time Redd tied it, it was already closing. I tied his knot back on so he wouldn't see."

Silas was quiet for a while.

"It's faster," he said. "Every time. That's what you're telling me."

"Every time."

The fire ticked in its pot. Somewhere under the arches, water slapped against wood.

"Does it hurt?" said Silas.

That wasn't what Alaric had expected. He didn't know what he'd expected.

"No," he said. "It itches."

"Then keep your sleeves down." Silas reached for a stick and pushed the coals together with it. "Don't bleed where people can see you stop. Don't let anyone take a knife to you to find out. And when we get the others back, you tell Redd yourself. Not me. You."

"I will."

"Anything else?"

Alaric looked at the fire.

He thought of the voice in the Expanse, that Silas knew about, and the thing it had said to him on the rock at the end, that Silas didn't. One word, broken at the front, in a woman's voice, from the heart of the sand.

His name.

"No," he said.

Silas looked at him across the fire. Then he threw the stick into the pot, and lay down in the bottom of the punt with his back to it, and said, "Sleep."

Alaric didn't. Not for hours.

---

Dab saw it first, on the third morning.

"There's a rag on the Keeper's door."

He was standing up in Hessa's punt with a basket of eels on his hip, pointing across the river. Alaric was up before he'd finished saying it.

There was a rag. It was tied to the iron ring in the wall beside the Keeper's door, the ring Silas had caught hold of the night they came. A strip of brown cloth, knotted, hanging in the damp just above the water.

Alaric couldn't see the knot from here. He didn't need to.

"It's a double," he said.

"You can't see that from here," said Silas.

"It's a double. It's Redd's."

Silas stood beside him on the stones and looked at it.

"It's my knot," he said at last. "I taught it to him."

"It's his."

"He tied it like a fool, too. Look at the tail on it." But his shoulders had come down a finger's width. "Well. He's alive, then. Or someone wants us to think so."

"He's alive."

"And the dog?"

Alaric didn't answer that. He didn't know. He looked at the rag hanging off the door until Hessa told him he was blocking her light.

---

It was Dab who carried the eels across.

It was Hessa's idea. She'd heard them talking, which Alaric was beginning to understand she did all the time, and she'd put her knife down, and said, "The Keeper's got a door on the river. The river's got eels in it. What does a door on the river want, of a morning?"

"Eels," said Dab.

"There you are, then."

So Dab poled across in the smallest punt at the middle of the morning with a basket of eels and knocked on the Keeper's door, and the watch on the wall above looked down at him, and then at each other, and let him be. A child selling eels. Every door on the river bought eels.

The door opened. A small white-haired woman in a dark robe looked out at him, and at the basket, and at the watch above, and bought the whole basket.

"She doesn't even like eels," Dab reported, when he came back. He was pleased with himself. "She said so. She said, *I cannot abide eels, give me all of them.* And she gave me this back in the basket, and said to give it to the tall one who doesn't talk."

He held it out to Silas.

It was a scrap of paper folded small and neat, corner to corner. Silas unfolded it. Alaric looked over his shoulder.

The handwriting leaned backwards, precise and small.

"'All three well,'" Silas read aloud, under his breath. "'The dog has eaten a candle and the Keeper's second-best slipper, and is unrepentant.'"

Alaric sat down on the stones, because his knees had stopped holding him.

"'I. proposes the following,'" read Silas. The corner of his mouth moved. "She proposes. He writes it down."

He read on in silence, and then aloud again, slowly. "'The public halls open at the morning bell on the Longest Light, for pilgrims. Come as pilgrims. The Great Hall.'"

"The Great Hall," said Alaric.

"'The temple has sent for the old hymnals for the office on the stair tonight, and I. will bring them up in a cart. When every eye in the city is on the saint, we go out by the front and down to the Library landing, to your boat. Bring nothing that looks like a sword. R. has the swords.'"

"Is that all?"

Silas turned the paper over. There was one more line on the back, smaller. "'The core is well. I have catalogued it.'"

Alaric laughed.

It came out of him before he knew it was there, loud, under the arches, and Hessa looked up from her bucket, and Dab grinned without knowing why. Silas didn't laugh. But he folded the paper up again, corner to corner, exactly as it had come, and put it inside his shirt next to the sergeant's book.

"Downriver," he said to Hessa. "The day after tomorrow. When the saint comes down. Five people and a dog."

"Downriver's two silver."

"One."

"Two. It's a feast day."

"It's a feast day for you, too."

"That's why it's two," said Hessa, and went back to her eels.

---

Now it was the day.

The morning bell stopped. The sky was going from grey to white behind the hill. Up on the hill the stair was all people, a long slope of them going up to the gold doors, and the singing came and went on the wind.

And under it, the voice.

It had grown while he sat there. It was still no words. But it was closer. It was as close as someone sitting at his back.

"Up," said Silas.

Alaric got up.

Hessa didn't look up from her eels. "The Library landing," she said. "When you hear the saint's bell. I'll be at the steps with the big punt. If you're not down by the time she's halfway down the hill, I'm going to sell eels, and you can swim."

"We'll be down."

"Hm," said Hessa.

Dab poled them across in the small punt, standing in the stern with his tongue between his teeth, and put them off at the Library landing on the far bank. He had a sun token on a cord round his neck, and before he pushed off again he took it off and held it out to Alaric.

"You've not got one," he said. "Pilgrims have one."

"I can't take your —"

"It's Gran's. She's got nine." He pushed it into Alaric's hand. "Bring it back after."

"I will."

"Say a prayer for her with it. For Gran's knees." He was already pushing off, back across the water. "She won't ask. But her knees are bad."

---

The far bank was nearly empty.

Everyone who could walk had crossed the river in the night for the vigil, or climbed onto a roof.

The streets round the Library were shuttered and quiet, strung with white banners that hung straight down in the still air, and with lanterns that no one had lit yet. A dog barked somewhere and stopped. An old woman sat on a stool in a doorway with a blanket on her knees and her face turned across the river towards the hill, and didn't see them go by.

"Hessa's taking us as far as the weir," said Silas, as they walked. "After that it's the road."

"Which road?"

"The one away from here, to start with." He glanced across the river at the hill, and away again. "Then north and east. The old man won't say it. But he wants to see her."

"Darcy."

"Darcy." Silas walked on a few steps. "That's a conversation for the road."

The voice came with him.

It didn't matter where he looked. It didn't get louder when he turned his head towards the hill, or quieter when he turned away. It was only there, close, the way a hand is there when someone rests it on your back.

"Where'd you go?" said Silas.

"Nowhere."

"You've been nowhere a lot, this morning."

Alaric kept walking.

"Is it her?" said Silas. "The one in the sand."

He could have said yes. It would have been easy. Silas had already heard the worst of it, by the fire. There was nothing in this that was worse than a cut that closed in an hour.

He didn't know why he wanted to keep it. He only knew that he did.

"No," he said.

Silas didn't ask again.

---

The Library's public doors stood open.

They were tall, taller than Helmi's house, bronze gone green, and they stood open to the street with a single old porter on a stool beside them, asleep with his chin on his chest. Beyond them lay a long passage, and beyond the passage, the hall.

Alaric had walked it once, in the middle of the night, in a copyist's smock, with his head down. He knew it the moment he came into it.

The long tables in rows. The lamps hanging on their chains from a ceiling so high the light hardly reached it. The benches along the walls with their thousands of names. And the ceiling itself, painted from end to end: armies, burning towns, a river full of the dead, people with their arms raised against a sky full of fire.

Over the doors behind them, everything ended in the sun. It was enormous and gold and it filled the ceiling from wall to wall, and its rays reached down into all the burning, and put it out.

*It's the first thing the pilgrims see.*

The hall was empty.

There were no copyists at the tables. There were no pilgrims on the benches. There was only, in an alcove near the doors, an old man in white kneeling before a painted sun, with a lamp on the floor beside him.

He lifted his head as they came in. It was a face like a walnut.

"Early," said Aubin.

"Yes, Father," said Silas.

"Pilgrims." The old eyes went over them: two men with sun tokens, hooded, in borrowed coats. They didn't seem much interested. "You've come the wrong way for the saint, my sons. She's over there." He nodded down the hall, towards the river wall, and the river, and the hill beyond it. "She's always over there."

"We came to see the ceiling," said Alaric.

Aubin looked at him.

He looked at him for a while, with his head a little on one side, and Alaric felt it: the same feeling he'd had on the back of his neck the night they came through here with Idony. He wondered what the old man saw.

"Well," said Aubin. "It's a good ceiling." He went back to his prayers.

---

They waited by the benches at the door end, under the sun.

The singing came in faintly through the high windows. Alaric sat on a bench and ran his thumb over the names cut into it, one on top of another, and waited, and the voice waited with him.

"'Bram was here,'" Silas read, over his shoulder. The letters were big and deep and crooked, cut right across three older names.

"Who's Bram?"

"Somebody who wanted it known." Silas sat down at the other end of the bench, with his back to the wall where he could see both doors. "Somebody with a better knife than the rest."

The cart came up out of the floor.

That was how it looked. At the far end of the hall, by the river wall, the door to Idony's stair opened, and a small handcart came up out of it backwards, bumping, with a small straight-backed woman hauling it by the handles and someone below heaving it up from underneath. It was heaped with books. Old, fat, black-bound books, spilling.

Idony got it over the top step, and stopped, and put a hand on her back.

"Well?" she said down the hall. "Don't stand there. I'm sixty-six."

They went.

They were halfway down the hall when the books heaved.

They heaved and slid and went over the side of the cart in a black avalanche, and out of the middle of them came Wena.

She came out like a stone from a sling. She hit the floor and skidded on the old stone and got her feet under her, and then she was coming down the hall at them, flat out, with her ears back and her whole body one long, low, silent run.

She hit Alaric at the knees.

He went down on the floor under the painted armies with his arms full of dog. She was licking his face, his ears, his hands, his hair, making a sound he'd never heard her make, a high, broken whining that went on and on. He had his face in her ruff. She smelled of candles, and old books, and wet dog, and home.

"Hello," he said into her fur. "Hello. Hello."

"She ate a candle," said Redd.

He'd come up the stair behind the cart, red in the face from shoving it. He had his father's sword on his back and two more swords under his arm, wrapped in a copyist's smock, and he was trying hard not to look glad.

"She ate a whole candle and threw it up in the Keeper's bed," he said. "The Keeper's own bed. I've never been so ashamed of anyone in my life."

"You're lying," said Alaric.

"I'm not. Ask her." Redd came down the hall. He stopped in front of Silas. He held out one of the swords, hilt first.

"You came back for yours," Redd said.

Silas took it.

He looked at it, and at Redd, and Alaric thought he was going to speak. Then he buckled it on instead, and gave the strap a hard tug, and said, "You took your time with that knot."

"The knot was perfect."

"Look at the tail on it."

"The tail was *artistic*," said Redd.

Then he turned and held the other sword out to Alaric, and Alaric got up off the floor with Wena leaning against his legs, and reached for it.

His right hand came out of his sleeve.

Redd looked at it.

He looked at the palm, turned up to take the hilt. He looked at the thin pale line across it, like a crease in paper, and the bare wrist, and no knot.

"Where's my knot?" said Redd.

"The grate had it."

"That was to the bone." Redd's voice had changed. "Al. I tied that. That was to the *bone*. That was five days ago."

"He heals quickly," said Silas.

Redd turned his head and looked at him. It was his own joke, from Foramen. Alaric watched him know it, and see that Silas knew it too.

"That's not quick," said Redd. "That's —"

"Later," said Silas.

Redd looked at Alaric. Alaric looked back at him, and didn't look away, and after a while Redd put the sword into his hand, hilt first, as he'd given Silas his.

"Later," Redd said. "You're telling me. You, not him."

"I will."

"You'd better." He looked at the hand once more. Then he grabbed Alaric by the back of the neck and pulled him in, hard, forehead to forehead, and held him there for the space of a breath, and let go. "You stupid bastard. They said you drowned."

"We didn't."

"I *know* you didn't. I tied a *rag* on a *door*."

"This," said Idony, from the cart, "is a library."

"It's a feast day," said Redd.

"It is a library on a feast day."

---

Helmi came up the stair last.

He came up slowly, one step at a time, with one hand on the wall and the other holding the satchel against his chest, and he stood at the top for a while getting his breath, as Idony had. He was thinner than five days ago. He'd been thin then.

He came down the hall, and stopped in front of Alaric, and held out the satchel.

"Yours," said Helmi.

Alaric took it. It was heavy in the old way, as it had been all across the sand. He could feel the shape of the core inside, through the cloth, against his hip.

"I've catalogued it," said Helmi.

"I read that."

"It seemed the least I could do. It's the first thing I've catalogued since they took my keys." Helmi looked him up and down. "You look dreadful."

"We've been living with eels."

"Yes. We had some. Idony bought a great many eels." Helmi's mouth moved. "She can't abide them."

"I cannot," said Idony. She'd come down the hall behind him, dusting her hands. "The dog ate most of them. It was the only thing it didn't bring back up."

"How did you hide them?" said Silas. "Books and a cat, the whole river's saying."

"I didn't hide them," said Idony. "I put them in my rooms, and I stood on my stair."

"A Keeper's rooms are sworn ground," said Helmi. "Even a priest with a seal can't cross it without the high priest's word. And the high priest has had rather a busy week."

"He stood at the bottom of her stair for an hour," said Redd. "The priest. Reading the Keepers' oath to himself, twice over, looking for a way round it."

"There isn't one," said Idony.

"And Wena snored," said Redd. "The whole hour. I had my hand round her muzzle, and she snored through my fingers."

Idony looked at the four of them, and at the dog, and at the books all over her floor.

"Well," she said. "You'd better go. The saint will be out soon, and when she's out, nobody in this city will be looking at anything else."

"You're coming," said Helmi.

"No."

"Idony."

"Someone has to be Keeper when they come back." She said it simply, as she'd said *yes* at her door. "Someone has to tell the priest with the seal that the Vaults are just as he left them. And they are, except for my slipper."

Helmi looked at her for a long time.

"I'll write," he said.

"You never wrote."

"I'll start."

"You'll be late," said Idony. "You were always late."

"I know," said Helmi.

Alaric heard it. He was standing right beside them, and he heard it go into him, as it had at her door in the dark. *You're late. I know.* A woman's voice with a laugh underneath it, somewhere with lamplight in it.

And close behind him, the other voice, the one with no words, answered it.

He put his hand on Wena's head, and kept it there.

---

"Should've brought a knife," said Redd.

He was sitting on the pilgrims' bench under the sun, with his fingers on the names. He'd sat down there while Helmi and Idony said what they had to say to each other, apart, by the cart, too quietly to hear.

"You did," said Silas.

"A little one. For names." Redd looked up. "Go on. Give it here."

Silas looked at him. Then he took a small knife out of his boot and gave it to him handle first, and Redd bent over the old wood and began to cut.

He cut REDD, in big square letters, over the top of BRAM WAS HERE. Then, underneath, smaller, he cut WENA, and after it a mark that was meant to be a paw and looked like a turnip.

"There," he said. "She's been to the Great Library. That's more than Gerolt ever did." He held the knife out to Alaric. "You want yours?"

Alaric took the knife.

He looked at the bench. At all the names. Hundreds of them, thousands, cut in with knives and pins and fingernails, one on top of another, some so old they'd worn back into the grain. People who'd been here. People who'd wanted it known.

He had one name. He'd had it since the floor of Gerolt's cabin, and he'd never told anyone where it came from.

He put the point of the knife to the wood, and cut the first line of an A.

Across the river, the great bell rang.

---

It was the deep one, the one over the temple doors. One note.

The hall went still. Even Idony, by the cart, lifted her head.

Then another note, and then all of them: the towers, the roofs, the shrines, every bell in Favale, one after another, near and far, until the high windows of the hall were ringing with it and the lamps on their chains shivered.

The saint's bell.

From the far side of the river came the sound of the whole city breathing in.

"Now," said Silas.

He was already up. Redd was up too, scooping hymnals off the floor by the armful and flinging them back onto the cart. Down at the far end, Helmi and Idony had turned from each other at last.

"Get the dog under," said Silas.

"She won't go under. She's been under books for five days. Look at her face."

"Get her under."

Across the river, the hill roared.

It was thousands of voices at once. It came in through the high windows and rolled down the hall and set the lamps swinging, and then the hymn came back behind it, louder than before.

"She's touched someone," said Idony. Her hand had gone to the token at her throat. "That's what that is. Someone's got up."

Alaric stood by the bench with Silas's knife still in his hand.

The voice had come up with the bells. It came up all at once, as the bells had, from close to closer, until it wasn't at his back any more. It was in him. It was under his ribs and behind his eyes. It had no words, and it was so close that it filled his head, and it was reaching.

It was reaching for something.

"Alaric," said Silas. "The cart."

He gave Silas his knife back. He went down the hall and took one handle of the cart, and Redd took the other, and they began to wheel it towards the doors, with the hymnals sliding and Wena walking alongside with her nose in Redd's free hand, refusing to get in.

The hill roared again. It seemed to come from a little further off. A little further down.

The voice climbed with it.

Helmi was coming down the hall behind the cart with Idony's arm under his. He was slow. He'd been slow on the stair. Idony matched him step for step, and neither of them said anything, and Alaric understood that they'd said it all already, and this was only the walking.

Another roar. Further down.

"Keep it moving," said Silas, from the doors. He was looking out into the street. "Porter's still asleep. Street's empty. Keep it moving."

They were three lengths from the doors, under the sun, when Wena stopped.

The bells were ringing and ringing, and she lifted her nose up to them, and her throat worked, and she began to howl. It was a long, rising farm dog's howl, the kind a dog gives to bells. It went up into the painted ceiling and hung there.

"Oi," said Redd. "Oi, girl. Not in the Library."

At the far end of the hall, a grey shape had come out from among the spilled hymnals by Idony's stair, with its tail up like a bottlebrush, to see what the noise was.

Wena saw it.

The howl stopped. Her whole body went rigid. Her lip lifted.

"Oh, no," said Redd. "Oh, no, no —"

"You promised her," said Alaric.

He didn't know why he said it. It came out of him from somewhere far off. He was looking at the cat, and the dog, and the long hall between them full of the saint's bells, and the voice in him was reaching and reaching and reaching.

"Tomorrow," he said. "You said. Tomorrow she could have every cat in —"

The voice screamed.

---

It screamed inside him.

It was the voice. The one with no words. It screamed. It wasn't a word. It was the sound of someone being torn in half. It came from inside his own head, and it was louder than the bells, and louder than anything, and it didn't stop.

**THOOM.**

His heart hit the inside of his ribs so hard it knocked him back a step.

**THOOM.**

The pain came after it. It came in behind his eyes like a spike, and down into his teeth, and through his chest, the way it had on the floor of Gerolt's cabin with the arrows coming in, but worse, worse than that, worse than anything, as if every part of him were being pulled in one direction at once, towards the river, towards the hill.

Something in him answered it.

He didn't know what. He didn't decide it. A part of him he'd never felt before turned towards the scream, as a face turns towards a sound it knows.

And the far end of the hall came apart.

He saw it. He'd see it for the rest of his life.

The river wall split. A line came through it, straight down, from the roof to the floor, and the white morning showed through the line. Then the line came down the hall.

It came faster than anything. It came faster than he could turn his head.

It went through the floor, and the floor opened along it like a seam unpicked. It went through the tables in their rows, and each of them fell apart in two halves. It went through the lamps on their chains, and the chains parted, and the lamps fell.

It went down the length of the painted ceiling, through the armies and the burning towns and the river full of the dead, and they opened, and the light of the sky came through.

It went through the benches. Through the names.

It went past Idony, standing with her hand at her throat, and past Helmi. It went through the cart, and the hymnals burst up out of it in a storm of pages. It went past Redd, and past Aubin's alcove.

It came straight at him.

It stopped.

---

It stopped at his feet.

The floor was cut in front of him, a black line in the old stone the width of a hand, running back from the toes of his boots all the way down the hall, all the way to the river wall and through it. It stopped there. At him.

Above him, the painted sun had been cut open to its heart.

He was on his back. He didn't remember falling. He was lying on the floor by the pilgrims' bench, with the sun above him split to its middle, and through the split the roof of the hall was open, and above the roof was the sky.

The scream stopped.

It stopped all at once, the way a thing stops when it's cut. There was no voice. There was nothing in his head at all. There was only his heart, hammering, and the lamps still swinging on the chains that were left, and the dust coming down through the long split in the ceiling onto his face.

Then the sound of the hill arrived.

It came across the river a long breath later, the way thunder comes after lightning. It came through the split wall and down the split hall, a roar so deep and so long that the floor shook with it, and Wena flattened herself against the stone beside him, and somewhere at the far end, Idony cried out.

It went on for a long time.

When it stopped, the bells had stopped too. All of them but one.

Somewhere close, on this side of the river, one small bell went on ringing, alone, thin and small in all that quiet.

No one in the hall moved.

---

"Al," said Redd.

He was on his knees beside him. Alaric didn't know how he'd got there. His face was white, and there was dust in his hair.

"Al. Al, look at me. Are you — where's it — are you hurt? Where are you hurt?"

"I'm not."

"You fell over. You screamed. You —"

"I'm not hurt."

He wasn't. He could feel it. There wasn't a mark on him. The pain had gone out of him with the scream, all of it, and left him lying on the floor with his heart going and nothing else.

"What was that?" Redd's voice had gone up high, like a boy's. "What *was* that? Al —"

"Don't touch the line," said Helmi, from down the hall.

"I wasn't going to *touch* the —"

"Don't."

Silas was standing over them.

He wasn't looking at Alaric. He was looking at the floor. At the black line cut in the stone, running away down the hall, straight as a rule, from the river wall to Alaric's feet. It went past Idony and Helmi. It went through the cart. It went past Redd, where Redd had been standing, a hand's breadth from his boot.

It went past all of them.

It didn't go past Alaric.

Silas lifted his eyes from the line and looked at him. His face was the stillest Alaric had ever seen it.

"It stopped at you," he said.

---

Helmi had come down the hall.

He came along beside the line, slowly, not touching it, with one hand out against the tables as he passed. He stopped at the foot of it and looked down at Alaric lying there, and at the line, and at where the two of them met.

He didn't say anything. He was an archivist. He was looking at something that had never been catalogued.

Behind him, Idony was standing with her face turned up to the ceiling. To the sun, cut open to its heart. Her hand had gone to the token at her throat, and it stayed there.

"That came from the hill," Helmi said quietly. "From the stair. It came across the river from the stair."

No one answered him.

In his alcove by the doors, Aubin was still on his knees. The line ran past him a pace from his lamp. He wasn't looking at it. He was looking at Alaric.

Idony had turned away from the sun. She was looking down the line, the whole length of the hall, to where it came in through the river wall.

"My rooms," she said. It wasn't to anyone. "That's my wall."

Then she picked up a hymnal and went and beat out the oil that was burning where a lamp had come down across the line, because it was a library, and somebody had to.

Alaric didn't know how long he lay there.

Long enough for the dust to stop coming down through the split. Long enough for Silas to go to the doors and look out, and come back, and go again. Across the river, smoke was standing up over the roofs of the city side, in twenty places, then thirty. The one small bell rang on, and on, and then it stopped.

Redd sat on the floor beside him with his father's sword across his knees and his hand on Wena, and didn't say anything, which Alaric had never known him do.

No one said that Hessa was waiting at the landing. No one said anything about going.

Alaric lay on his back on the floor of the Great Library, with Wena pressed against his side and the half-cut A on the bench beside his head, and looked up through the split in the roof.

---

The sky was open.

The hall's roof had been cut, and the sky above the roof had been cut too. It hung open, straight across the morning, a long wound in the air running away from him over the river, towards the hill. He could see its edges. They were ragged. They moved a little, like the edges of torn cloth in a wind.

He couldn't see into it. His eyes wouldn't stay.

Then there was a sound in it.

It came from far off. It came from further off than the hill, further off than anything, from somewhere so far away that the sound was old by the time it got here. It was a long, deep, grinding sound, like a mountain dragging itself over stone.

Wena began to shake.

The sound came again, closer. The edges of the wound in the sky shivered.

And something came through.

He couldn't understand it, at first. It was too big. His eyes kept trying to make it into something smaller, a cloud, a roof, a ship, and it wouldn't be any of them. It came through the torn air above the river the way a man comes through a doorway too low for him: head first, and then shoulders, forcing it.

It was a head.

It was a head as long as Helmi's house was tall, dark as old iron, scaled like a slate roof. It had a mouth. It had eyes, and they were open, and they were burning.

Then a neck, long and thick and armoured, and the neck was bleeding.

There were spears in it. Old spears, dozens of them, broken off short or hanging long, driven in between the plates. Their shafts were black. There was red mud caked to the scales round them, and more mud on the claw that came through after the neck and gripped the edge of the tear like a hand gripping the top of a wall.

From that claw hung a length of silver. It was a leg. A long silver leg, jointed, hooked through the claw at the knee, dragging, as if it had been torn from a body many times its size and never let go.

Then the wings.

They came through last, folded, and then they opened. They were torn to rags. Light showed through them in a hundred places. They opened anyway, over the river, over the Library, over the whole of the far bank, and their shadow came down across the split roof of the hall and across Alaric's face.

It was hurt. It was furious. It had come out of a war.

It lifted its head over Favale, over the river and the hill and all of it, and drew in its breath.

Then it roared.
