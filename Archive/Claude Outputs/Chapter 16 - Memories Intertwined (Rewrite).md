# Memories Intertwined

On the third day of the crossing, the first day Neldor trusted her with a brush, the ship rolled and the bucket got away from Seralune.

Its wooden base scraped across the deck, struck a coil of rope, and emptied itself over Neldor's boots.

He looked down.

Seawater ran off his ankles towards the narrow openings along the rail. The bucket carried on without it, turned once on its side, and came to rest against the mast.

Seralune kept both hands on the long-handled brush.

"You told me it would stay beside the brace."

Neldor lifted one wet boot. "I told you to put it on the other side."

She looked at the mast, then at the matching wooden support across from the one she'd chosen. The deck sloped towards it as the ship settled out of the roll.

"You pointed."

"I did."

"At the *mast*."

Neldor laughed before he could stop himself, deep enough that the sailor splicing a line near the bow looked round.

Seralune waited.

He got hold of himself, though it stayed in his voice. "I thought you'd know which side of a mast holds a bucket."

"Why would I know that?"

That finished off the rest of his laughter. He walked through her spill and fetched the bucket.

"You wouldn't," he admitted. "That was my mistake."

He set it against the high side of the brace and stopped the rope coil with his foot before the water reached it.

"When the deck leans, anything loose goes with it. Put the bucket where the roll pushes it into something, not away. And use half that much water. You're scrubbing the boards. You don't have to drown them."

Seralune looked along the stretch she'd finished.

Pale streaks crossed the wood wherever her brush had been. Some followed the grain. Most didn't. Water had pooled round her bare toes and soaked the hem of the trousers Nyana had found for her.

"You might have mentioned that before I started."

"I did mention the grain."

"You said to work *with* the wood."

"That's what it means."

"No," Seralune said, certain and annoyed about it. "That's what it means to someone who already knows."

Neldor looked at the brush as if deciding whether to take it back. Instead, he crouched and ran two fingers along a board.

"These lines. Follow them towards the bow. Short strokes where the pitch rises, longer ones across the middle. If the brush catches, change the angle. Don't force it with your shoulder."

She lowered the bristles and copied him. Her cut palm complained under Nyana's clean wrapping. The bruise near her shoulder pulled when she reached too far, so she shortened the stroke and tried again.

The pale crust on the wood thinned under the brush.

Neldor nodded. "That."

"That was considerably clearer."

"I'll remember it, the next time a lady asks to scrub my deck."

The brush stopped.

"I gave you the name Sera."

"And I've used it." He stood. "Doesn't mean I believe you grew up carrying buckets."

"Was that meant as mockery?"

"No." He nudged the bucket back beside her. "I've just never had one ask before."

He went off towards the stern, leaving wet footprints behind him.

Seralune thought about calling him back to dry them himself.

Then she looked at the clean strip under her brush. It was barely wider than her hand. She looked at all the boards still left and imagined the whole stretch clean.

She filled the bucket halfway and set it against the right brace.

This time, when the deck rolled, it stayed where she'd put it.

---

She'd nearly finished the stretch between the mainmast and the forward hatch when Nyana appeared at the top of the companionway, climbing backwards with one hand held down below her.

Nereth came up after her, slowly, her fingers locked round Nyana's wrist.

Seralune put the brush down.

Nereth wore a loose green shirt under a dark wrap Nyana had cut away from the healing bite. Two days' rest had brought some colour back to the unhurt half of her face. On the other side, the black-purple veins had stopped moving under her skin. They'd settled into long dark scars running from below her left eye, across her temple and up under her hair. A second branch vanished beneath her collar towards the shoulder.

The eye stayed closed.

Nereth reached the deck and let go of Nyana before both feet had settled.

Nyana kept her hand up. "You let go too early."

"I had both feet under me." Nereth leaned against the companionway.

"One of them was still on the step." Nyana turned to Seralune. "She's been awake since dawn, and she's spent most of it arguing that walking is a kind of rest."

"I said it was preferable to lying down," Nereth corrected.

Seralune came over. "Why are you walking?"

"Because I need to know whether I can. We won't have this ship, or Nyana's room, once we reach land."

"And finding out had to be today?"

Nereth's good eye went to the brush, the wet trousers, and the stretch of clean deck.

"You seem to have chosen today as well."

The old dryness in her voice warmed Seralune more than all Nyana's morning reassurances had.

"I've become useful," Seralune said.

Nereth considered the boards. "Then I'm glad one of us has."

Nyana laid a folded blanket on a low chest by the mast. "She may walk from here to that chest. Slowly. With one of us beside her."

"I can reach it alone."

Six ordinary steps. The deck between them was clear, though it never stayed level for long.

"Then try," Seralune said.

Nyana drew breath to object.

Nereth straightened.

Seralune moved out of her path but stayed close enough to reach. "I'll walk beside you. I won't hold you unless you need me."

"Thank you."

The first step landed carefully. The second came before the deck could shift. The third took longer, but she kept going. Seralune watched her feet, not her face. Nereth set each one flat before she trusted it.

She'd almost reached the chest when a stronger gust filled the sail above them.

The deck leaned.

Nereth's next step reached for a place that had moved. Her knee folded, and the hand she threw out towards the mast found only air.

Seralune caught her round the waist.

The weight dragged across her bad shoulder. She turned with it instead of fighting it and let Nereth fall against her good side, and Nyana reached them from behind. Nereth's boots scraped once across the boards. Nyana braced them until the deck came level.

"Please don't tell me I should have listened," Nereth said.

Seralune shifted her hold so Nereth could lean on her properly. "I was going to ask whether you'd let me walk the rest with you."

"I nearly reached it by myself."

"You did. Now let me help you finish."

Nyana brought Nereth's arm across Seralune's shoulders and checked the bandage under the wrap. "Did the scars pull?"

"They always pull." Some of the edge had gone out of it. "It's worse under the eye."

"Worse than this morning?"

Nereth weighed it before she answered. "No. The same."

Nyana touched the skin beside the lowest scar, carefully, not pressing. "No new heat. Nothing moving under the skin."

"They still throb," Nereth said. "You just can't see it now."

Seralune felt the admission through the arm across her shoulders. Nereth had walked six steps with that pain keeping time under her face, and had mentioned it only when she was asked straight out.

"We're sitting down," Seralune said.

"I can still walk if you help me."

"Then we'll finish together."

They lowered her onto the blanket. Seralune stayed beside her.

Nyana finished taking Nereth's pulse. "You two have got very close, haven't you?" She sounded delighted. "It's rather sweet."

Seralune looked down at Nereth's arm. It was still round her shoulders.

Nereth followed her glance and took it away.

"I was falling over," she told Nyana. "I'd have held on to your brother if he'd been nearest."

"She carried you through half a mountain," Nyana said. "And you've asked where she is every single time you've woken up."

"I needed to know whether she'd caused another disaster while I slept."

"Of course," Nyana said. "Nothing sweet about that at all."

Colour came into the unscarred side of Nereth's face.

Seralune sat beside her on the chest. "I asked about you as well."

"I know," Nereth muttered. She turned her good eye on Nyana. "Your patient would like to rest without being discussed."

"Then my patient should stop giving me reasons to discuss her."

Nyana laughed and shook the blanket out across Nereth's knees. Seralune drew one corner of it over the hand Nereth had tucked under her other arm.

Nereth didn't move it.

---

Neldor brought the road to them that afternoon.

He spread a narrow strip of sailcloth across the same chest while Nereth rested against the mast. Charcoal lines crossed it: the northern harbour, the road leaving it, and a dark mark where a second road came in from the west.

"Don't use this to count miles," he warned. "It shows you where the road bends. Nothing more. Follow it too closely and you'll end up walking into the hills."

Seralune crouched across from him. "Then what should we count?"

"Landmarks. The harbour road follows the coast to a stone shrine with a broken bell. Take the inland fork there. Two days after that, you should meet the pilgrim road into Favale."

"Why should?" Nereth asked.

He tapped the charcoal line. "The road should still be there. I can't promise who's holding it. If Mydean soldiers turn you back, don't stand there arguing over my map. Find a farm and ask which way the supply carts are going."

He set two small packs beside the sailcloth. One held oatcakes. The other held dried fruit, smoked fish, clean cloth and a stoppered jar that smelled faintly of Nyana's medicines.

"From my own stores," he said, before Seralune could glance towards the hold. "Nothing in the king's crates has been touched."

"This is a great deal more than a little food."

"It'll feel like less after a week between two of you. Ration it. Carry what water you can, and fill up whenever anyone offers. There are long stretches between the inland farms."

Seralune rested a hand on one pack. "What do you want for it?"

Neldor nodded at the clean stretch of deck. Sailors had been crossing it all day, and the pale salt hadn't come back.

"Finish what you started before we reach land. And help Nyana sort the medicine stores. She's been complaining about my labels since before Waluna."

"Your labels are numbers," Nyana called from the forward hatch. "Half the bottles aren't."

Neldor raised his voice without turning round. "You've always known which numbers mean fever."

"That doesn't make them better labels."

Seralune looked down at the map to hide her smile. "I accept."

Neldor reached behind him and brought out two hooded travelling cloaks, plain brown, worn pale at the fastenings. A pair of long scarves lay folded on top.

"Those aren't for the weather," Nereth said.

"Not only for the weather." He picked up one scarf and folded it lengthwise. "Mydea's already frightened of Natharul. Two elves stepping off a ship full of royal supplies will be remembered before they reach the end of the pier."

Seralune touched the pointed edge of her ear.

"You want us to hide them."

"Until you're clear of the harbour, at least. I'd rather they remembered my cargo than your faces."

Seralune took the scarf. "Show me."

He showed her on himself, drawing the cloth over his hair and fastening it under the hood so neither ear broke the line of the fabric. Her first try pulled too tight under her chin. The second left one ear showing every time she turned her head.

Nereth reached out.

"Bring the fold lower. Your hair can cover the edge."

Seralune did. Nereth tucked two strands over the cloth and drew the hood forward.

Neldor studied the result. "At a glance, that'll do."

"And when someone looks more closely?"

"Keep moving. Keep the hood down. Most people won't look twice."

Nereth touched the scars across her face. The hood could shadow them. It couldn't take them away.

"They'll look twice at me."

Neldor didn't offer her anything false. "Then let them think you were burned. There are enough wounded people on Mydean roads that most won't ask."

Nereth lowered her hand.

Seralune looked at the dark line of the road to Favale. "How many people know we're aboard?"

"My crew knows Nyana took charge of two hurt passengers, and that you're leaving at the northern harbour. Nothing more." He folded his arms. "That's all they need to know, unless trouble follows you onto my ship."

"It won't."

"The southern slope tore open two days ago. I'm asking what you know, Sera, not what you hope."

"I don't believe anyone has followed us."

"Thank you."

He started rolling the sailcloth up from one end.

"Why Favale?" he asked. "You told Nyana you're looking for your mother. You didn't say why the road starts there."

Seralune hesitated. He waited with the map half rolled, and she remembered the lie she'd told him in Waluna.

"A man helped us get out of the palace," she said. "He was hurt. I had to leave him there." She kept her eyes on the map. "My mother left instructions with his family. He told me someone in Favale could help me find her."

Neldor let the loose edge of the map curl over his thumb before he finished rolling it.

"Do you know she's alive?"

"No." The word hurt differently now that she could say it without demanding another answer. "I only know she left something for me, and that the trail starts in Favale."

"And if it ends there?"

Seralune looked past the bow at the horizon. No land broke it.

"Then I'll find out why."

He tied the map and laid it across the two packs. "Keep these below till we're close to shore. Leave them on deck and the sea'll have them before you need them."

He went back to the stern.

When he was out of earshot, Nereth said, "He respects you more than he did in Waluna."

Seralune pulled the scarf loose from under her hair. "He knows I can clean a deck."

"A rare royal talent."

"I intend to become unbearable about it."

Nereth started to smile, then winced as it pulled at the scars.

---

By night, only one lantern still burned near the bow.

The watch stayed at the stern with Neldor. Two sailors worked quietly under the forward sail, their voices lost every time the canvas filled. Everyone else had gone below or found shelter among the covered cargo.

Nereth had refused to go back to the medical store after her evening sleep.

"Nyana said you were to stay lying down," Seralune reminded her, as they settled against a lashed stack of empty barrels.

"I'm sitting. The difference is mostly direction."

"Nyana won't think much of that argument."

"Then you can improve it before she hears it."

Seralune tucked the blanket over Nereth's legs. The sea had cooled with the sun gone, and the wind found every gap the barrels left between them.

Far past the bow, the horizon divided black water from a sky crowded with stars. She'd known their names once. Some were still where she expected. Others had shifted so slightly that looking at them felt like seeing an old friend wearing someone else's expression.

Nereth rested her head back against the barrel.

"Does it still hurt?" Seralune asked.

"Yes."

"How badly?"

Nereth considered. "Less than the aqueduct. More than I'd choose."

Seralune looked at the scars disappearing under her hair. "Nyana can't tell whether they'll fade."

"I know."

"Does that frighten you?"

Nereth's fingers moved over the blanket until they found a loose thread. "The pain does. The face is still mine, whether people enjoy looking at it or not."

The thread survived one pull. Nereth let it go before the weave could open.

"It doesn't make me want to look away," Seralune said.

"Nyana has infected you with sentiment."

"I was capable of it before."

"You hid it better."

The wind moved round the barrels.

Seralune thought of the map waiting below. "What will you do when we find my mother?"

"That depends on what she's like."

"She's kind."

"She *was* kind." Nereth said it gently. "A thousand years is a long time to become someone else."

Seralune drew the blanket further over Nereth's knees. "It didn't feel long to me."

"No. But it belonged to everyone else."

Seralune remembered her mother by the south-garden fountain, the silver signet hanging at her throat. In memory her face was young. The hands in Seralune's hair hadn't carried the marks of another century, let alone ten.

"I want to ask her why she left," she said. "Then I want to know why Thaer wouldn't tell me."

"In that order?"

"I think so."

"Good. Your brother's further away."

Seralune almost smiled.

The stars went out.

---

Her room held the last warmth of evening.

Lamplight lay across the edge of her bed. The curtain moved beside the open window, and every time the wind came in, the loose latch lifted from the frame and tapped against the wood.

She had meant to sit on the edge of the bed until he came.

She was standing by the door instead.

She'd spent the last quarter of an hour arranging her anger into the greeting he deserved. It hadn't stopped her crossing to the door twice to listen for footsteps she refused to admit she recognised.

The handle moved.

She opened the door before he could.

Dark-brown hair had fallen across his forehead. He wore no armour, and no sword hung at his side. The lamplight caught green eyes level with her own, and the rounded edge of an ear that was unmistakably human.

Relief wiped out the greeting she'd prepared.

Seralune laughed and caught his hand.

"You're late again."

"I know."

He was trying not to smile.

She pulled him over the threshold before he could hesitate again. His fingers closed round hers with the ease of long practice, and his quiet laugh followed them into the room.

The door closed.

The latch tapped against the window.

She lifted his left hand between them. No cloth covered it. No blood marked the palm. His skin was warmer than hers, rougher under the fingers, and she knew where every small ridge would meet her touch before it did.

She pressed his palm to her cheek.

The warmth came into her without any resistance at all.

A strand of her black hair lay between his thumb and her skin. He moved it aside. Seralune turned her face into his hand as if she'd been waiting all evening to finish that one movement.

Perhaps she had.

He'd stopped smiling. Whatever had kept him outside her door had followed him in.

Her thumb rested against the inside of his wrist.

She tightened her fingers and began to speak —

The room tore away from her.

---

Pain opened under her breastbone.

It spread before she could pull away from it, through her ribs and down her spine, as if every part of her had been caught in the same invisible grip. The deck was still under her. Her body seemed to be dragged across a distance too vast to survive.

Her hands stopped belonging to her.

The sea vanished under a white pressure that held no light. Pain drove through the place where his hand had been and spread behind her eyes until she felt it in her teeth. It had no direction she could fight. It only pulled.

And her mana moved.

All her life it had filled her with nowhere to go. It pressed under her ribs and behind her eyes and beneath her skin, vast and patient and waiting for a direction nobody had ever been able to give it. It had never once gone where she wanted it. Not in the palace. Not in the aqueduct. Not anywhere.

Now, for one heartbeat, it went.

Not outward in every direction at once, the way it had gone into the garden and the flowers and the fire. It went one way. It poured out of her like a tide finding the only channel to the sea, towards something far past the bow, past the black water, somewhere inland in the dark. As though, at the other end of that impossible distance, someone had reached out and taken hold of it.

For one heartbeat, it knew exactly where it was going.

Then it was gone. The channel closed. Her mana was a sea again, with no shore anywhere.

She hit the deck on one hip.

Tears reached her mouth before she knew she was crying.

"Seralune!"

Nereth's voice cracked on the name. The forward sail banged full at the same moment and swallowed the cry before it could reach the stern.

Nereth caught her by the shoulders, then let go of the bad one at once when the pain forced a sound out of her.

"What's happening?" All the dryness had gone out of her. "Tell me what to do. Seralune, are you hurt?"

The bedroom was still behind Seralune's eyes. The human face. His palm. The sentence she'd lost.

Nereth tried to get in front of her and nearly fell doing it. One hand struck the deck. The other found Seralune's wrist.

Then Nereth flinched.

It went through her whole body, hard, as if something had struck her across the face. Her hand left Seralune's wrist and flew up to the scars and pressed flat over them. Through the blur of her own tears, Seralune thought she saw the dark lines move under Nereth's fingers. One slow turn beneath the skin, like something rolling over in its sleep.

She couldn't be sure. She couldn't be sure of anything.

Nereth's hand stayed where it was. With the other, she found Seralune's wrist again.

"Look at me," she said. Her voice shook. "Please. I don't know what to do."

Seralune tried to answer. The pain pulled the sound apart before it reached her mouth.

*Count.*

The word came from so far back it might have belonged to someone else. A dark room. Her brother's voice. *Slowly. One breath first.*

One.

The deck came back first. Hard boards under her hip, one seam pressing into her palm.

Two.

Salt on her lips. Canvas straining overhead.

Three.

Water opening and closing along the hull. Nereth's fingers on her wrist.

"I'm here," Nereth said. The words shook, but she said them again. "You're on the ship. I'm here."

The distance inside her began to narrow. The pain was still there, deep enough to turn her stomach, but it had stopped pulling every part of her in a different direction.

Seralune closed her hand round Nereth's.

"Stay," she managed.

"Good. You can hear me." Nereth held on. "I'm staying. Should I call Nyana?"

Seralune waited for the deck to finish another slow roll.

"Not yet. If it happens again, call her."

"You don't know what happened."

"I know. But it's passing. And you can't cross the deck on your own."

Nereth looked towards the forward hatch, measuring the distance against her legs. She stayed.

Seralune drew her knees up. Tears kept leaving her eyes without her permission. She wiped them with the back of her wrist and found more behind them.

Nereth shifted until their shoulders touched. Her other hand was still pressed against her face.

"What happened to you?" Nereth asked.

Dark water moved past the rail.

"I was in my room."

Nereth waited for the rest. She didn't fill it in for her.

"The room I had before I woke," Seralune said. "The curtain by the window. The loose latch. I knew where everything was."

"Was someone there?"

"A young man came to the door."

Nereth's fingers turned under hers until they could hold properly. "Who was he?"

"I don't know."

She reached for a name and found only the shape of his hand against her cheek.

"He was human. I could see one ear under his hair. It was rounded. He stood outside my room like he'd done it a hundred times."

"There hasn't been a human inside Natharul's royal castle for as long as I've been alive," Nereth said. "Not one who could walk into your rooms as if he belonged there."

"He *did* belong there."

She'd said it before deciding whether she believed it.

Nereth didn't argue. "Did he say anything?"

"I told him he was late. He said he knew." She could still hear the smile he'd been trying to hide. "Then I took his hand."

"Why?"

"Because seeing him made me happy. I wanted him close."

Seralune touched her cheek. There was no warmth left there now, but she knew the exact breadth of his palm, and the rough place under his first finger.

"I knew his hand, Nereth. I knew he'd move the hair off my face before he did it. I knew I'd been waiting for him."

Nereth looked at Seralune's empty palm. "But you can't remember his name."

"I can't remember *him*. At all."

"Outside what you just saw?"

Seralune shook her head.

"I remember that room. I remember the south garden under it, and the corridor outside. I remember my mother complaining about the evening bells, and Thaer leaning in my doorway when he wanted to get out of whatever Father had asked him to do." The last part came out smaller. "But he isn't in any of it."

The ship rolled. Their joined hands moved on the blanket between them.

"Could it have been a dream?" Nereth asked, though the doubt had gone out of the question before she finished it.

"It wasn't."

"How can you be sure?"

"Because I miss him."

She hadn't meant to admit that much. Now she couldn't take it back.

Nereth spoke more slowly. "You think the memory's from before you were gone?"

"The room was exactly how I remember it. It has to be from before."

Neither of them said the number.

Even the longest human life would have ended in a fraction of the years Seralune had lost. She didn't know whether he'd died soon after the moment she'd seen, or lived peacefully for decades before anyone now alive was born.

She only knew the memory had found her tonight.

"Something happened to him," she said. "To both of us. And I can't reach it."

"Perhaps your mother knew him. If he came to your rooms that often…"

"She might have." Seralune pressed her fingertips to the place on her cheek. "She must have known something."

"All right," Nereth said. "What do you want to do?"

Seralune looked at the horizon.

Her mother had left an order that had outlasted ten centuries. Cyrandor had paid in blood to put its first direction into Seralune's hands.

"We find my mother's trail. If she left Natharul after I disappeared, she might know what happened round me. She might know why nobody would answer me when I asked what came before."

"And if she knows him?"

Seralune's fingers closed round the queen's signet under her shirt.

"Then I ask her his name."

Nereth leaned back against the barrels. The exhaustion had come back into her face, but she kept her hand in Seralune's.

"Favale first," she said.

"Favale first."

The forward sailors finished with the line and moved amidships. Neither of them looked behind the lashed barrels. At the stern, the watch lantern stayed a small yellow point beside Neldor.

Seralune and Nereth stayed where they were.

Somewhere past the dark water lay Favale, and the first surviving piece of her mother's trail. If any part of her missing life had lasted, perhaps his name had too.

Beside her, Nereth had finally taken her hand down from her face. She'd tucked it under the blanket, out of sight, and she didn't mention it. Seralune didn't ask.

She'd felt her mana go somewhere tonight. Once. She didn't know where.

She kept watching for land anyway.
