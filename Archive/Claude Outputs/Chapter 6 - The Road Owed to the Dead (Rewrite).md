# The Road Owed to the Dead

"So," the stranger said over the river's roar. "You're the one they're hunting."

Wena's growl deepened.

Alaric put a hand on the wet fur at her neck. She had planted herself in front of him with her legs spread and her head low, river water still dripping from her belly. The growl was too tired to be much of a threat. It shook almost as badly as the rest of her.

The stranger looked past them at the river. Torchlight moved between the trees on the far bank, and another flame had appeared further downstream, its reflection opening and closing on the black water.

"Keep her quiet."

Alaric's fingers tightened under Wena's collar. "Who are you?"

The man's eyes came back to him, hard. "I said keep her quiet. The rest can wait until those torches are further away."

He pushed off the alder. His weather-dark cloak shifted, showing worn leather underneath and a short blade at his hip. A pale scar ran down the left side of his face from cheekbone to jaw.

He tilted his head, listening.

Alaric heard only the river. Then, beneath it, hooves pressing into wet ground upstream. Closer than the torches. Already on this side.

Wena heard them too, and her growl broke off into a breath.

The stranger looked down at the mud around Alaric's feet. Every step he'd taken from the river had left a deep dark print among the roots.

"They'll follow those straight to you." He backed into the trees. "Come with me or stay. I'm leaving."

And he turned and went.

Alaric didn't follow. The man had a weapon. He'd watched the two of them crawl out of the river without lifting a finger. Nothing in his face suggested Alaric's life was worth anything to him beyond whatever use he'd spotted in it.

A layered voice drifted through the forest. Wena pressed back against his legs.

A few paces ahead, the stranger stopped and looked over his shoulder, the scarred side of his face tight with impatience.

"If you're coming, move."

Another voice answered the first, close enough that Alaric heard tack shifting under it.

He followed.

The man crossed the slope without disturbing more than the lowest branches. Alaric hit nearly all of them. Wet leaves slapped his face, roots caught his shoes, and the pain under his ribs flared every time he twisted to stay upright. Wena kept close now. Once she turned back towards the river, and Alaric touched her neck, and she came on.

The ground rose around a shelf of pale stone. The stranger slipped behind it and vanished.

There was a crack there, between the limestone and the earth, barely wide enough for a man's shoulders. Darkness inside.

"In," the man whispered.

Behind them, torchlight was moving through the trees they'd crossed. Alaric pushed Wena into the gap and went in after her.

It opened out after a few steps into a low hollow, the ceiling close enough to brush his wet hair. It smelled of old smoke, leather and damp earth. Against one wall lay an oilskin bundle, a dented cup and a little stack of split wood. Someone used this place.

The stranger dragged a loose branch across the entrance and crouched to rub dry soil over the wet prints Alaric had tracked in.

"Is this yours?" Alaric whispered.

"Somewhere I go when armed elves come too close to my home." He pointed to the back of the hollow. "Behind the stone. Take the dog."

Alaric squeezed in behind a shoulder of rock. Wena fought him until he sat down beside her, then pressed against his chest, her cold soaking through his clothes.

Outside, a horse let out a long breath. Leather creaked. A boot touched earth.

The stranger stepped into a narrow fissure beside the entrance, and his cloak became one more fold of dark.

One of the layered voices spoke just outside the cave. Another answered from further off, and that one moved away with the sound of horses.

One rider had stayed.

Light crept in under the branch. A gloved hand pushed it aside, and the scout ducked in — torch in one hand, narrow sword in the other, pale hair bound against the back of his armour, two pointed ears rising through it.

The torchlight found the floor. Found the dry soil brushed across it.

Stopped.

Wena drew breath to bark.

Alaric locked both arms around her, one hand closing gently over her muzzle, the other clamped across her chest. She fought him instantly. Her paws hammered his ribs, and the pain nearly broke his grip.

"Please," he breathed into her fur.

The scout took another step. His torch passed the fissure.

The stranger came out behind him.

A thin cord went around the scout's throat and snapped tight. His head jerked back. Before his sword could rise, a knee drove into the back of his leg and pulled him down against the stranger's chest. His mouth opened, and nothing came out of it.

The short blade went in under his raised arm, between two fitted plates. Once. Deep.

Dust lifted off the floor. Air rushed towards the dying scout in one sharp gasp — the torch flame bent sideways, grit stung Alaric's cheek — and then let go.

The stranger held the body until it stopped moving. He lowered it without letting the armour touch stone, caught the falling torch and ground its burning end into the wet earth.

Dark again.

Wena shook in Alaric's arms, her growl trapped behind his fingers.

The stranger listened for a few breaths, then crawled out. Alaric heard a blade slide through leather, a snort, a hard slap — and hooves bolting downstream. A distant rider called after the loose horse. Another answered, and the search went with it.

The man came back and wiped his blade on the dead scout's cloak.

"They'll chase the horse for a while. Then they'll come back looking for him." He nodded at the body. "We're gone before then."

Alaric let go of Wena's muzzle. She turned in his arms and pushed her nose under his jaw, and he felt her heart hammering through both of them.

The dead elf lay a few paces away, eyes open on the low ceiling. Alaric wondered whether someone was waiting for that rider to come home. Whether another voice would call his name tonight and get nothing back.

Gerolt had killed one of them. This man had killed another.

Both of them were dead because Alaric was alive.

The stranger crouched in front of him.

"Show me what you keep touching."

Alaric's hand went to his pocket before he could stop it. "I don't know what you mean."

It sounded weak even to him.

"You held it through the river. Checked it when you climbed out. Covered it when you saw me." The man glanced at the entrance. "If it belongs to the elves, I need to know before I decide whether you're worth the trouble."

"It doesn't."

"Then show me."

This man had killed the scout before he could make a sound. Refusing him might be brave. It might also be pointless.

Alaric drew out the token but kept it in his hand, opening his fingers only far enough for the split shield to catch the faint light.

The man went still.

"Where did you get that?"

Too quickly.

Alaric closed his hand. "Gerolt gave it to me."

For the first time, the calculation left the stranger's face, and nothing replaced it.

"Gerolt?"

"You know him."

"Where is he?"

Alaric looked at the entrance. The orange glow beyond the river didn't reach through the trees anymore, but he could still see the cabin opening up around the flames every time he shut his eyes.

"He stayed behind."

The man waited.

"There were two riders. He killed one. The other put a stone through his side. More came after she got away."

"And he gave you the shield."

"He told me to cross the river. To find an inn near Kelmend's southern gate." Alaric swallowed. "To ask for Marta."

The hollow went quiet.

"And what did Gerolt call you?"

"Alaric."

Wena lowered her head onto his knee.

"He could still have got away," Alaric said.

"No."

There was no cruelty in it. That made it worse.

"You didn't see him."

"A stone under the ribs. Natharul riders in the field. A burning house." The man looked at Wena. "The dog knows."

Wena didn't lift her head.

The stranger sat back on his heels. "One dead Natharul would've been enough."

"Enough for what?"

"For them to burn every roof that ever sheltered him. Two dead will make them thorough."

Alaric looked at the body. "You killed him."

"Because he came into my hole with a sword."

"Won't they punish people for that too?"

"Only if they find out who did it."

He said it too easily. Then he stood and crossed to the oilskin bundle.

"If the rider who escaped saw you, the Mydean guards will have your description by sunrise."

"They're human."

"They're afraid of Natharul." He untied the bundle. "If handing you over stops the elves calling this an act of war, that's what they'll do."

Alaric looked down at the split shield. "Do you know Marta?"

The man's hands paused on the knot. "I know the inn."

"Can you take me there?"

"I can get you as far as her door."

He went back to the bundle and offered nothing else. Alaric understood the silence.

"What do you want?"

The stranger's eyes settled on the fist around Gerolt's token.

"I need to ask Marta for something. Turning up with Gerolt's token might make her listen."

"What do you need from her?"

"That's between Marta and me." He pulled out a folded coat. "Your choice is whether you want me to get you there."

He tossed it. It hit Alaric's chest and slid into his lap.

"I take you to the inn. No further." He held Alaric's eyes until the limit was clear. "If the riders find us, I take whatever road keeps me alive. If you stop walking, I won't carry you."

Then he looked at Wena.

"The dog doesn't come."

Alaric's fingers sank into the coat. "No."

"She leaves tracks. She barks. She eats. Poor qualities in cargo."

"Gerolt told me to take her."

"Gerolt's dead."

It went in clean. Alaric felt it a moment later.

Wena stayed pressed against his knee, waiting, with no idea what he was being asked to choose.

He was still afraid. The man could refuse. The riders could come back. Kelmend might be further than he could survive on his own. None of that changed what Gerolt had put into his hands.

"Then I can't go with you."

For a few breaths, only water moved somewhere under the stone.

The stranger looked at Wena again — not with any affection, the way a man looks at a sum he has to redo.

"Fine. Bring her." He stood and cinched the bundle shut. "But you're cargo, not company. Don't expect me to die keeping either of you alive."

Alaric didn't thank him. He pulled the coat around his shoulders.

It was dry.

"Mydean messenger's coat," the man said. "Keep the collar up."

Warmth settled over his wet clothes, and his whole body started shaking harder underneath it. The high collar reached almost to his jaw. One side crossed over the other and fastened with hidden clasps, and a short second layer fell from one shoulder like a cut-down cloak. It was a little broad on him, but it covered the strange fabric underneath.

The stranger caught one of Alaric's own sleeves before it disappeared and ran his thumb over the stitching. No branch had torn it. No rock in the river had opened a seam. Under the mud and Gerolt's blood, it was perfectly whole.

He let it go. "Where did you get this?"

"I don't know."

The man studied his face, and whatever he saw there stopped the next question. He tore two strips from a cloth in the bundle and dropped them beside Alaric.

"Wrap your hands. You're bleeding on my floor."

Alaric's fingers were stiff with cold, and the first knot slipped twice before it held. By the time he'd finished, the stranger had broken up the dead scout's torch and buried the pieces under loose stone. He dragged the body into the fissure where he'd waited and piled rock across it. The blood on the floor stayed. The corpse didn't show from the entrance.

He lifted the branch.

"Move."

---

They didn't use the road.

The man led them through drainage ditches, along low walls, across fields already stripped for harvest. Where the forest thickened, he found the places the roots broke the ground least. Where it opened, he waited under cover and watched before crossing. He never explained any of it.

Alaric tried to remember every turn. River behind them. A split oak to the north. A stone wall curving west. After that, exhaustion folded one path over another until every stand of trees looked like the last. His wet shoes rubbed the skin off his heels.

He tripped on a low wall and fell across it. Stone drove into his bruised ribs and emptied his lungs, and for a few heartbeats he could only hang there and wait for the dark to pull back from the edges of his sight.

Ahead, the stranger stopped. He didn't come back.

"Get up," he said without turning. "The riders are still behind us."

Alaric planted both wrapped hands on the stone. His arms shook once, then held. He stood.

Wena stayed with him until he moved.

Later, she stopped dead and turned south. The stranger crouched at once. Hoofbeats arrived a few breaths afterwards, and he changed direction before the first torch showed between the trees.

"Keep her close," he said. "She heard them before I did."

It was the nearest he'd come to approving of her.

Some time before dawn, a low roof appeared between the trees. No light behind the shutters. A narrow chimney against the greying sky, split logs stacked under one wall. The stranger looked at it once. Then torchlight moved along the distant road, and he turned his back on it and led them into another field.

"Is that where you live?" Alaric asked.

"Near enough that I won't lead the elves to it."

He didn't look back again.

"What's your name?"

"You don't need my name to get to Kelmend."

"You know mine."

"I know what Gerolt called you."

The difference unsettled him. "You knew him."

The man stepped over a fallen branch. "Enough to know he didn't give that shield away lightly."

"He saved me."

The stranger's stride shortened by half a step. "I know."

"Do you think he was a fool?"

"Yes."

No hesitation at all. Alaric stopped walking.

The man went two more steps before he noticed, then turned. "Gerolt decided you were worth staying for. That doesn't mean staying was wise."

Anger came up in Alaric before he knew what to do with it. "Then why are you helping me?"

"Because getting you to Marta isn't the same as dying for you." His eyes flicked to the hidden shape of the token. "And because I need something from her."

He turned away.

Alaric looked down at Wena. She was staring east, where the dark had started to thin.

Gerolt had made a foolish decision. The stranger hadn't said it was worthless.

Alaric followed.

---

Kelmend appeared under the last of the night.

It sat on a low rise past the fields, walled in dark stone. Two square towers guarded the southern gate, lamps burning above it, carts already queuing on the road. Behind the wall, roofs climbed in uneven rows, the first chimneys smoking. Somewhere inside, a hammer struck metal in a slow working rhythm.

Alaric saw the people first. Then the horses among them.

The stranger pulled him down behind a bank of thorn and dead grass. Wena crawled in between them without being told.

Three Natharul riders stood before the gate. The lamps caught their pale hair. Dark armour set them apart from the Mydean guards under the arch, though not by much — both sides carried weapons. The elves stood as if the ground already belonged to them.

"You've crossed a sovereign border under arms," the Mydean captain said.

The nearest rider didn't lower his head. "One of ours was murdered on that sovereign soil. Another was mutilated."

The captain's jaw tightened. "By a farmer, according to your own account."

"Then find the boy the farmer died protecting. Young. Human. Dark hair. Unfamiliar black clothing. He travels with a large farm dog and crossed the river south of the bridge."

Alaric touched the high collar. The coat hid the cut of his clothes. It couldn't hide his hair, or Wena.

"Your messenger demanded we close our southern gate over one boy."

"One scout is dead. Another carries the farmer's blade across her face." The words stayed controlled. Something under them didn't. The rider stepped closer. "If he gets past your wall, Kelmend has chosen to shelter those responsible. Decide whether that's the report you want carried home."

The captain glanced at the soldiers behind him. Then he turned.

"Search every cart. No dog comes in without being checked. Two men to the western postern, and double the wall patrol."

The orders went out at once. Human soldiers dragged the first driver off his seat. Sacks were opened at spear point. A woman holding a sleeping child was ordered down from her wagon so a guard could search under it.

"They're helping them," Alaric whispered.

"The captain's trying to keep Natharul on the outside of his wall."

"By handing me over."

"If he has to choose between one stranger and everyone in Kelmend, he'll give them the stranger." No satisfaction in it. No apology, either.

Alaric looked at the gate Gerolt had sent him to. Crossing the river hadn't brought him to allies. It had only changed which people would give him up.

The stranger backed out from under the thorn. "We can't use the gate."

"Then how do we get in?"

"There's a tannery runoff under the southern wall. If the morning sluice hasn't opened yet, we crawl through it."

They followed the wall south until the road fell out of sight. The stones there were stained dark by years of runoff, and the smell reached Alaric before he saw where it came from — rotting bark, old urine, and something sour enough to close his throat. An abandoned tannery leaned against the outer wall, half its roof caved in, empty drying frames standing black and thin behind it.

The stranger went in through a gap where boards had been pulled from the wall. Inside, shallow pits crossed the floor, rainwater gone green in them. Wena sniffed the nearest one and recoiled.

"Not drinking water," the man said.

At the back, a brick channel ran down under the town wall, its mouth barred with iron and clogged with mud, hair and strips of ruined hide. He pushed both hands into the filth and found a pin hidden beside the lowest bar.

It didn't move.

"You knew this was here," Alaric said.

"I helped open it. Years ago."

"For Marta?"

The man drew the short blade from his hip. "Hold the grate."

Fire opened across his fingers.

No tinder. No spark struck from stone. One moment his hand was empty, and the next an orange flame was turning between his knuckles as if it had come up out of his skin.

Alaric stepped back. The cabin roof burst open in his head again, flame rolling along the beams, Gerolt somewhere underneath it.

The stranger glanced at his face and said nothing.

He passed his hand along the blade, and the fire followed, narrowing to its edge without touching the leather grip.

Fire『Affinity』. The word surfaced with the same unwanted certainty as『Magic』had on the field.

He pressed the heated edge into the black pitch around the pin. A thin, bitter line of smoke rose. The pitch softened. He wrapped part of his cloak around the metal and twisted, and the rust broke with a quiet crack. The flame vanished.

He hauled the lowest section of the grate inward. The gap was barely as wide as Wena's shoulders.

"She goes first."

Wena put her nose to the channel, smelled what waited inside, and pulled back.

Alaric crouched beside her and laid his hand between her eyes.

"I'm coming too."

She went in, one careful step at a time, until she disappeared under the wall. Alaric followed on his stomach. The new coat snagged on the iron until the stranger freed its hem.

"Keep your elbows in."

Cold filth closed around his forearms. The channel narrowed straight away, brick pressing both shoulders, the ceiling so low he couldn't lift his head. Ahead, Wena's paws splashed in the shallow flow. Behind him, the stranger pulled the grate back into place without the pin.

"They'll see that," Alaric whispered.

"Only if they search the runoff. We'll be through first."

A bell rang above the wall.

The passage stripped distance of any meaning. Brick. Water. Wena's hind legs. The man's breathing behind him. His wrapped palms darkened and loosened until the cloth dragged along the floor. At the first bend, he became certain he wouldn't fit.

"I'm stuck."

"Turn your left shoulder."

He tried. Pain sliced under his ribs. "I can't."

The dryness went out of the man's voice. "You can. Left shoulder down, before the sluice gets here."

The second bell struck.

Something moved behind them. Not footsteps.

Water.

It came into the lower channel with a distant rush, gathering noise as it came.

Alaric forced his left shoulder down. Brick scraped along the coat. His chest was squeezed until there was no breath left in it — and then the corner let him go, and he dragged himself forward.

The first surge hit his boots. Dirty water climbed his legs and forced its way through every gap around his body. Ahead, Wena barked once, the sound huge in the brick.

"Keep moving."

He did. Not fast. Not well. One elbow. One knee. One breath wherever the ceiling allowed it. The water rose to his waist, shoved at the coat, found every bruise underneath. His hands slipped, and the current carried him half an arm's length before his knee caught the side of the channel.

Then pale light touched the bricks ahead.

He reached it on shaking arms and found a broken grille opening into a stone settling chamber. Wena stood beyond it, soaked all over again and whining. He dragged himself through. The drop was further than he expected, and his knees hit stone, and he rolled aside before the stranger came out behind him.

Water poured past them into a deep trench cut through the middle of the room.

Above, footsteps crossed wooden boards.

Wena's head came up. The man caught her collar before she could move.

There was a trapdoor in the ceiling, light leaking round its edges. Someone stopped right on top of it. Alaric held his breath.

A man coughed, spat through a gap in the boards, and walked on.

The stranger let go of Wena and looked up at the trapdoor. Whatever relief getting into Kelmend should have brought him never reached his face.

---

They came up into the town through a cellar that smelled worse than the channel.

He waited until the voices had moved away from the tannery yard, then lifted the trapdoor and climbed out into the morning. Alaric boosted Wena up and followed.

Kelmend was already at work around them. Carts groaned through narrow service lanes. Smoke from the first ovens mixed with the tannery stink and the damp dawn air. Labourers carried baskets between low stone buildings without looking closely at anyone who kept moving.

Alaric kept his collar up.

Twice the stranger pulled him into an alley before armed men crossed the street ahead. Once, a labourer opened his mouth at the sight of a huge wet dog climbing out of a tannery yard, and the stranger pressed a copper coin into his hand. The man looked at the coin. Then at the three of them, filthy to the eyes. He shut his mouth.

Nobody offered Alaric safety. Nobody asked if he was hurt. The town only let him through when it failed to notice him, or was paid not to care.

Still, every street carried them further from the gate.

At last the stranger turned into a narrow lane between two inns. He walked past the first without a glance. The second stood back from the street behind a small stable yard — stone below, dark timber above, a weathered sign over the door with its painted cup almost washed away by rain.

No light under the door.

The stranger stopped at the edge of the yard.

Alaric waited for him to go on. He didn't. The man who had stepped out of the dark behind an armed elf would not cross the last few paces to that door.

"You promised to bring me here."

"And here you are."

He didn't move towards the entrance.

"Knock."

"Why?"

His eyes stayed on the closed door.

"Because she won't open it for me."
