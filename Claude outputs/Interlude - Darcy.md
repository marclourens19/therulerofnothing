# Interlude: Darcy

The horns had stopped an hour before dawn.

Darcy had used the hour.

She sat at the desk with the unfinished map under her hands and built the night again, piece by piece, the way she'd build a river crossing for a man who wanted it done by noon. The service-yard horn. Armour that hadn't fitted the man wearing it. Blood on the boy's wrappings, not all of it his. Keys that had come off the gaoler's belt. The inspection grate at the far end of the lower row.

And one cell door, locked, with a woman behind it who had never left her desk.

Every piece was true. That was why it would hold. She only had to decide which way each one pointed.

Her lamp had burned down to a blue bead. The ash of the drawing still lay curled in the bottom of its cage. She'd thought about tipping it out and hadn't. Ash in a lamp was nothing. Ash on the floor was a question.

Boots came down the stair at first light. Only one pair walked without hurrying.

Captain Gilmot stopped outside her door and looked at the lock before he looked at her. He tried it. It held. The candle in the sconce beside him leaned towards his hand and stayed there.

"Good morning, Darcy."

"Captain."

"We had visitors." He folded his hands behind his back. His coat was buttoned to the collar and the gold on it was bright, as if he'd slept well. Perhaps he had. "One man dead in the kitchen. Another in the service passage with a knife under his ribs. A guard tied up in a storeroom without his armour. My gaoler senseless at his own gate, and his keys gone. The grate at the end of this row locked again behind them." He tilted his head. "Help me understand them."

It was how he'd always begun. Help me understand the eastern bank. Help me understand why the bridge will fail. As if they were two scholars bent over the same problem, and the collar had never been heated at all.

"Two men," she said. "Possibly three. They came in through the service yard and took the guard's armour so they could cross the command level. The man in the service passage must have known the face under the helmet."

"Go on."

"They came down here for the grate." She didn't look towards the end of the row. "It's the only way out of this fort that doesn't pass a gate."

"And how did they know about a grate that isn't on my plans?"

She'd expected that question first. He'd saved it.

"Because the men who built this fort left it off theirs, and I corrected what I could see from this desk." She let some of her tiredness into it. She didn't have to pretend very hard. "The sewer men who clear it every spring know it's there. So does anyone who's ever bought one of them a drink."

Gilmot considered that. The candle flame bent lower.

"You'll add it to the plans."

"Yes, Captain."

She had kept that throat of stone off every sheet she'd ever drawn of this fort. She gave it up in two words, and he never knew there had been anything to give.

"The prisoners," she said, because a strategist would ask. "Did they open any doors?"

"Not one. They walked past every cell on this row." He sounded almost disappointed in them. "Your neighbours say they never stopped."

Darcy kept her hand flat on the map.

The woman with the sleeping child had watched them come. So had the old man who broke his bread into portions for people he couldn't see. Every one of them had seen Silas stand at this door with the keys in his hand.

Not one of them had said so.

---

Lighter steps followed Gilmot's down the stair. The Natharul officer came along the row as if the fort were a room it had been asked to wait in. Gold lines moved over the dark armour. Grey light had reached the high grate by now, and in it the elf's face was pale, fine-boned and impossible to read.

"Captain." Each word came out cut clean from the next. "One of our hunters reports a human boy leaving the drain by the eastern wall before dawn. Bloodied hands. He ran into the settlement outside it." A pause. "Your Faint quarter."

Gilmot didn't turn. "Then the provision stands."

The candle flame climbed his fingers.

Darcy had known this was coming from the moment she told Alaric where the water came out. She'd had the whole of the dark to prepare for it. Her heart went anyway, hard and fast, and she made her voice go the other way.

"The drain empties where the fort's water goes," she said. "They built the quarter there because nobody else wanted the ground. Anyone coming out of that throat would run through it. There's nowhere else to run."

"That would be true," Gilmot said, "of a quarter that helped him."

"A quarter that helped him wouldn't have let your hunters see him." She turned the map slightly, as if the answer lay somewhere on it. "And the provision is for my removal. I'm still here."

Overhead, the Wind core pulsed. Blue-green light ran along the copper line above her door and was gone.

"Burn it, and you burn the hands that charge that core," she said. "Your ship leaves this morning with half a charge. The next one waits until the roads dry, and the frontier waits with it."

She hadn't asked him for anything. She'd only shown him the ground. He had always liked that best.

Gilmot smiled, the small way he did when a pupil surprised him.

"Precisely argued. The provision is for your removal, and you haven't been removed." He lowered his hand, and the flame settled. "The quarter stays as it is. It can go in the record as a burglary."

The elf hadn't moved. Its gaze went past Darcy to the lamp on her desk, where the drawing's ash lay grey in the bottom of the cage. It stayed there the length of one breath. Then it came back to her hand, to the ink on her forefinger, and to the tremor she hadn't quite managed to hide.

The elf said nothing at all.

"There is still the matter of the gate," Gilmot said.

Darcy's hand went still.

"A man slept at his post while thieves took his keys." He said it gently, the way he said everything. "Negligence needs correcting. What would you recommend?"

The old gaoler. Tobin. Sixty if he was a day, with knees that had given up years ago. He kept his ledger open on his stomach because he fell asleep over it every night and liked to pretend he didn't. The winter her fever broke, he'd let her keep a candle burning past curfew so she wouldn't wake in the dark. He'd never told anyone. She had never thanked him.

If she told Gilmot the truth, Tobin would be forgiven, and the quarter would burn before noon.

"Dismissal," she said.

"You were always merciful." He seemed pleased by it. "But a dismissed man teaches nobody. He slept while the fort was robbed. He can stay awake in the harness until we reach the frontier."

He nodded to the guard at the foot of the stair, and the guard went up it.

Darcy looked down at the map. The river on it had blurred. She blinked until it came back.

"We sail within the hour," Gilmot said. "I'll be beside you the whole way."

---

They brought her up through the fort in iron cuffs lined with felt, so they wouldn't mark her wrists. That had been Gilmot's idea. He'd never liked her hands damaged.

The yard was full of soldiers trying not to look at her. The airship hung above it on four chains, its hull dark with oil, its belly glowing blue-green where the core sat. A gangway ran up to it from the wall. Halfway along, the wind off the plain found her and pulled her hair loose, and for three steps she was only cold. It was almost a relief.

Through an open hatch below the deck, she saw the harness benches. Six of them, with copper threads running from the leather straps into the core's housing. Tobin sat on the end bench in his shirtsleeves with the rings already clamped round his wrists. He was looking at his hands as if he didn't know whose they were.

He didn't look up. She was grateful, and ashamed of being grateful.

The chains came free one at a time.

**KRRRNNNK.**

The deck tilted, steadied, and rose.

Gilmot stood at her shoulder by the rail. He didn't touch her. He'd never needed to.

Kelmend fell away beneath them.

She found the southern gate first, and the crooked roofline beside it that was Marta's inn. A thread of smoke rose from its chimney. Marta would be at the ovens by now, pretending not to watch the sky. Then came the walls, the fort, and the long grey line of the eastern wall.

Outside it lay the Faint quarter.

Darcy counted the smoke.

Eleven chimneys. Twelve. Thin grey threads going straight up in the still air, the way smoke rises from bread ovens and cookfires. No black. No roofs open to the sky. No fire companies in the lanes. A woman was pegging washing between two sheds, and she didn't look up at the ship at all.

Darcy's cuffed hands stopped shaking on the rail.

Not one house.

Somewhere beyond those roofs, a boy who couldn't remember his own life was walking towards Favale with her words in his head. He'd woken in a field of thousands of dead, the only one still breathing, and then he'd walked into a fort full of soldiers for a woman he'd never met. He'd carried Gerolt's death all that way, and he'd still stood between Silas and her door because she'd said no. He was carrying the shape of a war core in his memory because his hands had been too bloody to hold the paper.

She didn't know what he was. Nobody did. She'd heard the way he talked about those machines, the metal without rivets and the burned ground, as though his eyes had been there and the rest of him hadn't. It was a problem with no map, and she'd spent her whole life with nothing but maps.

She wanted, badly, to know the answer.

*Water always finds its way to the ocean.*

She shaped the words without a sound, facing into the wind so Gilmot wouldn't see her mouth move.

Beside her, he lifted a hand to shelter the candle he'd brought up from below. The flame leaned into his palm the way it always did, patient and bright, and the ship turned its nose towards the frontier.

Darcy watched Kelmend until the smoke was too small to count.

Then she kept watching the place where it had been.
