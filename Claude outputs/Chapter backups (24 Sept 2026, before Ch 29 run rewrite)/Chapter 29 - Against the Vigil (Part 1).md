# Against the Vigil (Part 1)

She was running.

The ground was mud to the ankle, and the mud was red. It sucked at her feet. Every step she pulled free cost her more than the last, and she took the next one anyway.

A man was on his knees in front of her with both hands pressed over his face. Blood was coming out between his fingers. She went round him.

Someone was screaming. Someone else was calling a name, over and over, the same name, in a voice that had stopped expecting an answer.

A soldier lay across her way with his helmet gone and his eyes open. She stepped over him. There was another beyond him, and another, and she stopped looking down.

The noise was everywhere. Steel and screaming, and under it a deep ringing, like a great bell struck and struck again, coming from somewhere she couldn't see through the smoke.

She was running towards something.

She couldn't see what. She only knew that it was ahead of her, and that she had to reach it, and that she was late —

The loft.

Seralune lay on her back among the sheets with her heart slamming and her hands knotted in the blanket. The sheets hung over her in the dark, pale and still. Somewhere below, water dripped into water.

She couldn't breathe.

She lay there and waited for her chest to remember how. Behind her eyes, the pain went on a heartbeat longer than the pictures had, a hot, bright ache, like a thumb pressed into each eye from the inside. Then it drained away.

*One breath first.*

She took it. It shook.

*One.* The sheet above her face. *Two.* The drip below. *Three.* Nereth's breathing beside her, slow and even, asleep.

The count went down into her and didn't reach the place the running had come from. It was like dropping a stone into a well and never hearing it land.

Nereth slept on her side with her back to Seralune and one arm curled under her head. Seralune's hand had been lying against her shoulder when she woke. Under it, through the thin stuff of Nereth's shift, the marks were warm.

They pricked once against her palm, like a sleeve catching on a nail. Then they went quiet.

Seralune took her hand away.

She lay in the dark with it held against her own chest, and she didn't let herself feel anything about any of it until it was light.

---

The coppers were cold.

Yesterday, Agna had been up before the light, raking the ash and banging the fire-doors. This morning there was no shouting. There was no light under the fire-doors. The laundry was grey and silent, and the sheets hung in it like washing left out in a house where someone had died.

Agna was sitting on the floor at the end of the coppers, in front of the little iron door.

She was still in yesterday's apron. She had her back against the brick and her hands in her lap, and she didn't look up when Seralune came down the ladder.

"Mistress Agna," said Seralune.

Agna didn't answer.

Nereth came down behind her. She stood at the foot of the ladder for a while, looking at the woman on the floor. Then she went to the first copper.

"Leave it," said Agna, without looking up. "Leave them cold. Let the whole quarter wear its sheets dirty."

"The Two Wells will want theirs," said Nereth.

"Let them want."

Nereth laid her right hand on the rim anyway, and didn't call anything yet.

"He'd have had the wood in by now," she said. "Wouldn't he? He'd have been at that door with his arms full before you were up."

Agna turned her head.

"Every morning," she said. "Every morning since he could carry a stick. I never once had to ask him."

"Then I'll do it for him," said Nereth. "Until he's back."

She called her fire.

It came orange, with the white at its heart. Seralune felt it through the floor.

Nereth moved to the second copper. Then the third. By the time she'd finished, all three were ticking and muttering, and the first had begun to steam.

Agna got up.

She did it slowly, one hand on the brick, like a much older woman. She took her paddle down from its hook and stood looking at it.

"The Sun and Stair want theirs by noon," she said, to nobody. "And the Two Wells. And Widow Ashby's, double, for the Longest Light."

Then she went to work, and she didn't say anything else for the rest of the morning.

---

The baskets came all day.

Every hostel in the quarter was full to the rafters, and every pilgrim in every hostel had brought someone sick, and every sick person needed a clean sheet to be laid on the stair.

The baskets came in at the door faster than the coppers could take them. By midday there were sheets on every line and sheets on the table and sheets on the floor in wet grey heaps, waiting their turn.

"It's always like this," said Bett. "The last days. Mam says it's the only time of year she'd sell her soul for a fourth copper."

"Mam never said that," said Tilde.

"She did. To Maud."

"She said she'd sell *you* for a fourth copper."

Bett considered this with her arms in the rinsing tub up to the elbows. "That's worse," she decided.

Tilde snorted. It was nearly a laugh. Then she looked across at her mother's back, bent over the coppers, and it wasn't anything.

Nobody said Kit's name. Seralune noticed the shape it made in the room, as she'd noticed the watchman's hand close on the ladder. She noticed it, and went on wringing.

Maud came in at the middle of the morning with the Sun and Stair's basket, and all the news in the quarter along with it.

"Scrubbed to the top," she announced, before the basket had touched the table. "Every step. They finished at dawn, and the high priest came down and blessed the buckets, and a man fainted."

"Why'd he faint?" said Bett.

"Holiness," said Maud. "Or the sun. And they turned out the Library yesterday."

Seralune's hands went on wringing.

"The priest went down into those vaults with his seal and his lamp, first thing, and he was down there till the midday bell." Maud leaned on the basket. "Found nothing. Books, and a cat. And the Keeper stood at the top of her stair the whole time with her hands folded, like this." She folded them. "Like a statue. My cousin's boy was on the door, and he says she never moved once. Not once, in all that time."

"Found nothing," said Agna, from the coppers.

"Nothing. They're saying the old archivist's gone in the river now. Him and his three men and the dog. Tried to swim it in the dark and drowned, the lot of them." Maud sniffed. "Good riddance, says the watch. I say it's a waste of a dog."

"Maud," said Agna.

Maud looked round the room: at the grey coppers, and Tilde's face, and the little iron door at the end of the row. Her own face changed.

"Oh, love," she said to Agna, in quite a different voice. "Oh, love. I'd forgotten. I'm a fool."

"You are," said Agna. "Take your basket."

A woman came to the door at midday with nothing to sell and nothing to fetch. She was thin, with a pilgrim's sun token round her neck and a pilgrim's dust to the knee.

"Mistress," she said. "I've no sheet. For my husband, for the stair. They say you have to lay them on something clean or it's no good. I can pay. I can pay a little."

Agna studied her. Then she pulled a sheet off the nearest line, still damp, and folded it in four and put it into the woman's arms.

"Bring it back clean," she said.

The woman stared at the sheet. "What do I owe you?"

"Nothing. Bring it back." Agna was already at the coppers again. "And mind he's on the left of the stair, not the right. The right's in the sun all morning. He'll cook before she gets to him."

The woman went away holding the sheet against her chest with both arms.

"You'll never see that again," said Tilde.

"No," said Agna.

Seralune wrung out a pillowcase and laid it over the line and watched the water drip out of its corners onto the floor.

"You're very quiet today," said Bett.

"Am I?"

"You're very quiet every day. But today you're quieter." Bett tipped her head on one side. "Is it the coast? Are all coast people quiet?"

"Yes," said Seralune.

"Do you ever say anything but yes?" said Tilde.

Seralune looked at her. "Sometimes."

Tilde held her eyes a moment too long. Then she went back to her tub.

Bett wasn't put off. Bett was never put off for long.

"Is it true the sea's all salt?" she said. "All of it? Right to the bottom?"

"Yes," said Seralune.

"Kit says there's a giant's skull in the Library as big as a cart," said Bett. "A priest told him. He says they keep it in the cellars under a sheet, and nobody's allowed to look at it but the priests. Were there giants on the coast?"

"No," said Seralune.

"It'll be a fish," said Nereth, from the coppers.

Bett swung round. "A fish? As big as a cart?"

"Bigger. There are fish in the sea bigger than this laundry."

Bett's mouth fell open. Then it shut, and her face went very quiet.

"He'll be so cross he missed that," she said.

In the afternoon, when the first of the day's sheets came dry off the lines, Agna put Seralune to folding.

She did it without a word, the way she did everything that day: she dumped an armful on the table and returned to the fires.

Seralune took the first sheet by its corners, shook it out, brought the corners together, and folded it. Then again. Then again, corner to corner, edge to edge, until it lay on the table in a square so exact that she could have laid a ruler along it.

She reached for the next one.

"Who taught you that?"

Agna was standing at her elbow. Seralune hadn't heard her come.

"Taught me what?"

"That." Agna nodded at the square on the table. "Nobody folds like that. Not for a hostel. Not for the temple. You fold like somebody's watching you do it."

Seralune considered the sheet.

She didn't know who'd taught her. She'd never folded a sheet in her life before this laundry. Her hands had simply done it: the corners first, and then the edges, and nothing handed on until it was right.

*He took it off. And then he folded it, Sera. Corners and all.*

"My brother," she said.

Agna grunted. She picked the square up, turned it over, and put it down again.

"Well, he'd have made a laundress," she said, and went back to her coppers.

---

On the second day, they took the baskets out themselves.

There were too many for the hostels to fetch, and Agna couldn't spare Tilde, so it was Bett who led the way, with Seralune and Nereth behind her carrying a basket between them, and another balanced on top of it, and Bett with a bundle on her own head like a market woman.

Favale had filled up in the night.

Seralune hadn't thought it could hold any more people than it had held the day they came in. It had found room somewhere.

Pilgrims slept in doorways and under carts and along the tops of walls. Whole families sat in the lanes with their backs against the houses and their sick laid across their knees. Every step and sill and horse-trough had somebody on it.

"There's more every year," said Bett, over her shoulder. "Mam says one year the whole city will just fall in the river."

"Watch the step," said Nereth.

"I know the step. I've been over that step a thousand times." Bett went over it. "That's Old Hobb. He sells the saint's water."

Old Hobb had a cart full of little clay bottles stopped with wax, and a crowd round it three deep.

"Is it really hers?" said Seralune.

Bett gave her a look Kit might have given her. "It's out of the river. He fills them at the bottom of the Lily Steps where the women wash. But he says a prayer over it, so." She shrugged the bundle higher on her head. "Mam says a prayer's a prayer."

Old Hobb saw them looking. "The saint's own water!" he sang out over the heads. "Drawn at dawn and prayed over! Two for a copper, and a blessing thrown in! You, miss, with the scarf. Your friend looks as if she could do with a blessing."

"Keep walking," said Nereth.

"I am walking."

"Walk faster."

At the Two Wells, the keeper, a big red woman with a ladle in her apron pocket, counted the sheets twice and paid Bett in coppers. She gave Seralune and Nereth a long look over the top of them.

"You're Agna's new girls," she said. "The quiet one and the burned one."

"Yes," said Seralune.

"Well, you've a good place." The keeper tucked the sheets under her arm. "She's hard, is Agna, but she's never sold anybody. Tell her we're sorry about her boy."

"We will."

"And tell her if she wants a word put in with the stewards, my husband's brother knows the fat one."

Bett's head came up. "Does he?"

"He knows him to nod to." The keeper's face softened. "It's something, pet. It's something to try."

They went up through the western market, and the western market was a roar.

Lanterns were going up on every street. Men on ladders fixed them to poles and brackets and the corners of houses, and boys went up after them to wash the glass.

There were banners over every door: white, with a sun in gold thread, or in yellow paint where people couldn't afford thread. Children were running in a pack between the stalls, singing the vigil hymn at the tops of their voices and getting the words wrong.

"'The Light that *keeps* the faithful,'" Bett told them, as they went by. "Not *heaps*."

"The Light that heaps the faithful," sang the smallest, defiantly, and ran.

Nereth made a sound under her scarf.

"What?" said Seralune.

"Nothing," said Nereth. "Heaps."

From the top of the market street they could see the stair.

It went up the side of the hill from the square at its foot to the temple at its top, wider than any road, and it was covered in people on their knees.

There were hundreds of them. They had buckets and brushes, and they were scrubbing it white all over again, a step at a time, from the bottom up, singing as they went.

"Penitents," said Bett, with deep respect. "They've been at it since the new moon. When they get to the top, they start again at the bottom. You go on your knees the whole way, and every step you scrub, something you did is forgiven. Maud's husband did it once."

"What had he done?" said Nereth.

"Maud never said. He did it twice, though." Bett shifted the bundle. "When Kit comes home, I'm going to bring him up here to see her. Close. Not from the roof. He's never seen her close."

Neither of them answered that.

Seralune looked at the white stair and the people on their knees on it, and felt nothing about them at all. It was very restful. She could see everything clearly, the brushes and the buckets and the water running down from step to step, shining. She could see all of it, and none of it could get in.

They crossed the market.

Halfway across, between a stall selling sun tokens and a stall selling candles, the market went away.

She was running.

Not in mud this time. On grass, burned grass, black and stubbled, and the smoke was lower and thicker, and there were more of the dead. Men in armour she didn't know. Pale men, with long ears like her own, lying among them.

And other shapes, too big, too broad, horned, lying where they'd fallen in the same line as the men, as though they'd stood there together.

Something was walking over them.

It was silver. It was as tall as a house, and it walked on long jointed legs that bent the wrong way, and there wasn't a seam in it anywhere. It stepped over the dead the way a heron steps through reeds. With every step it rang, deep and dull, a bell struck from the inside.

She ran under it. Its shadow went over her and was gone.

She was running towards —

The basket hit the ground.

Clean sheets spilled across the cobbles, into the dust and the dung and the feet. A man trod on one and swore. Seralune was standing with her hands open and empty, and she didn't remember letting go.

"Sera?" Bett had turned round, with her bundle still on her head. Her eyes were round. "Sera, are you ill?"

The pain was going out from behind her eyes, slowly, a tide going down.

"No," said Seralune.

"You've gone white."

"It's the sun."

Beside her, Nereth had set down her end of the basket very carefully. Her hand had gone to her collarbone and was pressing there, hard, over the scarf. Her face had gone the colour of the sheets.

"Your friend's gone grey again," said Bett.

"It's the sun," said Nereth.

They picked the sheets up out of the dust together, the three of them, and folded the dirty ones to the inside so that nobody would see.

"Mam's going to kill us," said Bett.

"I'll tell her it was me," said Seralune.

"It was you."

"Then I'll tell her the truth."

Bett giggled, and then looked guilty for giggling, and then did it again. Nereth didn't laugh. She didn't take her eyes off Seralune for the rest of the way.

On the way back, with the empty basket between them and Bett running on ahead to catch up with a friend, Nereth said, "The stair."

"What about it?"

"Where on it. If we go." Nereth kept her eyes on the road. "Somewhere near the top, where she'll pass close. Not down at the bottom with the crowd."

"We're going."

"And if they pull back my scarf? On the stair, in front of all of them?"

It was the question Nereth had asked her at the gate, turned round. Seralune had had no answer to it then.

"Nobody will be looking at anyone but her," she said.

"You keep saying that."

"Because it's true."

"You keep saying that, too." Nereth shifted her grip on the basket. "Everything's true, with you, lately. It's all true and it doesn't matter to you. I'd rather you lied to me and cared."

"I do care," said Seralune.

It came out like the rest. Like the price of barley. She heard it, and saw Nereth hear it, and there was nothing she could do about either.

---

That night the practice lanterns burned for the last time.

Tomorrow was the eve, and after tonight, Bett said, the lanterns wouldn't be lit again until the Longest Light itself. Then they'd light every one in the city at once, and the temple, and the stair, and it would all burn till morning.

Seralune and Nereth sat on the roof with their backs to the low wall and watched the gold lines run away across the dark.

Kit's place was at the corner, where the wall was lowest. Neither of them sat in it.

"He'd have counted them," Nereth said, after a while. "The lanterns. Every one."

"And the watch," said Seralune. "Eight hundred and something. Longer if the fat one's on."

She heard herself say it, Kit's own words, in her own flat voice. They sat there between the two of them like a stone. Neither of them picked it up.

"You've gone somewhere again," said Nereth.

"I'm here."

"No, you're not."

Seralune turned her head. Nereth was looking at the lanterns, not at her. She'd wound her scarf up high tonight, higher than she needed to, right up under her jaw.

"On the pilgrim road you wouldn't touch me," Nereth said. "After the thatcher. You sat a little apart from me all the way to Favale, in case you burned me. I hated it. I'd have it back now."

"Would you?"

"You were frightened. You were still somebody."

"I'm still somebody."

"Are you?" Nereth looked at her at last. "Go on, then. Tell me I'm wrong."

Seralune opened her mouth, and found she had nothing to put in it. The argument was there somewhere. She could see where it ought to be, as she could see where the thing on the hill ought to be. There was only a smooth place.

"I'm tired," she said.

"You're not tired. You're gone." Nereth's voice didn't rise. "You were gone at the Lily Steps. You were gone when they took him. You counted my drops last night and your hand never shook. It always shakes, Sera. It's shaken since the fold."

"Isn't that better?"

"Is it?"

Seralune looked down at her hands. They lay in her lap, perfectly still.

"Your marks stopped," she said. "At the Lily Steps. When I stopped, they stopped. You said so."

Nereth didn't answer.

"They've been quiet ever since. Haven't they?" Seralune kept her eyes on her hands. "Except today. In the market."

"What happened in the market?"

She could tell her. She could say: I was running. There was a field, and a silver thing walking over the dead, and I was running towards something I can't see, and I have to reach it, and I'm late.

She kept it.

She didn't know why. It was the one thing in two days that had felt like hers, even if it hurt. She closed her hands over it, as she'd closed her fingers round her mother's ring in the passage the night she ran, and she kept it.

"Nothing," she said. "The sun."

Nereth held her eyes. Then she looked back at the lanterns, and neither of them said anything more.

It came again in the dark, an hour later, when they'd gone down to the loft and Nereth was asleep.

Smoke. Crying. So much crying, from so many throats that it wasn't voices any longer, only a sound the field made. Her own feet under her, running. She was closer this time. She knew she was closer. The ringing was louder, and the smoke was thinner, and somewhere ahead through it there was —

She came back with her nails driven into her palms and her teeth locked.

Beside her in the dark, Nereth hadn't woken. But her breathing had changed, gone quick and shallow. Under the blanket, her hand had come up to her collarbone and was pressing there in her sleep.

Seralune lay very still and made herself nothing, until Nereth's breathing slowed.

It took longer than it had in the market. It had taken longer in the market than in the loft the morning before.

She counted how much longer, lying there. She knew it as a number.

---

Agna went to the stewards at first light.

She'd put on a clean apron and a shawl Seralune had never seen, dark blue, with a darn in one corner. She stood by the door in it while Nereth did the coppers, turning a square of paper over and over in her hands.

"What's that?" said Bett.

"His name." Agna didn't look at it. "Father Clem wrote it for me the week he was born. And the day. So they can see he's a real boy with a real mother and not something off the street."

"Can I come?"

"No."

"I could tell them he's good. I could tell them he fetches the wood."

"You could tell them he hides in a fuel hole," said Tilde. "That's what they've got in the book."

"*Tilde.*"

"Well, they have."

Agna folded the paper once more and put it inside her shawl, against her chest, and turned to the three coppers rolling behind Nereth.

"The Two Wells," she said. "The Sun and Stair. The rest of Ashby's. Don't let the fire go down on the middle one, it sulks." Her eyes rested on Seralune. "You. Mind the girls."

"I will."

Agna went.

The door banged. For a while nobody said anything. Then Tilde threw a sheet into the rinsing tub so hard that the water came up over the side and onto the floor.

"She'll be all day," she said. "They'll make her sit on a bench till the bell with every other mother in the quarter, and then they'll tell her to come back."

"You don't know that," said Bett.

"I know the stewards."

"You don't. You've never been."

"I know them." Tilde shoved the sheet under with both arms. "Everybody knows them. That's how they get you. You know them before you've ever been."

Bett's lip had started to go. She turned her back and folded something that was already folded.

Seralune wrung out a sheet and hung it on the line.

"You." Tilde had straightened up from the tub. Her arms were red to the elbow, and she was looking at Seralune. "Coast girl."

Seralune turned.

"He lied for you," said Tilde.

The laundry went quiet, except for the coppers.

"The sergeant asked him. Anybody else in the house? And he said *no, sir*." Tilde's voice was very steady. "He's twelve. He's never told a lie to a grown man in his life. He can't. Mam says his ears go red. And he stood in front of the watch and looked at the floor, and he lied for you. Two women he'd known a day."

"Tilde," said Nereth quietly, from the coppers.

"I'm not talking to you. I'm talking to her." Tilde hadn't taken her eyes off Seralune. "Do you know what they'd have done, if they'd found you two in our loft? One with a burned face and one who talks like a lady? The day after four watchmen got killed?"

The coppers muttered. Seralune said nothing.

"No," said Tilde. "Course you don't. You don't know anything about anything. You come in here and you talk like a lady and you say *yes* and *thank you*, and my brother's in a cell, and you haven't cried once. Not once."

Seralune could see it all very clearly: the red arms, the wet apron, the tears Tilde was holding back by main force under her anger. She could see how much the girl wanted her to say something. Anything, so long as it hurt.

"You're right," said Seralune.

Tilde stared at her.

"He did lie for us," Seralune said. "And I haven't cried."

Tilde flinched. Then she bent to the tub and put both arms into it up to the shoulders and didn't take them out.

Bett was looking from one of them to the other with her mouth open.

At the coppers, Nereth had gone very still. Her right hand was flat on the rim of the middle copper, the one that sulked. The flame under her palm was white at the heart, and it didn't waver. But she wasn't looking at the copper.

She was looking at Seralune.

Later, at the pump in the yard, with the bucket filling, Nereth said it.

"She's not wrong."

"I know."

"Then why did you say it like that?"

"Like what?"

"Like you were reading it off a list." Nereth let go of the handle. "*He did lie for us. And I haven't cried.* As if you were telling her the price of barley."

Seralune took the bucket off the spout. It was full to the brim, and not a drop went over.

"It was true," she said.

"That's what frightens me," said Nereth.

---

It came at the coppers, an hour later. Only a breath of it.

Running. A cry cut off short. Close, very close, and the ringing so loud now that it was inside her head and not outside it —

She came back holding on to the edge of the table. Tilde was at the tub, Bett was at the lines, and neither of them had seen. Only Nereth had turned round from the coppers, with one hand pressed to her collarbone.

Their eyes met.

"That's four," said Nereth, low.

"Two."

"Four. There were two in the night."

"You were asleep."

"My marks weren't." Nereth turned back to the fire. "I've been counting."

It came twice more before noon. Each time it was closer. The ache took longer to go out from behind her eyes, and it was harder, afterwards, to find the smooth, quiet place again and lie down in it.

She found it anyway. She went on finding it.

The baskets came and went. The coppers rolled.

In the afternoon, the girl from the Sun and Stair brought a message with her basket: the hostel was sending all its guests up the hill at the evening bell, sick and well, to hold their places on the stair for the morning, and could they have every sheet back by then, and a dozen more.

"A dozen," said Tilde. "Where does she think we keep them?"

"She says she'll pay double."

Tilde eyed the lines. "Give me an hour."

"It's the vigil tonight," Bett told Seralune, when the girl had gone. "You'll see. When the evening bell goes, it isn't for curfew. Not on the eve. Everybody takes a lamp and goes up the hill, and they sit on the stair all night with their sick, so they'll be there when she comes out. And they sing."

"All night?" said Nereth.

"All night. Nobody sleeps." Bett's face had gone soft. "Mam took us once, when Da was alive. I fell asleep on the forty-third step, and Da carried me all the way home."

"I thought nobody slept," said Tilde, without turning round.

"I was five."

"Why the forty-third?" said Nereth.

"That's where we got to." Bett shrugged. "You go as high as you can before the good places are gone. Da said the forty-third was high enough for anybody. He said the saint's shadow's very long in the morning. It reaches right down."

"Are you going tonight?" said Nereth.

"No." Bett's face closed up again. "Not without Mam. Not without —"

She didn't finish.

"When Kit's home," said Seralune.

Bett looked up at her, startled. Then she nodded, fast, several times, and held on to it like a promise.

Seralune hadn't meant it to sound like a promise. She'd only meant it as a fact, something that would happen or wouldn't. She watched Bett take it and hold on to it, and felt nothing about that either.

---

The evening bell rang.

It rang from the hill, the same deep note it had rung every night since the gate, and every night since the gate the shutters had come down under it and the doors had banged and the lanes had emptied.

Tonight, the doors opened.

Seralune went to the laundry door with a basket on her hip and looked out.

All down the lane, people were coming out of their houses with lamps. Clay lamps and tin lamps, candles in jars, lanterns on poles. They came out in families, with the sick carried between them on doors and hurdles and in their arms, and they turned uphill without a word, all of them the same way.

At the end of the lane, the lamps joined the lamps from the next lane, and the next, and the whole slope of the city above them was moving: a river of small lights running uphill towards the stair.

Somewhere up there, the singing had started.

"*The Light that keeps the faithful,*" sang the lane, as it went past the door, "*through the dark, through the dark.*"

"There," said Bett, at her elbow. "Told you."

A man went by with a little girl asleep on his shoulder and a lamp in his free hand. An old couple went by holding each other up.

Four men went by with a door on their shoulders, and on the door lay a boy about Kit's age, grey-faced, with his eyes open, watching the lamps above him go by.

"Shut the door, Bett," said Tilde. "You're letting the steam out."

Bett didn't shut the door.

That was how they were when Agna came back: Seralune and Bett in the doorway, Tilde at her tub, and Nereth at the middle copper, with her hand on its rim, bringing it back to the boil for the last of the dozen.

Agna came down the lane against the lamps, the only person in it going the wrong way. The lamplight went over her face as the people passed, and passed, and left it dark.

She came in and shut the door behind her on the lamps. She took off the blue shawl and hung it on its peg, and smoothed it flat. Then she sat down on the stool by the coppers, where she sat to feed the fires, and put her hands on her knees.

"Mam?" said Tilde.

"After," said Agna.

The laundry went still.

"They'll sit after." Her voice was perfectly ordinary. "When the pilgrims have gone home. There's too many cases, with the Longest Light, and the stewards all want to be on the stair. So they'll sit after. Five days, maybe. Or ten." Her eyes were on her hands. "They wouldn't look at his paper. They said they had his name already. In the book."

"Where is he?" said Bett. "Did you see him?"

"No."

"Did they say —"

"They said he's being fed." Agna's hands were flat on her knees. "They said he's with the others, and he's being fed, and he's got a blanket. That's what they said."

"Did you tell them he can't warm his own hands?" said Tilde. "Did you tell them three drops?"

"I told them."

"Did you tell them he fetches the wood? Did you tell them —"

"I told them everything, Tilde." Agna's voice didn't rise. "I told a man with a book everything about my son, for as long as he let me. Then he said *after*, and turned the page."

Bett began to cry.

She did it quietly at first, with her hands over her face, and then not quietly.

Tilde went to her and put both arms round her and held on, as she'd held her when the watch took him, so hard that neither of them could move. Over her sister's head, Tilde was looking at her mother, and her own face was wet.

Agna didn't look up. She sat on the stool with her hands on her knees and stared at the little iron door at the end of the coppers.

Seralune stood with the basket on her hip.

It came up in her.

Not the running. Something smaller, first. Bett's hands over her face. The paper with his name on it, that nobody would look at. *He's being fed.* A boy on a door in the lane with his eyes open, watching the lamps. Kit on the roof, tracing the watch's way with a sooty finger, *eight hundred and something*. Kit saying *no, sir*, with his ears going red.

It rose under the quiet like water under ice. She felt the quiet give.

She reached for it. She reached for the smooth place, the easy place, and it wasn't where she'd left it —

And the field came up through the crack.

All of it. The noise first, so loud it had weight: steel and screaming and the ringing of the walking things, and under all of it the crying, from so many throats that the ground itself seemed to be crying.

The smoke, and the red mud, and the dead underfoot: soldiers, horned things, pale long-eared men like her own people, and her own feet running over them. The man with his hands over his face. The voice calling the same name and the same name and the same name.

Blood on her hands. Whose blood?

She was running. She was so close now. The smoke was tearing open in front of her, and there was someone there, someone just ahead, just there —

She was crying. It was her. The crying was hers.

— and she was late, she was late, she was —

The copper roared.

Seralune was on her knees on the wet floor. She didn't know how she'd got there. The basket had gone. The sheets were everywhere.

At the coppers, the middle one was boiling over. Nereth's hand had been on its rim when the marks took her, and her fire had gone through it all at once. Water came up over the rim in a grey, rolling surge and went down the brick into the fire and hissed, and the steam burst out across the room in a cloud.

Nereth was on the floor in front of it.

She'd folded up where she stood. She was lying on her side with her knees drawn up and both hands clamped to her chest, and a sound was coming out of her that Seralune had never heard a person make. Not a scream. It was lower than a scream, and it didn't stop.

Her scarf had come loose. Along her throat and under her jaw, the dark lines stood out against her skin like cracks in glass, and they were moving.

"Nereth —"

Agna was already there.

She came off the stool and down onto the wet floor in one movement, heavily, on both knees, and got her arms under Nereth's shoulders and pulled her up against her own chest, out of the water.

She held her there. She put one big red hand flat on Nereth's hair and held her head still, the way you'd hold a child's in a fever, and said, "There. There. I've got you. I've got you."

Nereth's hands clawed at her apron.

"What is it?" Tilde's voice was high. She had Bett pushed against the wall at her back, and the paddle in her hand, held up across her body like a spear. "Mam, what is it? What's wrong with her?"

"Get me a cloth. Cold water. Now, Tilde."

Tilde didn't move. She was staring at Seralune.

Seralune looked down at herself.

At her hands, on the wet floor, open. At the grey water running off her knees.

At Bett, pressed flat against the wall behind her sister with both fists jammed against her mouth.

At Tilde's face.

At Nereth, in Agna's arms, with the lines crawling under the skin of her throat.

It was her.

It was all her. The copper, and the heat, and the sound Nereth was making. The thatcher on the road with fire coming out of his eyes. The woman on the arch in her grey shift. Kit, walking out between the watch with his head up. Every one of them had paid for her: for standing too close to her, or for looking like her, or for hiding her.

She got up.

"Sera." Agna's voice. "Sera, sit down. Sit down, girl. Whatever it is —"

Seralune backed away from them. Her shoulders hit the door. The latch was under her hand.

"I'm sorry," she said.

On the floor, Nereth was trying to get up.

She had one hand on the brick and one on Agna's shoulder, and her knees kept going out from under her, and she kept getting them back.

"You can't stand!" Agna had hold of her skirt. "You can't *stand* —"

"Watch me," said Nereth.

Seralune pulled the door open and ran from her, out into the lamps.

---

She ran downhill.

Everyone else was going up. The lane was a river of lamps, and she ran against it with her shoulders turned, pushing through, and the lamps parted round her and closed again and went on.

"Wrong way, girl!"

"Mind the door —"

"Are you lost, love? The stair's up there. Up."

"Let her be, Hal. She'll have forgotten her lamp."

"You'll want a lamp, girl! You'll never find your place without a lamp —"

She didn't answer them. She didn't know where she was going. She only knew it was away: away from the laundry and the copper and the sound Nereth had made, away from every one of these people with their lamps and their sick and their hymn, before it could happen to them.

"*Through the dark,*" sang the lane, "*through the dark.*"

At the bottom of the lane she turned without deciding to. At the next corner, she turned again. Her feet knew the way. She'd walked it once, with a laundry basket between her and Nereth, in the middle of the morning with the whole city watching.

The river road was a flood of lights. She crossed it through the gaps. Past the old palace, where the gate-guards' torches lit the two banners from below: the sun and, a hand's breadth higher, her father's tree. She didn't look at them.

Then the old quarter.

It was dark. There were no banners here and no lanterns, and nobody had stayed behind to light a window. Everyone who could walk had gone up to the hill, and everyone who couldn't had been carried.

The leaning houses stood over the lanes with their shutters closed, and her feet sounded on the stones, and nothing else did.

Somewhere far behind her, a voice called her name. Once. The hymn swallowed it.

She knew where she was going now. She'd known since the laundry door.

It was the only door in the whole of Favale with her mother's mark on it, and she was running to it as she'd once run to her mother's rooms when she was small and frightened and the palace was too big: without thinking, without asking whether anyone would be there.

There'd be no one there. There hadn't been for twenty years.

She ran anyway.

The lane opened. The Lily Steps went down to the river on her left, pale in the dark. At the top of them, where a house should have been, was the gap.

She stopped.

Someone was standing in it.

He stood in the middle of the burned house, among the black stumps of its walls, with his back to her. He was facing the doorway, the one that still stood on its own with its charred lintel and nothing on either side. He was looking at it.

He was tall. He wore dark armour, and over it a travelling cloak that the road had turned grey. A sword hung at his hip in a plain black scabbard.

He wasn't moving. The dust lay on his shoulders, settled and undisturbed.

Seralune knew him from behind. She'd have known him in any crowd in the world, by the set of his shoulders alone: a man who stood, even in a burned house at night, like one waiting for a council to finish.

Up on the hill, the vigil sang. The lamps climbed the stair in their thousands, and their light came down across the city and just reached the old quarter, and just reached him.

He lifted one hand, and knocked twice on the doorpost.

The spacing was perfect.

*Two knocks are a warning,* her mother said, a thousand years away. *They are not permission.*

He lowered his hand. Then he turned round.

"I knew you would come here," said Thaeroval.
