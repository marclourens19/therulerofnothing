# Interlude: III

The bird found them at the marker.

It came out of the west with the sun behind it, a grey-backed message falcon from the Kelmend lofts, and dropped onto the stone as if it had flown there on purpose to sit on the words. *NO CLAIM OF RESCUE WILL BE HONOURED.* It folded its wings over the last of them and looked at Liluth with one orange eye.

She knew it before it landed. She'd heard the bell on its jess from half a mile off. She'd been waiting for it for two days.

She didn't dismount. She held out her left arm, and the falcon came to it, and she turned her head all the way round to see it properly. Everything on her left came to her like that now: late, and only if she went looking for it.

The message was tied in blue thread. Field command.

*The survivor is to be taken alive and returned to Kelmend for His Majesty's questioning. No settlement or civilian gathering is to be engaged. Hold at the Expanse marker. A relief party of forty follows within six days.*

She read it twice. Then she held it out to the side without looking.

"Burn it."

The Fire rider hesitated for exactly as long as it took to glance at the others. Then a small flame came up off his fingers and took the corner of the paper, and Liluth held it until the fire reached her glove.

"Command says press on," she told them. "They want him before the relief party gets here and takes the credit."

Nobody asked to see the ashes. They were scouts. They'd been told the truth by commanders all their lives, so they'd never learned to hear a lie in the same voice.

She put the falcon back in its hood and rode past the marker into the Expanse.

---

Forty riders. Six days. His Majesty's questions.

Liluth knew what that meant. It meant men in good armour from the capital would take the boy out of her hands at the marker and put him in a closed wagon, and he'd go north to the palace and answer questions in a warm room about the field and the machines and why he was alive. And when they'd finished asking, someone else would decide what he was worth.

Nobody would ask her anything. Nobody in the capital would ask what the farmer had used, or how long it had taken, or what she'd found when she crawled back to the yard. The field was a question now. The king had flown over it himself. The boy was a question.

He'd never been a question to her.

She'd been hunting him since the farmer's yard. They'd given her orders and riders and birds, and she'd let them think she was the thing they'd aimed. Somewhere along the way, she'd understood that she was the one being aimed. Command didn't care about Gerolt's farm, or the man who'd died in its yard. Command cared that the boy had walked out of a thousand-year-old battlefield alive, and that the hunter they'd sent after him was the only one who'd seen his face.

Very well. Let them aim her.

She would get there first.

---

The ground paled as they rode. By afternoon their tracks showed dark behind them, and the strange dust had started ticking against the buckles of her armour.

She kept turning her head to the left.

She didn't mean to. Her body did it for her, every few breaths, the way a tongue keeps finding a missing tooth. She'd turn, and there'd be the dark where her eye had been, and she'd have to keep turning until the pale land swung into her right eye. And there'd be nothing there.

He had ridden on her left for a hundred and twelve years.

She'd pulled left since she was a girl learning to sit a horse. Her instructor had said her mare had a grudge against the right. So Eldrin had taken the left side on their first patrol, when they were two green scouts who'd sworn their partnership before the Tree the week before. He'd kept it on every patrol after that. He'd put his horse between hers and every ford they ever crossed, and every time, he said the same thing. *You'd walk into the sea if I let you.*

He'd been on her left in the farmer's yard.

She turned her head again. Dark. Then the land.

Ahead of her, the tracks went on. Three sets of boots and a dog. She'd known the dog's prints for a week. She'd known the boy's since the wet ground below the kilns, where the right foot dragged and the left bore the weight.

It didn't drag anymore.

She'd noticed it two days past the inn, and had ridden back along the trail to be sure. The stride had evened out in four days. Five at the most. The foot she'd watched him limp on through the Faint quarter, with half of Kelmend's garrison behind him, was carrying him now as if nothing had ever happened to it.

Wounds like that didn't close in five days. She knew. She'd been waiting for her own to do it since the farmer's yard.

---

They stopped at dusk to water the horses at a channel that still ran, a thin grey trickle between stones.

Liluth took her pack behind a spur of rock where the others couldn't see.

The dressings came first. She peeled the cloth away from the socket, and it came off wet, the way it always did. The field physician in Kelmend had told her it would stop weeping eventually. He'd also told her the arm would heal clean. She washed the socket with water from her own skin, not looking at it, and packed it, and tied the new cloth by feel.

Then the arm.

The new bracer was good work, fitted in Kelmend to hide the break from anyone who might look for it. Under it, the arm was thin and yellow and wrong. She got the bracer off. She got the old binding off. She flexed her fingers, and they came halfway to a fist and stopped there, as they had every evening, and the pain went up to her shoulder and sat down in her teeth.

She tried to tighten the buckle one-handed. Her hand slipped.

She tried again. It slipped again.

She thought, suddenly and without wanting to, of the innkeeper's hands.

Broad. Freckled across the backs, with an old oven burn over three knuckles like a pale ribbon. Flat on the hearthstone. The woman hadn't screamed. She'd looked at the fire the whole time and said nothing at all, not one word, while the stone closed, and Liluth had understood halfway through that it wasn't going to work. That the woman would let the stone finish her before she gave the boy up.

So she'd stopped, and sent for the peat-cutter and his girl.

*It works on people who love someone else.*

She had said it out loud, in that kitchen, as if she were explaining the weather.

It was true. It would have worked on her, once. If anyone had ever held Eldrin over her head, she'd have given them anything they wanted before the first knuckle went. She knew that as surely as she knew her own name.

Nobody could hold anything over her now. That was the only thing the farmer had left her. There was nobody left to lose.

She got the buckle closed on the fourth try.

---

When she came back round the rock, the youngest of them was filling skins at the channel.

Aelis. A Water rider, barely forty, from the river patrols on the southern border. She had a way of looking at Liluth's face and then very carefully not looking at it, which was worse than staring.

"Commander." She held out a full skin. "The light's going. I only wondered —"

She stopped, then went on, as if it were nothing.

"Eldrin would have waited for dawn, wouldn't he? He always said the Expanse played tricks on you after dark."

The whole camp went quiet.

The Earth rider stopped rubbing down his horse. The Fire rider looked at the ground. At the far end of the channel, the Wind rider, who was older than all of them and had patrolled with Eldrin once, went on checking his girth strap without looking up.

Aelis's face changed. She understood what she'd done a heartbeat too late to undo it.

Liluth took the water skin from her hand.

She didn't answer. She didn't need to. Nobody said his name again.

---

"I'm checking the trail," she told the Wind rider, and rode out alone.

A mile on, she stopped in the lee of a fallen wall and took the falcon out of its basket.

She wrote the report with her left hand, because her right couldn't hold the pen. The letters came out large and crooked, like a child's. *Survivor's party entered the Great Expanse by the eastern track two days past the last farm. Three and a dog. Pursuing. Present position:* and the marks for it, as close as she could make them.

She tied it to the bird's leg with the blue thread from the burned order.

Then she sat with the falcon on her wrist and looked at it.

If she sent it, the forty would come on after her with a map. Somebody from the capital would ride at their head, and when they reached her, he'd thank her for her diligence and take the boy out of her hands. And she'd ride back to Kelmend with her one eye and her half an arm and nothing to show for any of it.

She untied the thread.

She folded the report small and pushed it down inside her glove, against her palm, where it would stay.

The falcon looked at her.

"Go on," she said, and lifted her arm.

It went up, circled once, and turned west towards the lofts, carrying nothing at all.

---

The tracks went over the ridge.

She followed them up in the last of the light. The riders came behind her in a loose line. Halfway up, she smelled smoke and slowed. At the top, she stopped.

The city filled the land below. Half of it was gone. The other half leaned towards the hole where the rest had been, as if it had been dragged there and left. She'd heard stories about the Expanse since she was a child in Natharul. None of them had been big enough.

And in the part of it that was still standing, there were fires.

Not one. Not the small cook-fire of three fugitives and a dog. Dozens of them, small and careful, set in clay-lined pits between brown walls. Smoke drawn up through open roofs in thin, pale threads. Lights in windows. The faint shapes of people moving between them.

Sound carried strangely out here. It reached her from the city as if it came from the next ridge. Voices. A pot lid. And somewhere under it all, a stringed instrument playing the same short tune over and over. Two notes, rising. Then a pause where the third one should have gone, and then the two notes again.

The Wind rider drew up beside her and looked down for a long moment.

"Commander," he said quietly. "That's a settlement."

"Then he's hiding in it."

"Command said no settlement was to be —"

"Command said to take him alive." She didn't look at him. "Look at the tracks."

He looked. The three sets of boots and the dog went down the old road into the city, and didn't come back up.

Below, the tune reached its gap again. The third note didn't come.

Liluth turned her head to the left to say something.

There was only the dark.

She turned back, slowly, until the fires swung into her one good eye.

"Down," she said.
