# Into the Great Expanse

The road to Favale split in three under Silas's finger.

One black line ran east across the map before bending south. Another disappeared under a painted mass of green. The third had been drawn more faintly than either, as if whoever made the map had expected the ink to regret going there.

Silas held the curling parchment flat with his cup.

"The main road's the easiest," he said. "Wells, farms, somewhere to sleep every few miles. Which also makes it the first place they'll look."

Alaric leaned in. The satchel Silas had given him pressed between his coat and the edge of the table, its strap crossing his chest under the dark travelling cloak Silas had bought off the innkeeper.

The wrapped orb inside hadn't stirred since Redd moved it out of the sack. No light had come back to its centre. No warmth reached through the cloth. It sat against him with the plain weight of glass and metal, as if it had never carried him into someone else's room.

"How long would the road take?"

"A little over a week, with horses." Silas moved his finger along the black line. "Longer on foot. Longer still if we spend every mile avoiding people."

Redd turned the map towards himself. He'd pushed his hood back far enough for his hair to escape round his face, though the rest of him was lost inside a cloak that had once belonged to someone much broader.

"And this cheerful green stain?"

"Inrandeel."

"That's a forest."

"It's a rainforest," Silas said, and drank. "Twice the rain, three times the trees, and enough under them to make you miss soldiers."

Redd studied the painted canopy. "Trees sound manageable."

"Trolls?"

Redd studied it again. "Less manageable."

"Forest imps hunt in packs. And further in, there are things that don't have names outside Inrandeel, because the people who meet them don't often come back to give them one."

Alaric looked under the table. Wena lay across their feet with her nose by Redd's boot. The bench, their cloaks and the table's solid side hid most of her from the room. One ear twitched whenever somebody laughed near the hearth, but her eyes stayed shut.

"You've crossed it before?" Alaric asked.

"Alone." Silas tipped his cup at Wena, then round the rest of the table. "I can hide one man from something I can't kill. I can't hide two Faints, a dog and myself while one of you argues that a troll might listen to reason."

"Why am I the one talking to the troll?" Redd asked.

"Because Alaric would apologise to it, and we'd all die politely."

Redd laughed into his cup.

Alaric tried to smile with them. The word *Faint* stayed behind after the joke had gone. Redd thought it described him. Anyone who saw two young men travelling under Silas's protection would think the same.

They'd be wrong. Faint meant something was there, however weak. Three drops of water in a silver cup. A little earth that moved when fear reached it. Marta had searched him for even that much and found nothing at all.

He pushed the thought away and pointed at the faded line.

"What's along this one?"

Silas took the cup off the map and pressed the corner flat with two fingers.

"The Great Expanse."

Redd's grin went out. "That's a real place?"

"You thought people made it up?"

"I thought people made it bigger every time the story reached a new tavern."

"They usually do." Silas followed the faint line east. "This one doesn't need much help."

Round them, the inn went on without noticing the map. A woman by the hearth was arguing about the price of a room. Two wagon drivers shared bread at the bar. The innkeeper wiped the same stretch of wood without listening closely enough to either to become involved.

The smell of fried onions clung to the low ceiling. Alaric had eaten until the hollow in his stomach stopped aching. He'd expected every mouthful to pull at his ribs.

It hadn't.

He drew a deeper breath under his cloak. No pain met it. He sat up straighter and waited for the ache to arrive late.

It didn't.

Silas tapped the faded route. "Parts of the Expanse are bare. Water's unreliable. Some of what grows there grew wrong, and most of what lives there has learned that travellers carry food."

"You're describing Inrandeel with fewer trees," Redd said.

"There are open stretches where we can see what's coming. Inrandeel doesn't give you that."

"Have you crossed it?" Alaric asked.

"Twice. Round the edges, keeping to the outer ruins. I wouldn't take you through the middle."

"Does it get us to Favale?"

"It brings us out close enough to join the eastern road past the worst of the checkpoints. If the paths haven't shifted since I last went through."

"Paths *shift*?" said Redd.

"Sand moves. Old stone gives way. People pull up the markers, because they think getting someone else lost improves their own chances." Silas folded one side of the map inwards, then opened it again. "We don't take it unless the road leaves us no choice."

Alaric looked at the three lines.

The road offered water, shelter and soldiers. Inrandeel offered cover from people, and things Silas could only slip past alone. The Expanse offered no safety at all. Only a danger the search might refuse to follow them into.

"If you were looking for us," he said, "where would you wait?"

"On the road."

"And if we weren't on it?"

"I'd send riders along it until someone found our tracks leaving."

"Would you go into the Expanse?"

Silas looked at the map for a while. "Only if I knew you'd gone that way."

Redd rubbed the side of his cup against his lip. "I could try keeping things off us in the forest."

"With Earth?" Silas asked.

"It works when it feels like it."

"Trolls are famously respectful of effort."

Redd put the cup down. This time he didn't joke back.

"You really don't think we'd make it?"

Silas turned to face him properly. "I could hide myself. With four of us, something would hear us sooner or later. And if it did, I'd need both of you able to run." He nodded at Alaric's boot. "Look at his foot."

Redd looked down at Wena instead. Her head had shifted until it lay across the toe of his boot.

"All right," he said. "Not the forest."

Silas turned the map towards Alaric.

Alaric touched the satchel through his cloak. Favale lay past every choice on the map. Helmi was there, if Darcy's words could reach him and he was willing to listen. Somewhere in the city stood the Great Library.

And perhaps, somewhere, whatever was left of the woman.

"The soldiers expect us to take the road," he said. "If the Expanse gets us closer, and you know how to cross it, I think we should take it."

Silas looked from Alaric to Redd.

"Once we're past the last farms, we can't decide we've had enough and find an inn. If the water's bad, we keep moving. If we see something in the ruins, we don't go and look at it because Redd thinks dying somewhere new counts as an adventure."

"I've never said that."

"You were about to."

Redd thought about denying it, then grinned. "The Expanse, then."

"The Expanse," Alaric said.

The front door banged against the wall.

Cold air crossed the room ahead of six Mydean soldiers.

Every conversation near the door moved out of their way. Wet road dust caked their boots and the hems of their blue-grey cloaks. One had left his breastplate unbuckled under the right arm. Another had his helmet under his elbow and nearly knocked a cup out of a wagon driver's hand going past.

"Ale," the first one called. "Six, and whatever you've kept warm."

The innkeeper laid down his cloth. "Payment first."

The soldier laughed and dropped a handful of coins hard enough that one rolled off across the bar.

"There. Now find something that doesn't taste like horse piss."

Silas folded the map without looking down.

Alaric dipped his chin into his cloak. Across the table, Redd eased his foot out from under Wena's head. Her eyes opened. He laid two fingers beside her nose before she could get up.

The soldiers crowded the bar. None of them had started looking at the other drinkers yet. Their voices filled the room as if getting there first were another privilege of rank.

"Eastern post's already doubled," one said. "Captain wants another pair on every bridge by morning."

"Then he can grow the men himself." The soldier with the loose armour took the first cup. "Half the company's hauling wagons for the elves."

"Keep your voice down."

"Why? Everyone knows who we're guarding." He drank and wiped his mouth on his wrist. "Beastfolk cross the border, Natharul comes to *help*, and we're the ones standing in the mud while they decide where the war goes. They even flew the strategist out of Kelmend. Put her on the airship three days back and sent her east to the frontier, with that captain of hers holding the leash."

Under the table, Alaric's hand closed on the edge of the bench.

The first soldier had taken the chair nearest the bar and turned it round to straddle it. "You can complain after we've got the boy. Until then, every cart going east gets checked."

"They'll have him before midday," said another. "Bad foot, dark hair, farm dog big enough to pull a plough. There's only one road he can take."

"Unless he goes into Inrandeel."

Several of them laughed.

"Then the imps save us the trouble."

"Or the Expanse," the one with the helmet said.

"An injured boy and a dog?" The first soldier reached for the cup coming his way. "Nobody's that stupid."

"Elf riders join us at first bell. If he leaves the road before then, they'll find the tracks."

"The elves are busy tonight." The man with the helmet lowered his voice, which only made the others lean in. "The one with the face is going round to that inn by the south gate after closing. The woman whose uncle had the farm. They found fort armour in a house in the old quarter, and they think she knows who carried it out."

Alaric didn't look at Silas. He didn't have to.

Across the table, Silas had gone completely still. Not the stillness of someone listening. The stillness of something that had been struck and hadn't fallen over yet.

"She'll talk," the loose-armoured one said. "They always talk for that one."

Silas slid the folded map inside his cloak.

He tipped his head, very slightly, towards the narrow passage by the kitchen. At the far end, a half-door stood open onto the stable yard. A serving woman went through it with an empty bucket, and the soldiers shouted for another round before she'd taken three steps.

Silas stood while every eye in the room followed her.

He was out of his seat before Alaric had understood he meant to move.

Redd found Wena's rope under the table without letting the buckle scrape the floor. Alaric pulled the ash stick from under the bench and stood between the dog and the bar while Redd guided her behind it. His foot took his weight before he could brace for the pain.

None came.

They went into the passage one at a time. Silas first. Alaric after him, cloak closed over the satchel. Redd last, keeping Wena tight against the wall and under the shadow of his cloak for as long as the cloth would reach.

One of the soldiers shouted that the bread was stale.

"It was fresh before you walked in," the innkeeper said.

Laughter broke over the bar. Alaric reached the passage before anyone turned round.

The stable yard smelled of wet straw and dung. Silas crossed it without hurrying until the inn wall stood between them and its windows. Then he ran.

Alaric ran after him.

Behind him, Wena's claws skittered across the stones as Redd kept her rope short. His foot hit mud, then stone, then the hard lip of a wagon rut.

It didn't hurt.

---

They stopped under a derelict lime shed once the inn's roof had vanished behind two folds of land.

Only three walls were left. The fourth had fallen outwards and left a low gap facing away from the road. Silas went in first, checked the broken kiln and the weeds behind it, then waved the others inside.

Redd let Wena's rope go. She turned twice on the dry earth and sat down beside him.

Silas didn't sit. He stood at the gap with his back to them, looking west, where there was nothing to see but the dark shape of the land.

"Silas," Alaric said.

"I know."

"We could —"

"If I go back, she's dead." Silas didn't turn round. His voice was quite level. "That's why they said it in a room full of strangers, loud enough for anyone to hear. They want whoever carried that armour to come back for her. If I walk into Kelmend tonight, they'll kill her in front of me to make me talk, and then they'll kill me, and then they'll come for you."

Nobody answered.

"She knew what she was risking when she sent me after Darcy." His good hand closed on the edge of the broken wall. "She made me promise to get you to the farm and away alive. That's the only thing I can still do for her."

He stood there a while longer. Then he let go of the wall, and when he turned round, his face had closed over whatever had been in it.

"Sit down," he told Alaric.

"I'm all right."

"You ran. That's why I want to see it."

Alaric sat on a fallen block. Silas crouched over his boot. Dirt had hardened round the knots after days of wear, and he worked them loose with the point of his knife before drawing the boot off.

Redd brought the covered lantern closer.

The swelling round Alaric's ankle had almost gone.

Silas unwound the inner wrapping. Where the torn skin had still been red and open that morning, a thin new line had closed across it. The bruising along the side of his foot had faded towards yellow.

Silas pressed beside the injury. "Anything?"

"Pressure."

"Pain?"

"No."

Silas pressed harder. Alaric shook his head.

Redd leaned over Silas's shoulder. "Did Marta swap his foot for a new one while he was asleep?"

The name went through the shed like a draught. Redd heard it too late.

"She treated it," Silas said.

"She treated your hand as well," Redd said, quieter. "Look at that."

Silas didn't. He looked at Alaric. "Breathe in as deep as you can."

Alaric did.

The air reached the bottom of his lungs without catching on his ribs. The plain fullness of it frightened him more than the pain had.

"Again."

The second breath came just as easily.

Silas checked his shoulder next, then unwrapped his palms. The joint was still stiff, but the deep pain had gone. Thin pink skin had closed under both dressings, and flexing his fingers didn't pull either wound open.

Silas wrapped them again, more loosely, and sat back on his heels. "She must have done more than I realised."

"Wouldn't she have told him?" Redd asked.

"I don't know." Silas looked at the new skin once more. "I've never seen her do anything like this."

Alaric looked at it too. "Could the core have done it?"

Silas nodded at the satchel. "Has it warmed again?"

"No."

"Any light?"

"Nothing."

Silas retied the cleanest length of wrapping round Alaric's foot. "Then we don't give it credit for something we can't prove. Keep the cloth round it. Don't let it touch your skin."

He hitched the satchel higher, so its weight rode against Alaric's coat. The orb inside lay still.

Redd sat down by Wena and scratched behind her ear. "If you wake up tomorrow with a third foot, I'm blaming the orb."

Alaric pulled his boot back on.

When he stood, the ground felt level under both feet. He took the ash stick when they left, though he didn't lean on it as hard.

---

The next afternoon, after a day of moving between low hills north of the road, they stopped inside the ruins of a shepherd's fold. Far to their right, wagons and mounted patrols crawled east in a line that appeared and vanished behind the rise and fall of the land. None of them turned towards the hills.

The stone wall reached Alaric's knee in some places and his waist in others. Wena had claimed the only patch of grass without burrs in it.

Silas was running a whetstone along his sword when Alaric came over.

"Can you teach me?"

Silas looked from him to the blade across his knees. "Teach you what?"

"How to use one."

By the wall, Redd stopped dividing up the dried meat. Wena lifted her head.

Silas put the whetstone back in its pouch. "Why?"

Alaric had worked out an answer on the walk. Now the question made the words sound borrowed.

"At the fort, I pulled the guard off you, and he landed on me," he said. "If I hadn't got to the knife first… I didn't know what else to do."

The memory came without the room round it. The guard's weight. The way the blade had resisted, then hadn't. The warmth running over his fingers.

"I thought he was going to kill us." He made himself keep looking at Silas. "If it happens again, I want to be able to help. Not just grab at someone and hope."

Silas slid his sword into its sheath.

"Learning to fight won't guarantee that."

"I know."

"Do you?" Silas stood. "A sword won't stop you being afraid. And carrying one might put you in fights you'd have got away from without it."

Alaric remembered the knife going in between the guard's ribs. "Then teach me how not to make things worse."

Silas looked at him for a long moment. Then he went to the packs.

He unbuckled a narrow roll strapped under his own pack and laid it on the wall. Inside was the second-hand sword he'd bought at the inn along with their cloaks. Its leather grip had darkened with years of other people's hands, and shallow nicks crossed the plain guard. The sheath carried no crest, no mark at all.

He held it out.

Alaric took it in both hands. It was heavier than he'd expected.

"It's yours," Silas said. "As long as you learn to carry it without cutting yourself. That means you practise whenever we've got ground and time. You don't draw it to threaten anyone. You don't put your hand on it because you're angry. And you don't think having it makes you any use in a fight."

"All right."

"Say that after the first lesson."

Redd had settled beside Wena with the food forgotten between them. "You're doing wonders for his confidence."

Silas led Alaric out past the wall. "Draw it."

Alaric closed his right hand round the grip and pulled. The blade came halfway, and the sheath came up with it.

Redd started laughing.

Alaric tried again, holding the sheath lower. The point came clear and swung down towards the ground with enough weight to drag his shoulder after it.

"Not at your foot," Silas said.

"I wasn't aiming there."

"That's the worry."

Silas moved behind him and shifted the angle of his elbow. He set Alaric's feet further apart, then nudged the left one forward with the side of his boot.

"Don't stand like the ground's offended you. Bend your knees."

Alaric did. The healing foot held.

Silas stepped round in front of him. "Keep the point where you can see it without staring at it. I'm the one trying to hurt you. Not the sword."

He drew his own blade left-handed, keeping the dressing on his burned palm well clear of the hilt.

Alaric's eyes went straight to the edge.

Silas tapped the flat against Alaric's sword and knocked the point aside.

"Again."

Alaric brought it back to where Silas had shown him.

Silas moved first. The attack came slowly enough to follow, a falling line towards Alaric's left shoulder. Alaric brought his sword up and stopped before the blades met.

Silas's edge came to rest against his coat.

"You stopped."

"I thought you were going to change direction."

"I wasn't."

"You could have."

"Then you'd have learned something else. Again."

The second try ended with Alaric stepping backwards over his own heel. On the third, his sword went too high. On the fourth, he watched Silas's hands and missed the turn of his shoulders. Every failure got its own correction. None of them made the next movement any easier.

Redd offered advice until Silas threatened to let him demonstrate.

"I'm preserving the dignity of the lesson," Redd said.

"You're eating all the meat."

Redd looked down and found that he had, in fact, eaten most of his share. He broke the last piece in two and offered half to Wena.

"Traitor," Silas told the dog when she took it.

Wena chewed, untroubled.

Alaric set his feet again.

Silas lifted his sword. "Watch me. Not the blade."

The attack came from Alaric's right. He moved to meet it, heard the edges ring, and went slack at the sound.

Silas pushed straight through. Alaric's point dropped.

"You committed until the blades met," Silas said. "Then you pulled back."

Alaric looked at the two blades crossed between them.

The guard under Fort Kelmend had made a sound when the knife went in. Alaric had felt his body resist and pushed harder, desperate to get the arm off his throat.

Silas lowered his sword.

"We can stop."

"I don't want to."

"You don't have to prove anything to me."

"I'm not trying to." Alaric kept the sword up. "I need another go."

Silas gave him one.

Alaric failed again.

Redd leaned back against the wall. "Move your left foot, Al. At this rate the rest of you'll get to Favale without it."

Alaric fixed his stance before the next attack came.

Then he heard what Redd had called him.

"Al?"

Redd shrugged. "Alaric's too long when there's a sword coming at you."

Silas waited with his blade down.

Alaric said it once inside his head. Gerolt had given him *Alaric*, beside a dying fire, with blood on the floor. This was something else. Something that belonged to now.

"Al's fine," he said.

Redd grinned. "Good. Move your foot, Al."

He moved it.

Silas came at him again.

---

The inhabited land thinned out over the days that followed.

Fields turned into strips cut between long reaches of scrub. The houses turned their backs on the eastern track, doors facing west even where that put them into the wind. They passed wells capped with fitted stones, and a bridge whose sign pointed travellers south to the royal road, though the lesser path carried on east.

Silas kept them well clear of every door.

The patrols on the main road grew. From the shelter of a dry streambed, Alaric watched twelve Mydean riders escort two wagons east. An elven banner hung beside the Mydean colours on the second wagon, pale enough to catch the light through the dust.

He thought of an airship crawling east over the same country three days ahead of him. Darcy at a rail somewhere, with a captain beside her holding the leash.

Nobody left the road to search the scrub. The soldiers at the inn had been right. An injured boy and a farm dog were expected somewhere safer.

Alaric stopped using the stick on the second day. By the third, he was carrying his own share of the water without any pain in his ribs. Silas checked his foot every evening, until the closed line was hard to tell from the skin round it.

"Marta should charge more," Redd said after the last inspection. Then he heard himself, and his face changed.

Silas retied the boot and didn't answer.

They kept practising whenever the ground allowed it.

Alaric learned that a sword gets heavy long before your arms admit they're tired. He learned that Silas could strike the same place five times and make every approach look different. He learned that watching someone meant more than watching their eyes. Hips turned. Weight shifted. A back heel lifted before the body committed to moving.

Knowing those things didn't let him stop them.

Silas disarmed him twice on the fourth morning, and once before breakfast on the fifth. Redd counted every failure, until Alaric asked how often he'd practised with the sword at his own hip.

"Enough to know I'm safer over here," Redd said.

Wena agreed by staying beside him.

She'd started following Redd whenever he left camp to fill the water skins. The first time, Alaric nearly called her back. He caught himself before her name got out and watched Redd bring her back on the rope. The next time, the rope hung over Redd's shoulder while she walked at his knee. When Alaric trained, she lay down wherever Redd sat and watched the moving blades without trying to get to him.

On the fifth night, they camped under a stone shelf above the last occupied farm they'd see before the Expanse.

Redd was on first watch. It should have been Alaric's, but Redd had swapped with him without telling Silas so that Alaric could sleep.

"You can't keep taking his watches because you'd rather be useful than asleep," Silas said.

"I've spent enough of my life being useless. I thought I'd try being tired instead."

Silas handed him the covered lantern. "Two hours. Then wake me."

"You've got very comfortable ordering me about."

"You keep following us."

"Where else would I go?"

It went on sounding in the dark after the joke had gone out of it.

Alaric sat with his back to the shelf. Wena lay between him and Redd, though her head was on Redd's folded cloak.

"Do you wish we could have gone back?" Alaric asked. "Before they burned it?"

Redd turned the lantern once between his palms.

"I wish they hadn't held my things up to the light like they'd found something." His voice had more anger in it than grief. "I wish they hadn't walked in like the place already belonged to them. And I wish they'd let me watch it go without laughing. One of them laughed, when the roof went."

He looked west. The land had hidden Kelmend days ago.

"But it was a house. A bad one. The chimney smoked whenever it rained, and the north wall made a noise at night that convinced me someone was dying inside it. The roof was the only good thing about it, and they had that too." He let out a breath. "I'm alive. I've got my father's sword. And I get to see what's at the end of this road."

"Favale," Silas said.

"I was hoping for something less obvious." Redd scratched Wena's neck. "An adventure. A better chimney."

Alaric smiled.

Redd glanced from him to Silas. "I was on my own there, you know. Three years. Now I've got friends. If that means sleeping under rocks and being insulted at every meal, I've put up with worse arrangements."

Silas set the provisions beside him. "Then take second watch tomorrow without trading it away. If we're travelling together, everyone takes a turn."

"That sounded almost kind," Redd said.

"It was an instruction."

"Of course."

Silas lay down with his cloak under his head.

Redd kept the lantern. When Wena shifted closer, he made room for her without stopping the hand moving through her fur.

---

The next morning, the farms ended.

A stone marker stood where the last fence turned west. Its lettering had been cut deep, in Mydean, deep enough that the windblown grit hadn't worn it away.

**THE GREAT EXPANSE**

**NO ROAD BEYOND THIS MARKER IS KEPT BY THE CROWN.**

**NO CLAIM OF RESCUE WILL BE HONOURED.**

Redd read the last line twice. "Well. That's comforting."

Silas checked the water skins and walked on east.

The land didn't change all at once. Scrub carried on past the marker, and insects still moved under the stones. Through the afternoon, the soil paled until their tracks looked dark on it. By evening, the clouds had thinned to long strips that seemed unwilling to go any further.

On the second day past the marker, the wind started carrying a dust that clicked softly against the buckles of their packs.

Silas wrapped cloth over Wena's nose and showed Redd how to knot it loose enough for her to pant. Alaric covered the lower half of his own face. Fine grains collected in his hair and glittered like ground glass, then lost their light in his palm.

The sun stayed in the sky. Its warmth didn't.

Colour thinned under it. Redd's brown cloak went nearly grey. When Silas lit their evening meal with a small flame, it looked painfully bright against the drained earth.

Sound behaved strangely out in the open. A stone knocked loose by Wena went down a slope, and each strike arrived clear and separate long after Alaric had lost sight of it. Later, Redd called from behind a ridge, and his voice came to them as if he were standing at Alaric's shoulder. It went on after he'd stopped, thinning out across the empty ground.

Plants came back in scattered patches near the dry channels. They looked like thorn bushes until Alaric saw that every branch grew downwards first, into the ground, before turning up again at the tip. Their pale leaves had veins on both sides.

They gave the first patch a wide berth.

They saw no animals. Silas kept a hand near his sword anyway.

---

On the seventh evening after the inn, the track climbed between two ridges of dark stone.

Alaric had spent the last hour practising the same movement whenever the path widened: left foot forward, weight centred, blade meeting the attack without stopping short. The worn sword rode at his hip now. It no longer knocked his knee every tenth step.

At the top of the ridge, Silas drew and turned to face him.

"Once."

Alaric set down his share of the water and drew.

Redd moved aside with Wena. For once, he didn't offer any advice.

Silas came at him from the right.

Alaric saw his weight change before the blade moved. Fear tightened low in his stomach. He stepped, raised the sword, and finished the motion anyway.

The edges met.

Silas changed direction. Alaric followed him without looking at his feet. Their blades struck again, and Silas pulled back before Alaric's arms could lose the position.

"Better," Silas said.

That single word pleased Alaric far more than it should have.

Redd lifted the water skin. "One week in, and Al has survived two whole attacks, delivered slowly, by a man trying very hard not to hurt him."

Alaric sheathed the sword. "You could try."

"I'm in charge of the water."

Silas took the skin off him. "Now you're not."

Redd opened his mouth to object.

Then he saw what lay past the ridge.

The last of the daylight lay across the first city.

It filled the land below them.

Alaric had expected walls half-buried in sand. A few towers. Streets that only survived as a direction.

The city had been bigger than Kelmend many times over. Its surviving districts spread across the basin and climbed the far slope, crowded with buildings whose roofs leaned towards each other. Towers rose from them at angles no builder could have meant. Bridges curved down between upper storeys and vanished through broken windows.

Half of the city was gone.

The absence began near the centre and carried on past the eastern edge of the basin. Streets ended at a sheer drop. Foundations stuck out of the exposed earth like cut bone. Several buildings still stood with their front rooms open to the air, furniture still in place in chambers whose other halves no longer existed.

There wasn't enough rubble anywhere to account for what had gone.

The half that was left leaned towards the emptiness.

Walls curved inwards. Towers had been stretched until their top floors pointed at the missing ground. Whole rows of houses seemed to have slid on their foundations before hardening in place, each one pulled a little further than the one before.

Pale growth covered parts of the outer district. Trees came up through the roofs with their trunks split into flat ribbons. Vines climbed down the walls and went into the soil before their leaves opened. Nothing moved among them.

Alaric stood beside Silas and didn't reach for the satchel.

He could only look at the streets and imagine them full.

People had built those houses. They'd crossed those bridges and opened those doors, and trusted the ground under them to stay where it had always been.

"How many people lived here, do you think?"

"More than Kelmend could ever hold," Silas said. "Past that, I couldn't guess."

Redd came up onto the ridge beside them. Wena stayed pressed against his leg.

The sun sank behind them, and the warped city took their shadows.

A thin thread of smoke rose from the surviving outer district.

Silas leaned forward. "There wasn't smoke when I crossed before."

Another thread appeared further along the ruined street. Beneath it, sheltered between two buildings bent towards the missing centre, a small orange fire came into view.

Then a second.

Far below them, inside a city the world had abandoned, someone was getting ready for the night.
