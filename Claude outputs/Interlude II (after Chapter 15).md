# Interlude: II

Marta baked when she couldn't sleep.

She'd done it since she was a girl, in her uncle's kitchen, when the wind came off the hills hard enough to find the gaps in the shutters. Gerolt used to come down in his shirt and find her elbow-deep in dough at the second bell and say nothing at all, only sit at the table and wait for the first loaf, and eat the heel of it standing up so he could pretend he hadn't been waiting.

The inn was shut. The last of the drinkers had gone home an hour ago, most of them still talking about last night's fire in the old quarter. The smell of it hung over the wall all day. Somebody's roof, they said. Some sack-collector's place. Elves with lanterns.

Marta had kept her face still and poured their ale.

Now she stood alone in the kitchen with the big oven breathing behind her and a batch of dough under her hands. She pushed it away with the heels of her palms, folded it back, turned it. Pushed. Folded. Turned. Her hands knew it better than her head did. They were broad hands, freckled across the backs, with an old burn scar from the oven door that ran over three knuckles like a pale ribbon. Every scar on them was the inn's.

On the shelf above the table sat the jar of dried apple.

She'd bought it in the spring, the week the stewardship men had sent word that Darcy might be allowed one letter a season. The letter had never come. Marta kept buying the apple anyway.

*Tell her I'm sorry I won't get to take it out.*

She pushed the dough harder than it needed.

When the fire in the oven sagged, she didn't reach for the bellows. She breathed out across her open palm, and the air left her in a thin, steady stream that went through the grate and woke the coals orange. It was the smallest thing her Affinity could do. The testers had called it Eminent when she was eight, and for a year afterwards men in good coats had come round her uncle's farm asking whether she'd like to serve. Gerolt had sent every one of them away with a basket of eggs and a door shut in their faces.

Someone knocked.

Not the way the drinkers knocked, or the baker's boy from across the road. Three blows with the flat of a fist, and then the latch lifting before she'd answered.

---

Sergeant Hollis came in first, because it was his town.

She'd been feeding him for eleven years. He had a wife who couldn't cook and a son who'd gone to the frontier, and on bad nights he sat at the end of her bar and didn't talk, and she'd put a bowl in front of him without charging. He wouldn't look at her now. He stood aside from the door with his helmet under his arm and studied the flour on the floor.

"Marta," he said. "I'm sorry. It's orders."

The elf came in behind him.

Marta had heard the talk about her in the bar for days: the elf with the ruined face who'd come in off the eastern road. She hadn't seen her until now. White bandages covered half her face. The cloth over the left eye had gone brown at its centre, and round its edges the skin had healed badly, pulling her brow and cheek towards each other into a shape faces weren't meant to have. Her right arm was strapped against her chest. She walked with a hitch in her left leg that she didn't try to hide.

Four Natharul soldiers came in after her and stood along the wall, between Marta and the stairs.

The elf looked round the kitchen. At the oven. At the dough. At the jar of apple on its shelf. Then she nodded to one of the soldiers, and he stepped forward and set something down on Marta's table, in the flour, beside the bread.

A breastplate. Dented across the front. There was blood dried along one edge of it that wasn't old enough to be black.

"This was in a house in the old quarter," the elf said. She spoke the human tongue as if every word tasted of something spoiled. "The house of a man who collects sacks. He collects them from the baker across your road."

"Half of Kelmend buys from that baker," Marta said.

"A Faint in the quarter saw a provision cart at the broken well three mornings ago. Before the first bell. It carried your seal." The elf's one eye didn't leave her face. "It met a limping boy, a man in stolen armour, and the sack collector. And a dog."

Marta wiped her hands on her apron.

"I take my cart out most mornings. The workers' postern knows it."

"Where did they go?"

"I don't know who you mean."

The elf came closer. She moved slowly, because of the leg, and she didn't seem to mind that everyone in the room had to wait for her.

"The farm east of here," she said. "The man who lived there did this." She lifted her good hand and touched the edge of the bandage, very lightly, the way you'd touch something that still belonged to someone else. "An old man with a farmer's sword. The garrison tells me his name was Gerolt, and that he was your uncle."

Marta looked at the ruined face for a long moment.

"Then he died well," she said.

Hollis made a small sound by the door. The elf didn't.

"Where did they go?"

"I don't know."

"You're lying."

"Then you've wasted your walk."

The elf held her eye a moment longer. Then she said something in her own tongue to the soldiers, the layered speech that always sounded to Marta like three people talking through one mouth, and two of them came and took Marta by the arms.

They walked her to the hearth.

It was a good hearth. Gerolt had helped her father lay it, before either of them was grey. Broad flagstones, worn smooth by forty years of pots and boots and the cat that had slept there before this one. The soldiers pushed her down onto her knees in front of it and held her wrists out, one each, and pressed her palms flat on the stone.

The elf knelt on her good knee beside her. She laid her own hand flat on the flagstone a finger's width from Marta's.

"Where did they go?"

Marta said nothing.

The stone moved.

It didn't crack. It softened, the way dough softens under the heel of a hand, and rose round her fingers, warm from the fire. For one strange heartbeat it felt almost like being held. Then it began to close.

It closed slowly. The elf was taking her time. Marta felt the stone fill the gaps between her fingers and press them together, then press harder, then harder than that, until the first knuckle went with a small, dull pop she felt all the way up to her teeth.

She didn't scream. She'd decided that on her knees, in the time it had taken them to walk her across the kitchen. She clamped her jaw and breathed through her nose and looked at the fire.

Every candle in the kitchen bent sideways at once.

Flour lifted off the table in a white sheet. The pot lids rattled on their hooks. The shutters strained at their latches, and the soldiers holding her arms glanced up at them, and one of them tightened his grip. Marta could feel the wind in her. All of it. Eminent. Enough to throw every one of them out through the front of the inn and into the street.

Past the soldier's shoulder, Tam was standing on the bottom stair in his nightshirt. Twelve years old. The potboy. He'd come down at the noise.

Hollis was by the door with his helmet under his arm.

There were fourteen rooms upstairs, and nine of them had people sleeping in them.

Marta let the wind go.

The candles straightened. The flour settled over everything, over the dough and the breastplate and the soldiers' boots, soft as the first snow.

"Where did they go?" the elf asked again, exactly as she had the first time.

The second knuckle went. Then the rest of the first hand, all together.

Marta looked at the fire and said nothing at all.

---

It could have been a long time. It could have been no time. When the stone finally let go of her hands, it did it all at once, flowing back down flat into the flagstone as if it had never moved, and her hands came away with it.

She didn't look at them. She knew better than to look at them yet.

The elf stood, slowly, favouring the leg. She looked down at Marta with no more expression than she'd had when she came in.

"You loved them," she said. "The boy. The girl in the fort. Your uncle."

Marta said nothing.

"That's why it doesn't work on you." The elf said it as if she were noting the weather. "It works on people who love someone else."

She spoke to the soldiers again, and one of them went out into the yard.

He came back with Hob.

Marta knew Hob. He brought her peat from the cutting on the eastern moss twice a week, and she paid him a copper over the price when she could, because he had a daughter and no wife and lived out in the quarter past the wall. He was Faint. There were blue-green bruises round both his wrists from the harness, and he stood in her kitchen doorway looking at the flour and the blood and her hands, and his face went the colour of the ash under the grate.

His girl came in holding the back of his coat.

Marta had seen her a dozen times, stirring the quarter's big pot out by the wall with both hands. She was nine or ten. She had a raw red line all the way round her throat, where something had been fastened too tight for too long.

The elf didn't touch the girl. She didn't even look at her.

She only laid her hand flat on the kitchen floor.

The girl sank.

Not fast. The boards didn't break. The flagstones beneath them simply gave, the way the hearth had, and the girl's bare feet went down into the floor to the ankle, and then to the shin, and she made a small surprised sound and grabbed at her father's coat with both hands, and kept sinking.

"The farm," the elf said to Hob, in the same flat voice. "The boy. Where did they go?"

"Hob," Marta said. "*Hob* —"

"The inland road." It came out of him in one rush, as if it had been sitting behind his teeth all night waiting. "Before dawn. I was out at the cutting. I saw them on the track past the fields where it goes to the inland road. Three of them and a dog. The boy was limping. The one with the sacks was carrying him half the way. That's all, that's everything, please, that's all I saw —"

The elf lifted her hand.

The floor gave the girl back. It pushed her up out of the stone as gently as it had taken her, and set her on the boards with her feet whole and grey with dust, and the girl stood there in her father's coat-tails, shaking, and didn't understand what had happened.

"There," the elf said to nobody in particular. "Nobody needed to be hurt."

She looked at Marta's hands when she said it.

Then she turned and went out, slowly, favouring the leg, and her soldiers went after her. Hollis went last. At the door he stopped and turned his helmet round in his hands and opened his mouth.

"Get out, Hollis," Marta said.

He went.

Hob didn't. He stood in the doorway with his daughter pressed against his legs and looked at Marta on her knees by the hearth.

"I'm sorry," he whispered. "Marta, I'm sorry, she's all I've —"

"I know." Her voice came out steadier than she expected. "I know she is. Take her home, Hob. Go on."

He looked at her a moment longer. Then he picked the girl up, though she was too big for it, and carried her out into the dark.

---

Tam was still on the stairs.

"Go back to bed," Marta told him.

"Your *hands* —"

"I know about my hands. Go back to bed, love. Don't wake the guests."

He went up, looking back at her the whole way.

Then she was alone in the kitchen, with the oven breathing behind her and flour over everything, and she finally looked down.

She'd known what she'd see. It didn't help.

The fingers were wrong. All of them. They bent in places fingers didn't bend, and the skin had split over three of the knuckles, and when she tried to close either hand, nothing happened except a pain so enormous and so white that it seemed to come from outside her body. The old burn scar that ran across her right knuckles like a pale ribbon was still there. It was the only part she recognised.

She would never knead dough again. She knew that the way she knew the weather.

The jar of dried apple had fallen off its shelf when the wind went through the room. It had broken on the floor, and the slices lay scattered through the flour, pale curls of them, all the way to the hearth.

Marta looked at them for a long time.

Kelmend had taken her uncle and burned him on his own field. It had taken her girl and put her on a ship with a man who heated collars. Tonight it had taken her hands, in her own kitchen, in front of her own hearth, and the man she'd fed for eleven years had stood by the door and looked at the floor.

And the boy was on the inland road with a limp and a dog, and now they knew it.

She tried to pick up the nearest slice of apple. Her fingers wouldn't do it.

So she breathed out across the floor instead.

The air left her in a thin, steady stream. The slice lifted out of the flour and rose into the space in front of her face, and turned there, slowly, in the lamplight. She held it there. It was the smallest thing her Affinity could do. The testers had called it Eminent. Men in good coats had come to her uncle's farm for a year.

She'd told herself, all her life, that she was only an innkeeper.

Marta watched the slice of apple turn in the air, and her ruined hands lay in her lap, and she didn't cry this time.

She thought about the elf's face. About the one good eye, and the leg, and the road.

She thought about how far the wind could carry a thing, if it wanted to.
