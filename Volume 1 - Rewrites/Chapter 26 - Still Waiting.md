# Still Waiting

"Please go," the man said, and the door began to close.

It didn't slam. He closed it carefully, as you'd close the door on someone sleeping, and that was worse.

Alaric had carried a whole speech across the Expanse: who they were, who had sent them, what they'd brought. It went out of him all at once. The gap narrowed to the width of a face. Then a hand.

"Water always finds its way to the ocean," he said.

The door stopped.

Nothing moved behind it. Above them, the water ran on in the channel along the top of the arch, and the rope creaked. Alaric kept his eyes on the door.

Through the gap he could see one eye, part of a cheek, and the fingers of a hand on the edge of the wood. The fingers had started to shake.

"Who told you to say that?"

"She did," said Alaric.

The fingers pressed white against the door. Then they let go of it.

The door opened.

The man didn't look at Alaric first. He looked past him, up the old river road and down it, then across at the aqueduct steps. A child sat on the bottom step in the shade of the arch, barefoot, with a heel of bread in both hands, watching the street.

"Inside," the man said. "All of you. The dog too."

---

The room had been scrubbed until the wood had gone pale.

It was one long room. At the front, under the window, stood the table. At the back was the kitchen: a hearth, a pot hook, and flagstones instead of boards.

A stair climbed the side wall. Books stood on the shelves in oilcloth jackets, and in the corner a water clock let one drop at a time fall into a copper bowl.

Everything had a place. Nothing looked as though it had left its place in years.

The table was laid for breakfast. Two bowls. Two cups. Two chairs.

"Two chairs," Redd said, before he could stop himself.

"I don't have a great many visitors." The man slid the bolt across and stood with his back to the door. "I'm Helmi. You know that, or you wouldn't have knocked. Who are you?"

"Alaric."

"Redd."

Silas had already found the wall beside the window, where a gap in the shutter showed the street. "Silas."

"And the dog?" Helmi asked.

"Wena," said Alaric.

Wena had found the space under the table and lain down in it, nose on her paws, as if she'd been coming here all her life.

Helmi didn't sit, so none of them did.

"Where did you see her?"

"In Kelmend," said Alaric. "In the fort."

"When?"

"Three weeks ago. About."

"About," Helmi repeated.

"Twenty-two days," said Silas, without taking his eyes off the street.

"Thank you." Helmi looked back at Alaric. "Where in the fort?"

"A cell under the command level. There were maps all over the walls. She'd been working on them."

"Of course she had." It came out dry, almost amused. Then it didn't. "Was she well?"

Alaric opened his mouth and didn't know what to put in it.

"All of it," said Helmi. "Please. I've had thirty years to imagine what they've done to her. Whatever you saw, I'd rather have it than what I've made up."

So Alaric told him.

"She's thin," he said. "When she stood up, her legs weren't steady. She kept one hand near the desk, in case."

Helmi nodded once.

"She wore her sleeves down to her wrists. When one of them caught, there were burns underneath." Alaric made himself go on. "Some were thin. Like someone had drawn them with a fingertip. Some went all the way round her arm."

Helmi's hands hung at his sides. The fingers closed once, on nothing.

"Who?"

"Gilmot," said Silas. "The captain who owns her."

Helmi flinched at the word. He didn't correct it.

"Eminent Fire," Silas went on. "He's careful with her hands. He needs them."

"Careful with her hands," Helmi repeated, like a man writing something down to look at later. "And her voice?"

Alaric thought about it. "Quiet. She decided every word before she let it out."

"She did that at ten." Something moved at the corner of Helmi's mouth. "It took her half a supper to ask for the salt."

"She folded the paper," Alaric said. "When she was thinking. Under one finger."

Helmi turned to the window.

Silas moved along the wall to give him the shutter. Helmi didn't take it. He stood with his back to all of them, looking down at the two cups, while the water clock dripped and dripped again.

When he turned round, his face was composed. His hands weren't.

"You got as far as her cell," he said. "Why is she still in it?"

"We came to get her out," said Alaric. "Silas and me. Marta had a road ready past the wall. Food. Coin." He looked down. "This dog."

"Marta?"

"An innkeeper in Kelmend. Darcy's friend. Since they were girls, Marta said."

Helmi took that in slowly. "She has a friend," he said, turning it over.

"She said no," said Alaric.

"No," said Helmi.

"Gilmot told her that if she ever left him, he'd burn Kelmend's Faint quarter," said Alaric. "Hundreds of people." He hadn't meant to say the next part. It came out anyway. "Children."

"And she believed him," said Helmi.

"He meant it," said Silas. "I heard him say it to a Natharul officer."

Helmi shut his eyes.

"She'll have done the sum before you'd finished asking," he said. "Hundreds against one. She never could leave a sum alone." His voice didn't change. His hand found the back of one of the two chairs and held on. "Where is she now?"

"They flew her east," said Alaric. "To the frontier, on an airship. Gilmot went with her."

"With her," said Helmi.

"He keeps her close," said Silas. "Every day she's his, the quarter lives. That's how she put it."

The water clock dripped. Out in the road, someone was calling the price of onions.

"She said something to me, too," Silas said. "While I was trying to carry her out of there against her will."

Helmi looked at him.

"She said my decisions always leave someone else to bury the dead."

"Did she."

"She was right."

For the first time since the door, Helmi looked at Silas properly. Whatever he found there, he nodded at it.

Redd had been quiet longer than Alaric had ever known him be quiet. He was looking at the table.

"Is the other cup your wife's?" he asked.

"Redd," said Silas.

"My wife died twenty-six years ago." Helmi didn't say it unkindly. "She never used that cup."

Redd looked at the small cup. His ears went red to the tips.

"Did she say anything else?" Helmi asked Alaric. "For me."

Alaric had lost Darcy's words once, in the worst of the heat in the Expanse. He'd walked a whole hour with a hole where they should have been. When they came back, they'd been about water.

"She said to tell you that water always finds its way to the ocean."

"Yes."

"And then she said, 'Then tell him I'm still waiting.'"

Helmi sat down.

He sat in the chair facing the small cup. He reached out and turned the cup a little on the table, so its handle pointed where it had always pointed.

"I set it out," he said. "Every morning. In case she —"

He didn't finish.

His hands had begun to shake so hard that the cup chattered against the wood. He took them away from it and put them in his lap and held them there.

No one said anything.

Wena came out from under the table and laid her chin on his knee.

After a while, Helmi put one hand on her head. He didn't look at her. He was looking at the cup.

---

When he stood up again, he was somebody else.

"Darcy didn't send three men across half of Mydea to repeat two sentences to her father," he said. "What did she send you to find?"

Alaric looked at Silas. Silas nodded once.

Alaric lifted the satchel strap over his head.

"Not on the table," said Helmi. "Not like that. Wait."

He went to the stair and knelt, stiffly, and dragged a chest out from under it. It was locked. He drew a cord out of his collar with a small key on the end of it. The key had been round his neck the whole time, and Alaric thought it had probably been there every day for thirty years.

Inside the chest, everything had its own place. Cotton gloves, folded. Brass rings with lenses in them. A thin silver rod. A notebook. A heavy book bound in grey leather, with a sun stamped on the spine.

"They never asked for my tools back," Helmi said. "I don't think it occurred to them I'd want them again."

He shook a clean cloth out over the table. Then he stopped, and moved the two cups to the windowsill, one at a time, carefully.

"It didn't occur to me either."

He pulled on the gloves. Alaric unwrapped the core and set it in the middle of the cloth.

It hadn't changed since the farm: a clear shell as big as two cupped hands, thick as a thumb, with a black centre hanging in it. It looked like nothing. It looked like a fisherman's glass float with the bottom of a well inside it.

Helmi didn't touch it straight away. He bent over it and looked. Then he looked through one of the lenses, and then through another.

His hands had stopped shaking.

"Where did you get it?"

"Gerolt's farm," said Alaric. "Near Kelmend. The field."

"What field?"

"There are machines in the wheat," said Alaric. "Nobody has a name for them. Silver, with no joins anywhere."

"Go on."

"The soldiers are clearing the field with hooks and lamps. They drag the dead into stacks." He didn't look at anyone. "We crawled into one of the machines at night, with the whole camp round us. It was behind the thickest armour, near the middle. Where she said it would be."

"She told you where to look?"

"She drew it. Two circles."

"She remembered." Helmi said it to the core, not to them. "I used to tell her about the Vaults at bedtime, instead of stories. She'd ask what the oldest thing down there was, and I'd make it older every night." He changed lenses. "How deep was it seated?"

"Deep. Behind braces."

"Who's touched it with bare skin?"

"Me," said Alaric. "Redd. Silas, the outside."

"Has it ever been wet?"

"No," said Alaric.

"Heated?"

"I heated the housing to get it out," said Silas. "There was a thread of orange in the middle. A breath or two."

Helmi's head came up. "It lit?"

"Once," said Alaric. "When I lifted it out. It's been dark ever since."

He didn't say anything else. Silas looked at him across the table and didn't say anything else either.

Helmi wrote in the notebook. "Has it been dropped?"

"Once," said Redd.

Helmi looked at him over the lens.

"Onto a blanket," said Redd. "From about this high. It was a very gentle drop. Me and Al both apologised to it."

"Al and I," said Helmi.

Redd stared at him.

"Forgive me," Helmi said. "Habit. I corrected my daughter for ten years, and then she started correcting me back." Something caught in his throat, and it came out as a laugh, short and rusty, and he looked surprised to hear it. "She was better at it than I was."

Redd grinned. It was a real one, the first Alaric had seen on him since the arch.

Helmi took up the silver rod. He ran its tip round the shell, slowly, the whole way, with his head on one side as if he were listening to it breathe. Then he held a lens close to where the clear shell met the black.

"Well?" said Silas.

"It's real," said Helmi.

"Real how?" said Redd.

"Real in the only way that matters to an archivist." Helmi sat back. "It isn't from the Campaign. They've sold the Campaign piece by piece on every pilgrim road for twenty years, and I've looked at most of it."

He turned the lens over in his fingers.

"It isn't a forgery. I spent fifteen years in the Vaults learning every trick a forger has. And it isn't anything Natharul makes." He looked at the black centre. "I don't have a mark for it. Not in that book. Not in my head."

"Then what is it?" Alaric asked.

"I don't know yet." He said it as though he'd been handed a present. "And I can't find out here. Darcy was right about that, too. The first mana poured into a core leaves an impression, and water will hold its shape, if the water is still enough."

He nodded at the water clock. "This isn't. There's a pool under the Library that has been still for longer than Favale has had a name. That's where it has to go."

"The Library," said Redd. "The big grey one."

"The big grey one." Helmi pulled off the gloves, one finger at a time. "Which belongs to the temple."

He laid the first glove in the chest.

"Every door is kept by priests, and the watch stand behind the priests. Pilgrims may walk through the public halls on feast days and look up at the ceilings. Only the sworn go below." He laid the second glove beside the first. "I haven't been sworn for thirty years."

"So we can't get in," said Redd.

"I didn't say that," said Helmi.

Silas turned from the shutter.

"There's one person," Helmi went on. "Idony."

"Who's Idony?" said Redd.

"The Keeper of the Vaults. She was given my keys the morning after they took them from me." He closed the notebook. "We came up together. Two junior archivists with ink on our cuffs. She held Darcy the day she was born. She's had thirty years to become important."

"Will she help?" Alaric asked.

"She owes me."

"For what?" said Silas.

Helmi didn't answer straight away.

"The night before the examination," he said, "we tried something foolish with the silver cup. It didn't work. In the morning they asked who had done it, and I told them I'd done it alone."

"Did she let you?" asked Silas.

"She let me."

"That's not a friend," said Redd.

"No," said Helmi. "It's worse. It's someone who knows to the drop what she owes."

He folded the cloth over the core, corner by corner.

"Not today," said Silas. "Not in daylight. Not with a dog the whole road watched go for a witch."

Helmi's hands stopped. "What does that mean?"

"There was a woman at the Pilgrims' Gate yesterday," Alaric said. "Giving out water for the temple. She poured some into her own hands for Wena."

He kept going, because if he stopped he didn't think he'd start again.

"This morning they hanged her from your arch. Wena knew her. She strained at the end of Redd's rope the whole time, with half of Favale watching."

Helmi listened without interrupting. When Alaric had finished, he looked at the gap in the shutter, where a strip of the arch showed.

"I saw her," he said. "This morning. From the step."

He didn't say anything else about it.

"There's a child on the steps over the road," Silas said. "Been watching your door since we came."

"There's always a child on the steps." Helmi put the core back into the chest and locked it. "First light, then. Tomorrow."

"First light," said Silas.

---

Around midday, Silas decided they needed bread.

"And I want to know what the city's saying," he said. "You two go."

Redd looked at Alaric, then away. "Why us?"

"Because I'm not spending the afternoon listening to the two of you not talking." Silas dropped coins into Redd's palm. "Bread. Something that isn't bread. And keep your ears open."

"And the dog?" asked Redd.

"Stays here. The dog's famous."

Outside the door, Redd said, "He thinks he's clever."

"He is clever," said Alaric.

"That's what's annoying about it."

It was the most they'd said to each other since the arch.

The midday bell rang before they'd gone ten paces.

It came down from the temple hill in one long note that went on after it had stopped, and the whole road stopped with it. A carter pulled up his mule. A woman at a window stopped beating a rug. All along the old river road, people touched two fingers to their foreheads, waited, and went on.

Alaric didn't know to do it. Redd did it a beat behind everyone else.

The road had changed since the morning. Men on ladders were tying banners to the poles along the river side: white cloth with a gold sun on each, long enough to brush the heads of the people walking under them. Between the poles, others were hanging lanterns glazed with thick yellow glass. A boy lit one to test it. Even in daylight, it threw a small gold light onto the stones.

The bread seller had her stall in the shade under the aqueduct, a few doors from the arch. She had flour in her eyebrows and a voice that could have called the whole road in to supper.

"What's all that for?" Redd asked her, nodding at the banners.

She looked at him as if he'd fallen out of a cart. "The Longest Light, love. Five days."

"What happens in five days?"

"Where've you been?" She was already wrapping a loaf. "Last day of pilgrim season. Midsummer. They carry her down the stair among the sick. It's the one day in the year she comes down off the top step. The whole city turns out. You won't get down this road for pilgrims."

"Who's her?"

"The saint. Atera." She lowered her voice. "Only you say the Ruler, if there's a priest about." She leaned closer over the loaves. "They say this year she'll walk it. The whole stair, top to bottom, on her own feet. They say anyone her shadow touches will get up and follow her down."

A man buying rolls beside them snorted. "They said that last year."

"Well, this year they mean it."

"They'll mean it next year and all." He tucked the rolls under his arm. "My cousin works the palace kitchens. That Natharul lot won't touch anything cooked. Raw fruit and water, three times a day, and they send half the fruit back."

"The envoy?" said the bread seller.

"Lodged in the old palace since yesterday. And the king bowed first." He said it with a kind of disgusted relish. "In his own hall. Bowed first."

"He never."

"He did. My cousin saw it."

"Your cousin sees a lot for a man who scrubs pots."

"How much for the loaf?" Redd asked.

"Four."

"Four? That's not a loaf, that's a rumour of a loaf."

"Then go and eat a rumour somewhere else."

Redd paid. The bread seller counted his change into his hand and gave his face a long look.

"You're not from here."

"Kelmend."

"Long way to come for the Longest Light." She dropped her voice again. "You mind yourselves this week. My youngest is in the white this year, first year, and he says they've been pulling hoods since yesterday. His poor feet." She glanced towards the arch and didn't look up at it. "They're saying there were two witches. Only hanged the one."

"Where's the other one?" asked Redd.

"Gone, if she's got any sense. Or here." She shrugged. "Here for the saint, same as everyone."

Under the arch, some children were playing.

Alaric saw what the game was before he wanted to. A boy with a stick for a spear stood on the bottom step of the pier, where the child with the bread had sat that morning. Two bigger girls held a smaller one by the arms.

"By the Light the Ruler carries!" the boy shouted.

The little girl giggled, and dropped to the ground, and kicked her legs.

The others cheered. Somebody's mother called them in for their dinner, and they ran, all of them, laughing, under the woman on the rope.

None of them looked up at her. A pilgrim passing under the arch touched his sun token without lifting his eyes.

Alaric stood with the bread against his chest.

"Don't," said Redd.

"I didn't say anything."

"You don't have to. You're doing it so loud they can hear you in the temple."

"They were playing it, Redd."

"They're children. Children play what they've seen."

"And we've seen it."

"Here it comes." Redd shifted the parcel under his arm. "Go on, then. Say it. You've been wanting to since the arch."

"She gave Wena water," said Alaric. "Out of her own hands."

"You said. On the road."

"And you said good."

"I said what I thought." Redd kept his voice low, but there was an edge in it now. "That's more than you managed. At least I said what I thought."

"You thought she deserved it."

"I thought they all did," said Redd.

It came out flat. They both heard it.

"All of them," said Alaric. "Orieth? Her little girl?"

"Don't."

"Nell ran from them, Redd. From the same people who burned your village. She got further than any of us, and they hanged her here for it, and you said good."

Redd put his parcel down on the edge of the step, carefully, so he'd have his hands free.

"You want to know what I thought?" he said. "When I was eight, they came through with torches. My mam was in the yard. My da was up in the top field, and he came running, and he called for me. Twice."

His voice cracked on it. He pushed straight on through, the way he always did.

"Twice, Al. The second time meant he'd already come looking. He never got as far as the ditch. I lay in it and watched them do it. So when I see one of them swing, I don't stop and ask what she did in a kitchen."

Alaric didn't have an answer for that. He found one anyway, and it was the wrong one.

"You'd have pulled the rope yourself."

"Maybe I would." Redd stepped closer. "And you'd have held my coat while I did it. And said nothing. And felt bad about it after, so that'd make it all right."

The bread seller had stopped wrapping. The man with the rolls had gone.

They stood under the arch with her hanging above them, and neither of them said anything else. Each of them had said something true about the other, and there was nowhere to go from there.

Redd picked his parcel up off the step and walked back to the house. Alaric followed him. It was thirty paces, and neither of them spoke for any of them.

Helmi opened the door before they reached it. He'd been at the window.

"Voices carry, under that arch," he said. He looked at the loaf in Alaric's arms. "The bread's still warm. That's something."

---

The evening bell rang at dusk.

All along the old river road, shutters came down. Doors shut. Somebody went on calling a child's name for a while, and then stopped. On the banner poles, the practice lanterns were lit one after another, and the road went gold and empty under them.

"Curfew," said Helmi. "Pilgrim season. After the evening bell, only the watch walk."

They ate at the table set for two: Helmi and Silas in the chairs, because Redd insisted, Redd on the tool chest, Alaric on an upturned crate from the yard, and Wena underneath, where every scrap ended up sooner or later.

Helmi laid the table as he must have laid it every night. He put out the small cup at the place across from his own.

Then he stopped, with his hand still on it.

He picked it up again and put it in his coat pocket.

No one asked.

Silas had watched him do it. Later, while Redd and Alaric sat at opposite ends of the table not speaking, he said, "How long have you set that out?"

"Every morning for thirty years."

"Does it help?"

"No."

Silas nodded. "No."

---

The knock came an hour after the bell.

It wasn't loud. It was a flat, tired knock, from someone who'd knocked on a great many doors that week and expected to knock on a great many more.

Silas was on his feet before it had finished.

"Evening, archivist," said a voice through the wood. "Temple watch. Sorry about the hour. We hear you've got guests. We'll just have a word."

Helmi looked at Silas. Silas looked at the stair, where the swords were leaning, then at the door, and shook his head once.

Helmi opened it.

There were four of them. They came in without waiting to be asked, wiping their boots on the mat out of habit. Two of them leaned their spears against the wall inside the door, because the room was too small for spears. Their white and gold was grey with a long day's dust.

The one in front had a sergeant's knot on his shoulder and a small book in his hand. He looked tired. He smiled at Helmi.

Behind him came a heavy man with a weighted staff. Then a boy, or near enough a boy, whose white was so new it still had the folds from the chest in it. His hand kept going to the short sword on his hip and coming away again.

The last one came in slowly, and closed the door behind him.

Alaric knew his face before he knew why.

It was a patient face. That morning it had leaned on the parapet over the old river road and watched a rope.

"Now then," said the sergeant, opening his book. "Names."

"Garrad," said Silas. It was a flat, dull drover's voice that Alaric had never heard him use. "My sister's boys. Wat and Col."

Redd, who had never been anybody's sister's boy, kept his face very still.

"From?"

"Kelmend."

"And what brings you to Favale in pilgrim season, Garrad?"

"The archivist's wife's sister. She's poorly. She wanted him told, so we've told him." Silas made it sound so ordinary that Alaric almost believed it himself.

"Long way," said the sergeant, writing.

"It felt it," said Silas.

"She's not expected to last," said Helmi. His voice was quite steady. "They came to tell me."

"Sorry to hear it." The sergeant seemed to mean it, as far as it went. He looked round the room. "Tidy house."

"I don't have many visitors," said Helmi.

"No," said the sergeant. "We know."

He tipped his head at the last man, the patient one, who hadn't looked at the books or the room or any of them since he came in.

"My man here's been asking after a dog since the morning bell," the sergeant said. "Put a child on the steps across from your door with a loaf to watch for it, if you can believe that. I told him there's more dogs in Favale than priests." He gave Helmi a tired smile. "But here we are."

It went through Alaric cold. A child on the bottom step with a heel of bread in both hands. *There's always a child on the steps.*

The heavy man yawned. The boy was reading the spines of the books with his lips moving.

The patient man went to the table. He bent a little, easily, and looked underneath.

Wena looked back at him.

"That's the dog," he said.

The sergeant didn't look up from his book. "You're sure."

"This morning. Under the arch. It went for her. Pulling at its rope like it knew her." He straightened without any hurry. "Like it was hers."

The boy's hand went to his sword, and this time it stayed there.

The sergeant sighed. He closed the book on his thumb, as a man does when he knows he'll have to open it again for a longer entry.

"Well," he said. "That's a shame." He looked at Helmi almost kindly. "Archivist, you'll come with us. Your guests too. And the dog."

"On what charge?" Helmi's voice had gone very exact.

"Harbouring. Keeping company with a witch's creature, if the priests want to call it that." The sergeant shrugged. "I don't make the words."

"It's a farm dog," said Alaric. "It's only a farm dog. It —"

"Then the priests will find that out," said the sergeant. "Get your coat, archivist."

Helmi didn't move.

Silas put a hand into his coat, slowly, so that all four of them could watch it go, and brought out a fold of cloth. He laid it on the table beside the sergeant's book and let it fall open. There was more coin in it than Alaric had known Silas owned.

"For your feet," said Silas, in the drover's voice. "Long week."

The sergeant looked at it.

He looked at it for long enough that Alaric started to breathe again. His thumb moved along the edge of his book. Even the heavy man had stopped yawning.

"Sergeant," said the patient man. Only that.

The sergeant let a breath out through his nose. With one finger, he pushed the cloth back across the table. "Not this week."

Behind him, the heavy man had squatted by the table with a grunt and got his fist into the scruff of Wena's neck. "Come on, then. Out."

Wena bit him.

After that, everything happened at once, and Alaric was never afterwards able to put it in order.

The heavy man roared and dragged his arm back with the dog still hanging off it. Redd went for Wena. The boy saw a man lunge across the room, and his short sword came out, and he swung it at whoever was nearest.

Helmi was nearest. He'd stepped forward with his hands out, towards the dog or towards Redd, and the blade went for his arm.

Alaric was already moving.

He didn't decide to. He was simply between them, and his right hand closed on the blade because it was the thing in front of him.

**THOOM.**

His heartbeat hammered inside his skull.

The edge went across his palm and up the inside of his forearm, and for one breath there was no pain at all, only heat and a wide, stupid surprise. Then his hand was full of blood, and his fingers wouldn't close, and the pain arrived.

The boy stared at him. "He moved," he said. "They all moved, he —"

Silas killed the sergeant.

He did it across the corner of the table, with the knife from his belt, up under the jaw, while the sergeant's hand was still finding the hilt of his own sword. The sergeant's book fell open on the floor.

Then Silas was round the table, and the heavy man had kicked Wena loose and was swinging his weighted staff at Silas's head, blood running off his bitten wrist, and Silas stepped in, inside the swing, close, as he'd shown Alaric under the colossi, and put the knife in under the man's arm.

There was a smell like meat left too long on a griddle. The staff hit the wall. The heavy man sat down against the foot of the stair with a puzzled look, as if he'd forgotten why he'd come.

Two men had died in the time it took Alaric to understand he was bleeding.

"Door," said Silas.

He went to it and put his back against it, and that was where he stayed.

The lamp had gone over when the table jumped. Flame ran low and blue across the spilled oil on the boards. Helmi stamped on it, once, twice, with his old careful shoe, until it went out.

The patient man hadn't moved yet.

He stood with a long knife out, looking from Silas at the door to Helmi by the table, doing a sum of his own. Then he went for Helmi.

He wasn't fast. He didn't need to be. Helmi was an old man with nowhere to go but back against his own table.

Alaric hit him from the side.

It wasn't anything Silas had taught him. He threw himself at the man shoulder first, with his ruined hand clutched to his chest, and they went over together into the back of the room. A chair went with them and broke under them, and then there was nothing under Alaric's back but the kitchen flagstones.

They were hard. The man was on top. He was heavier than he looked, and he knew what to do with his weight, and Alaric didn't.

"Lad," the man said. He was hardly out of breath. "Lad. Put it down. There's no need for this."

Alaric didn't have anything to put down.

Behind them, the boy was crying.

Alaric could hear him: high, choking, saying "please" and "I didn't" and "he moved" over and over. Then his boots on the boards, coming. Alaric couldn't turn his head. The patient man had one forearm across his throat and the knife hand free, and he was looking for a way in past Alaric's arm, calmly, taking his time about it.

Then the floor heaved.

The flagstones under the boy's feet lifted, as if something underneath them had shrugged. The boy staggered, arms out, the short sword swinging wide.

Redd had got his father's sword from the stair, from beside the dead man sitting at the foot of it. He came round the table behind the boy with it in both hands, his face screwed up like a child about to cry, and hit him.

It wasn't clean. Redd didn't know how to make it clean. The first blow took the boy between the neck and shoulder and didn't stop him, and the boy turned, still crying, and Redd hit him again, and again, swearing the whole time. The boy went down against the hearth and stayed there.

"You stupid —" Redd was sobbing it. "Al, you stupid, stupid fucking —"

The patient man's knife came up.

Alaric caught the wrist with his left hand. Not the elbow. He couldn't reach the elbow. He had the wrist, and the man was stronger, and the point came down towards his face as steadily as a bucket being let down a well.

Alaric wrenched his head sideways.

The knife struck the flagstone beside his ear with a sound like a dropped plate. The jar went up the man's arm, and for half a breath his grip was loose. Alaric got his fingers round the handle over the man's fingers and twisted, and then the knife was between them, and he couldn't tell whose it was.

He pushed.

It went into the man's side, under the ribs, not far. The man grunted. He didn't let go.

"Easy," he said. "Easy, lad."

Alaric pushed again, with everything he had, his ruined hand flat against the man's chest because it was the only way he could brace himself. The knife went further. The man's weight changed on top of him. He was still talking, but the words had stopped making sense, and then they stopped being words, and then they stopped.

It took too long. Afterwards, Alaric would remember how long it took.

He lay under the weight until Redd dragged it off him.

---

For a while, the room was full of breathing.

Alaric's, fast and high. Redd's, which kept catching. Wena's whine from under the table, on and on. Silas at the door, slow and steady. There was a cut along his cheekbone, bleeding into his collar, and he didn't mention it.

In the corner, the water clock went on dripping, as it had all day.

Redd was sitting on the flagstones with his father's sword across his knees, looking at the boy.

The boy's white was new. There was a sun token on a string round his neck, like the pilgrims wore, and it had slipped out of his collar and lay on the flags in the blood.

"He was —" Redd said, and didn't finish.

Silas left the door. He crouched by the sergeant, picked the little book out of the blood, looked at the last page, and put it inside his coat.

Helmi stood with his back to the table set for two. He hadn't moved since the lamp. The toe of his shoe was black with it.

He looked at the four men on his floor. He looked at the table: the bowls, the bread, the crust Redd hadn't finished. Then he looked at the water clock.

"They'll be missed at the change of watch," he said. It was the archivist's voice again, exact to the word. "That's the second bell after midnight. We have until then."

Nobody argued.

He stepped over the heavy man at the foot of the stair, went up, and came down with a bag.

He took the core out of the chest, still in its cloth, and put it in Alaric's satchel, and slung the satchel over his own shoulder, because Alaric's hand was no good for anything now. He touched his coat pocket, where the small cup was.

Then he went to the peg by the door and took down the blue coat.

He folded it once, carefully, as you'd fold a letter you meant to keep, and buttoned it inside his own.

He didn't clear the table. He didn't look at it. He left the bowls where they were, and the broken chair where it lay, and the four men where they'd fallen, and stood by the door with his hand on the bolt.

Redd got up.

He came over to where Alaric sat against the wall holding his arm, and knelt, and tore a strip from his own good sleeve with his teeth. He bound Alaric's hand with it. He did it badly, and far too tight, and his hands were shaking so hard he had to tie the knot twice.

It was a double knot. Silas had tied one like it round Redd's arm in the Expanse.

Redd didn't say anything. He didn't look at Alaric's face. When he'd finished, he got up and went to stand by Wena.

Silas looked at Helmi.

"Where?"

"Idony," said Helmi, and drew the bolt.

---

The old river road lay empty and gold under the practice lanterns.

Every shutter was down. The banners hung still in the warm dark, and high above them the woman on the arch turned a little on her rope, and turned back. The step where the child had sat with the bread was empty.

Helmi didn't take the road. He led them along the foot of the aqueduct, pier by pier, in the shadow the lanterns didn't reach. He walked fast for an old man, and he didn't look back at his house once.

They'd gone three streets when they heard boots.

Helmi turned off without a word into what Alaric took for an alley. It wasn't an alley. It was a stair. It went down beside the pier into the dark and the sound of water, a narrow stone throat with a rail worn smooth by hands.

At the bottom was a ledge above a black pool, where the aqueduct let its water rest before the city took it.

"Down," Helmi whispered. "Against the wall."

They crouched on the ledge. Wena pressed herself against Redd's legs.

Above them, the boots came on. A lantern swung past the top of the stair. Two voices went by, complaining about their feet, and about the Longest Light, and about how they'd be on nights for the rest of the week, and how it was always the same ones who got put on nights.

The boots went on. The lantern went. Nobody moved.

Silas climbed three steps to watch the top. Helmi sat on the ledge with the satchel in his lap and one hand inside his coat, where Darcy's coat and the cup were. Redd was a shape in the dark with the dog.

Alaric turned his back on all of them.

His hand had stopped hurting the way it had hurt at first. It itched now, deep down, and it was hot. He'd thought that meant it was going bad.

He worked at Redd's double knot with his teeth until it came loose, unwound the strip, and held his hand up to the thin light that came down the stair from the lanterns.

An hour ago it had been open to the bone. He'd seen the bone.

The cut was closed.

It wasn't healed. It was an angry red line across his palm and up the inside of his forearm, puckered and raised, the skin still tender at the edges. But it was closed, the way a cut is closed after a week, or two. He pressed it with his thumb, and it held.

He sat in the dark and looked at it.

He didn't know what it meant. He knew what it would mean to anyone who saw it. He thought of the arch, and a dog that had known a witch, and a sergeant's book with a last page in it, and a city that had hanged a woman for less than this.

He wound Redd's strip back round it, tighter than before. He tied the double knot again with his teeth and his good hand, so that it looked exactly as Redd had left it.

Then he held the hand against his chest in the dark, and didn't tell anyone.
