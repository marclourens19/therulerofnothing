# Foramen

The second fire vanished as soon as Alaric started down the ridge.

It came back three turns later, lower and further to the left, burning between two walls that leaned towards each other without quite touching. The first stayed hidden behind a tower whose upper floors had folded across the street.

Silas led them along the ridge until he found a way down that had once been a road. Most of its fitted stones had split under pale roots. Sand filled the gaps and smoothed the middle into a narrow path that wound towards the surviving outer district.

"Someone's moved in," Silas said.

Redd hitched the strap of his travelling sack. "Unless the creatures have learned to cook."

"Last time I came through, that district was open to the wind. No wall. No roofs."

Alaric followed the line of his chin. Below them, three low buildings had been joined by a wall of brown blocks. The blocks didn't match the grey stone round them. One stretch had been laid recently enough that it was still darker than the rest.

"How long ago were you here?"

"Twelve years. Thirteen, maybe." Silas stepped over a root lifting through the road. "I didn't mark the day."

"Were the creatures here then?"

"Some of them."

Redd moved closer to Wena. "I was hoping for more than that."

"You wanted the comforting version."

"I was hoping age had made you generous."

"It hasn't."

The city closed round them before they'd reached its lowest street.

What had looked crowded from the ridge was impossible to hold in one look from inside it. A broken arch stood over the road with room enough under it for six wagons abreast. Past it, narrow houses had been built against the outer wall of a hall whose columns were thicker than Gerolt's cabin. Their roofs had fallen long ago. Ribbon-trunked trees lived in the rooms now, roots going out through the doors and back in through the windows.

Alaric kept expecting the scale to become ordinary once he was walking through it.

It didn't.

A stair climbed the face of one building and ended against open air. Stone channels crossed above the street, carrying nothing. Under one archway, where the rain couldn't reach, faded colour clung to the plaster: blue figures painted round a circle where the plaster had fallen away, each of them holding something that time had taken out of their hands.

Wena stopped beneath them.

Redd let the rope slacken while she smelled the ground. Dust whitened her nose. She sneezed twice and went on.

The missing half of the city stayed in sight between the buildings. Every street angled towards it. The closer they came, the more Alaric noticed the pull in the walls. The upper stones had shifted further than the ones beneath them. The windows narrowed on one side. The metal gutters had stretched into points.

Pressure gathered behind his forehead.

It was slight enough to mistake for thirst. He drank when Redd passed him the bottle, and handed it back without mentioning the pain.

Silas had started watching the windows instead of the road.

"Do you see anyone?" Alaric asked.

"No."

"They can see us," Redd said.

Silas didn't ask how he knew.

A chunk of stone struck the road in front of Silas's boot.

It had come from above. Alaric found no movement anywhere along the roofs before a woman called out from somewhere behind the broken arch.

"Stop there."

Silas stopped.

Redd shortened Wena's rope and brought her in against his leg. Alaric kept his arms well away from the sword at his hip.

"We only need shelter for the night," Silas said. His voice carried further than it should have and came back thin from the empty windows. "We'll pay for food and somewhere off the road."

"There is no road here."

"A room, then. A yard. Anywhere out of the wind."

The point of a spear rose above the wall to their right.

Another came up out of a roofless room ahead. A man stepped into the doorway holding it in both hands. He wore a patched brown coat and no armour, and the spear was shaking so hard it scraped against the stone beside him.

More people came out after him.

An old woman with a woodcutter's axe. A boy younger than Alaric, holding a bow with no arrow on the string. Two men blocked the nearest side street with a plank held between them, though neither end reached the walls.

They didn't look like bandits springing an ambush.

They looked like people who'd been warned in time to be frightened.

The unseen woman spoke again. "Put down your swords."

Silas turned his head slightly, counting the places the voice might have come from.

"No."

"Then turn round."

"Dark will catch us before we're out of the basin."

"That's your mistake."

The man in the doorway swallowed. His spear came down towards Silas's chest.

"Let the two behind you come forward," he said. "You can leave without them."

Redd stared at him. "Without *us*?"

"Both of you. The dog as well."

Silas glanced back at them. His good thumb hooked under the strap across his shoulder, as if he meant to hitch his pack higher. "They're already free to walk wherever they like."

"He says that now," someone muttered from the roofless room.

Redd pushed his hood back. "He's spent a week telling me where to sleep, when to walk, and when to keep my hand off my sword. I can see how you'd be confused."

Silas gave him a flat look.

"But no," Redd said. "He doesn't own me. He just complains like he paid too much."

The spear in the doorway stopped shaking for a beat. The corners of its owner's mouth twitched before he forced them flat.

Alaric unbuckled the belt that carried his sword.

"What are you doing?" Silas asked.

Alaric laid the sheathed sword on the road and stepped back from it.

"Pick it up."

"They warned us before they threw anything sharper than a stone."

"That doesn't make the next thing harmless."

"No. It means they'd rather we left than died." Alaric turned to the man in the doorway. "My name's Alaric. Redd's my friend. Silas brought us through the Expanse because he knows the way to Favale. None of us came here looking for you."

The people along the walls murmured to each other. Alaric couldn't make out the words.

"And the dog?" the old woman asked.

Redd laid the end of the rope across Wena's back. "She belongs to herself. She mostly picks me."

Wena smelled his boot.

"A convincing demonstration," Silas said.

Stone clicked somewhere past the barricade.

The people by the side street moved aside.

At first Alaric thought another wall had shifted behind them. Then grey-white fur caught the last light where stone should have been. A long muzzle came out from under a hood of faded cloth, followed by shoulders broad enough to fill the passage.

The beastman walked upright, a metal-banded staff in one hand. Old scars crossed his muzzle and vanished into the fur under his left cheek. Pieces of armour covered one shoulder and one forearm, none of them from the same set. The rest of him was brown cloth, wrapped and belted for the dry country.

He stopped behind the plank blocking the street.

It came up only to his thigh.

Redd's hand moved towards his father's sword. Alaric reached across and caught his sleeve before he touched it.

The beastman's muzzle turned first to Redd's hand, then to Alaric's.

"If they had come to collect us," he said, "there would be chains behind them."

His voice was quiet. The people nearest him lowered their weapons before anyone gave an order.

The man in the doorway didn't. "Kurdag, he's got more Fire in him than anyone on this wall. I can feel it from here."

Silas's expression didn't improve. "I can hear you. I also haven't used it."

"You haven't needed to." Kurdag stepped round the plank. His clawed feet made almost no sound on the sand. "Why are you going to Favale?"

"We have business there," Silas said.

"People usually call it business when they don't want to explain themselves."

The satchel pressed against Alaric's coat beneath his cloak.

Silas didn't offer anything more. "We need one night. At dawn, we leave."

"We don't take coin."

"What do you take?"

Kurdag glanced along the walls at his people. "What we need. What we can use. Sometimes a promise that our fires were never here."

"No one hears about them from us."

Silas untied the smaller pouch strapped under his pack. "We've got clean bandage cloth. Marta packed salve as well."

"You may need both before you reach the capital."

"We've enough to share."

Alaric didn't argue.

Redd crouched by his sack. After some digging under his spare shirt, he pulled out a little cloth roll of waxed thread, with two steel needles pushed through its edge.

"I've got these," he said. "They were meant to keep my coat together, but the coat seems determined to die."

Kurdag looked at the frayed cuff at Redd's wrist. "It has made considerable progress."

Redd looked up at him. "I like you already."

The corners of Kurdag's mouth drew back far enough to show the tips of his teeth. It took Alaric a heartbeat to recognise it as amusement.

"You may come in until morning," Kurdag said. "Your weapons stay sheathed. If anyone asks you to leave their home, you leave it. If you see something you don't understand, you ask before you touch it."

He turned to Alaric's sword on the road.

The old woman got to it first. She tested its weight and tucked it under one arm.

"This one stays with me till morning."

Silas started to object.

Alaric spoke first. "All right."

The plank was pulled away.

Kurdag stepped through and waited for them.

"Welcome to Foramen," he said.

---

Foramen had been built inside the rooms of the dead city.

Brown walls divided the surviving halls into smaller homes, roofed with stretched cloth or reeds round open courtyards. Scavenged doors had been cut down to fit their new frames.

The fires Alaric had seen from the ridge burned in clay-lined pits, each with a metal cover close to hand. As the strangers passed, children were called in off the street and bundles disappeared through doorways.

"They will need time," Kurdag said.

"So will we," said Silas.

"You've had practice at being unwelcome."

"More than most."

Redd kept close enough to Wena that the rope hung slack between them. "Does Foramen mean anything?"

"In Kozmagari," Kurdag said. "An opening, through which something survives."

Redd glanced towards the missing half of the city. "Cheerful."

"The old name died with the people who used it. We needed another."

They passed under a channel held up on cracked pillars. Water ran along it in a line no deeper than Alaric's finger. Three women stood on a platform beside the nearest cistern, each holding both hands above a stone bowl. Moisture gathered against their palms, drew together into separate drops, and fell.

One drop.

Then another.

A child waited with a clay jug. When the bowl had filled enough to pour, he carried it to plants growing in a roofless house. The soil there was dark, nothing like the pale ground beyond the city.

"You brought that here?" Alaric asked.

Kurdag nodded. "A sack at a time. There's good earth west of the marker, if you know where the patrols stop looking."

At the far end of the garden, a row of small stone cairns stood along the wall. Each was no taller than a child's knee. Some had a scrap of cloth tucked under the top stone. One had a wooden spoon.

Alaric didn't ask. He counted them without meaning to. Eleven.

Near the cairns, two Earth users were pressing soil into the gaps of a cracked wall. A boy watched them, then copied them. The ground pushed up under his hands and lifted a whole row of onions out of the bed.

One of the women caught the nearest stalks before they fell over.

"Less," she told him. "You're holding the roots up, not sending them to the moon."

The boy muttered an apology. She showed him how to spread his fingers and waited while he tried again.

"What classification is he?" Alaric asked.

"We haven't tested him," Kurdag said. "His mother won't have it. Nor will I."

"Then how do you know what he should learn?"

"We ask him what he wants to learn. After that, we stop him uprooting supper."

The boy tried once more. This time only the loose soil moved.

On his way back past the wall, he laid his hand on top of one of the cairns without looking at it, the way you'd touch a doorframe you'd walked through a thousand times, and went on.

Redd had fallen behind.

Alaric found him watching the boy. His father's sword hung at his hip, and his hand was near the hilt. It made Alaric think of the ridge of earth that had risen under Redd's palm on the bank above the farm, when the king came down the stair.

Kurdag noticed as well. He said nothing.

They reached a long building whose front had been rebuilt in brown stone. Light spilled from the open middle of its roof. The old woman carried Alaric's sword inside.

A second figure came out to meet them.

She was an elf.

Her hair had been cut close on one side of her head. Half of her right ear was gone, ending in a ragged scar. Her sleeveless work tunic showed pale lines crossing both arms and disappearing under the cloth at her shoulders.

Redd stopped in the doorway.

"You let *elves* live here?"

The people inside turned to look at him.

The elf didn't step back. "I'm an elf. And I live here."

Redd let Wena's rope drop against his leg. "Your people burned my village. I watched them do it. A week ago they burned my house."

Silas moved to stand between Redd and the nearest resident with a spear. He didn't touch his sword.

Kurdag stayed by the door. "Her name is Orieth. She sits on this council."

"That doesn't change what she is."

Orieth pulled the edge of her tunic aside from the thickest scar, the one crossing her collarbone. "The elf who owned me cut off half my ear because I spoke while he was speaking. He never cared that I was an elf too."

Redd's anger didn't go away. But it lost the easy direction it had carried in with it.

"That doesn't make Natharul innocent."

"I know." Orieth went back to a low table and began sorting dried leaves into separate bowls. "You need somewhere to sleep. I was willing to give you one. If sharing a roof with me is too much, leave before dark. I'm not going anywhere."

"Redd," Alaric said.

Redd turned on him. "You didn't see what they did."

"No. I didn't." Alaric thought of Gerolt's cabin going up orange behind him. "One rider got away from Gerolt and came back with more. I watched them burn his home."

"And if she'd been with them?"

"Was she?"

Redd started to answer, then stopped. "That isn't the point."

"Then what did Orieth do?"

Redd's voice dropped. "It's easy for you to pull them apart. You didn't grow up watching them burn everything you knew."

Alaric fought to answer without raising his voice. "I know I wasn't there. But she's standing here offering us a bed, and you haven't even asked her where she came from."

Redd looked at Orieth again.

She went on sorting leaves.

"I'm staying," he said.

"Then keep your sword in its sheath," said Orieth. "My daughter lives here."

Redd moved his hand off the hilt.

He didn't apologise. Orieth didn't ask him to.

Kurdag came in behind him and leaned his staff against the table.

The council began its questions.

---

They gave Foramen the smallest useful piece of the truth.

Silas explained that the patrols had made the royal road unsafe, and that Inrandeel would kill anyone as inexperienced as his companions. He said they were going to Favale for information. He added nothing to Alaric's account of Gerolt's farm, and didn't mention what was wrapped in the satchel.

When Kurdag asked whether anyone was following them, Alaric answered.

"There were soldiers searching for us near Kelmend. We haven't seen them since we came into the Expanse."

"Why were they searching?" Orieth asked.

Alaric weighed how much of the answer could bring danger down on this place.

"I survived something they don't understand."

Orieth's hands stopped. "Do you understand it?"

"No."

"That's usually when powerful people get most interested."

Silas set Marta's medicine pouch on the table. "If we'd seen anyone behind us, we wouldn't have come down."

"Tracks last for days out here," Kurdag said. "And the ruins give plenty of cover to anyone following them."

"We changed direction whenever we could."

"Then we'll double the watches tonight."

"If you decide we're too dangerous to keep," Silas said, "tell us before the last light goes."

"You wouldn't clear the basin now," Orieth said. "The creatures past the outer streets start hunting after sunset."

Redd glanced at the doorway. "What keeps them out of here?"

"If we knew that, I'd have put it on the council table before the bread." Orieth went back to her leaves. "They stop before the lived-in streets. We make sure no one's outside those streets when they do."

A thin woman with her left leg splinted between two lengths of wood gathered up the medicine and Redd's needles.

Orieth offered them bread, roasted roots, and a place to sleep in an old storehouse. Silas asked what they owed.

"You've already paid," she said. "If your medicine's useless, we'll reconsider."

"Marta packed it."

Orieth lifted one shoulder. "I don't know Marta."

"Then smell it again. She knows what she's doing."

For the first time since Redd had walked in, Orieth nearly smiled.

Alaric looked at the medicine pouch in the splinted woman's hands. Marta's neat knots were still on it. The soldiers at the inn had said the elf with the ruined face would go round to Marta's after closing. That had been a week ago. Marta knew he was Empty.

He didn't let himself follow the thought any further than that.

---

They ate in the central courtyard, under the open sky.

The evening meal belonged to everyone. Pots came from separate homes and went back empty. A woman whose Fire was barely strong enough to keep a flame on the end of her thumb moved from pit to pit, relighting the tinder whenever the wind took it. Two Wind users drew the smoke away from the covered rooms in uneven breaths. Every few minutes the air turned and pushed it straight back under the roofs, and people coughed and complained and went on eating.

Redd sat with Wena between his feet.

Orieth's daughter appeared by the table after the first bowl had gone round. She looked four or five, with black hair tied back from her pointed ears with a strip of red cloth. When she held out a hand towards Wena, Redd pulled the dog closer.

The girl stopped.

From beside the cooking pit, Orieth called, "Leave the dog alone, little one."

Redd looked from the girl to Orieth. Then he let the rope go.

"Wait. Let her smell your hand first."

The child crouched and held out her knuckles.

Wena smelled them. Her tail struck Redd's boot once.

"She hasn't rejected you," Redd said. "Food will settle the matter."

The girl took a piece of roasted root from her bowl and held it out. Wena took it before Redd could object.

"You've ruined the whole process," he told the dog.

The girl smiled and buried both hands in the fur round Wena's neck. Wena leaned into her so hard that the child sat down, and Redd caught her bowl before it tipped.

Across the courtyard, Orieth watched. She said nothing. Redd kept one hand near Wena's collar while the girl stroked her back.

Alaric ate his bread and listened to the cistern outside, taking in water.

Drop after drop struck the channel. Each one was small enough to ignore. Together, they'd filled the covered reservoir beyond the doorway.

Here, Darcy's three drops would have gone in with all the others.

Kurdag lowered himself onto the end of the bench. Sitting, his shoulders were still level with several of the people standing nearby.

"You've been watching them a long while."

"I've never seen Faints living like this."

"Most people haven't. That's how we stay alive."

Alaric watched a Water user carry an empty bowl back to the platform. "In Kelmend, they took a woman from her family because she could only make three drops."

"What's her name?"

"Darcy."

"Where is she now?"

"At the frontier by now, most likely. The army needs her mind for the war, so they took it." He looked round the roofless hall. "Is everyone here Faint?"

"Most were called that. A few were never tested. Some came here with enough power that any other city would have given them rank."

"You?"

Kurdag's claws rested across the end of his staff. "Especially me."

"Why do they listen to you?"

"Because I can find water where the ground looks dead. Because I can hold a wall up while people get out from under it. Because I've made enough mistakes to listen before I decide anything." He pointed his staff at Orieth. "And because she reminds everyone I'm not the only person in Foramen with an opinion."

Orieth didn't leave the fire. "He needs frequent reminding."

"There. You see how tirelessly she serves."

The girl beside Wena laughed.

Kurdag stood. "Walk with me, Alaric."

Silas had been checking the strap round his burned hand. He stopped.

"Where?"

"The edge." Kurdag spoke to Alaric, not to Silas. "If he wants to see it."

Only when Kurdag named the edge did Alaric notice how long the pressure had been sitting behind his forehead.

"Why?" he asked.

"Because you've spent the whole evening measuring the distance to it."

Silas shifted on the bench. "We leave at dawn. He doesn't need a tour through unstable ruins."

"No," Alaric said. "But I want to see it."

Silas looked at the darkening streets past the doorway. "Stay inside the watch line."

"We will," Kurdag said.

Alaric stood. The satchel stayed hidden under his cloak.

Redd was showing Orieth's daughter where Wena liked to be scratched. He looked up as Alaric left, but didn't call the dog away from the child.

---

The lived-in district ended three streets from the vanished ground.

No wall marked the line. The brown repairs simply stopped. Whole doorways stood open with no cloth or wood behind them. The fires of Foramen were still visible between the ruins, shrunk to small orange points.

Kurdag carried no lamp. He crossed the broken floors without hesitating and tested the doubtful stretches with the iron tip of his staff. Alaric put his feet where it had already been.

The headache grew. It crept back from his forehead and spread above his ears. He kept going until the houses opened out round a broad stone avenue.

The avenue ended halfway across the city.

There had been no collapse. The fitted stones ran on under Alaric's boots and stopped along a line far too straight to be damage. Beyond it, the earth dropped sheer into the dark. The far side was so distant that the buildings still standing there looked pale and thin in the last light. Past them, the land rose again and ran on towards the heart of the Expanse.

A house stood at the edge with two of its rooms laid open.

One held half a stair. Every step ended in a smooth face where the rest of it should have gone on. The other room held a stone table cut clean through the middle. There was nothing beneath it. No broken half anywhere Alaric could see.

He went closer.

Pain drove through his temples.

He stopped beside the house and pressed his tongue against the backs of his teeth until the sickness eased.

Kurdag had stayed several paces behind him. "Does it hurt everywhere in the city?"

Alaric turned round. The question had been asked too exactly.

"How did you know?"

"You keep stopping and pressing your teeth together." Kurdag planted his staff. "You were doing it on the way down. The water didn't seem to help."

Only then did Alaric notice that his fingers were resting on the smooth edge of the open room. He drew them back.

"It started on the road down."

"Has it happened in other ruins?"

"This is the first one I've been inside."

"Do you remember this place?"

Alaric waited for the doorway, for the woman's laughter, for the tap of a loose latch. None of it came.

"No."

Kurdag was quiet for long enough that Alaric wondered whether he could feel the same absence Marta had found.

"There's another reason I brought you away from the others."

Alaric thought of the satchel. His cloak was still closed over it. No light came through the cloth.

"There is nothing coming from you," Kurdag said.

Alaric forgot the headache.

"Only Silas and Marta know that," he said.

"Nothing at all," Kurdag went on. "A Faint presence can be hard to feel. Yours isn't hard to feel. It isn't there."

"Marta called me Empty."

Kurdag said the word once, slowly. "Empty."

Alaric didn't like hearing it from someone strong enough to know exactly what he was sensing.

"Will you tell the council?"

"Do you want me to?"

"No."

"Then I won't."

There was no bargaining in it.

Alaric looked at the divided table. "You don't think they should know what they've let in?"

"They need to know about the soldiers following you. You've told them that." Kurdag leaned on his staff. "The rest can wait until you want to tell them."

"The rest of the world wouldn't agree."

"Then the rest of the world can keep its opinion." His voice didn't change. "A demon lord's soldiers killed my daughter."

He went to the vanished line and looked down into the dark below, as if making sure it had stayed where he'd left it.

"She was five. They came into our settlement before sunrise. I had enough Earth to tear their road apart. Enough to bury their first rank. None of it put me beside her when she needed me."

Alaric didn't ask how she'd died.

"A woman found me after the fighting," Kurdag said. "She'd been tested Faint. Her Earth could barely stir the dust under one hand. She crossed a burning house to pull the stones off my face."

The iron bands round his staff had been mended in several places. He turned it until one of the seams lay against his palm.

"She tore her hands to pieces getting me out. I told her to stop. She told me to save my breath." He rubbed his thumb along the mended band. "I'd been ready to stay under those stones."

He was quiet for a moment.

"I was born with enough Earth to break a road apart. The woman who saved my life could barely move the dirt under her hand. One of us understood strength. It wasn't me."

Alaric thought of Gerolt opening his door to a stranger covered in blood. Of Darcy staying in her cell while the captain held the Faint quarter over her head. Of Redd watching his house burn, and then laughing about its chimney.

"What happened to her?" he asked. "The woman."

"She lived. She refused to follow me out here. Said living beyond every road sounded exhausting." A low laugh roughened the end of it. "She was usually right."

The daylight faded another shade. Across the absence, a stretched tower threw its shadow into empty air.

"No one in Foramen will measure you," Kurdag said. "That doesn't mean everyone will know what to do with you. Tell them when you're ready. Not because you think you owe it to them."

"I don't know when that'll be."

"Then don't tell them tonight."

Alaric took a step nearer the vanished line.

The pain sharpened at once. His heartbeat struck inside his skull.

**THOOM.**

The divided room blurred. He forced his eyes open and found the table still cut, the stair still leading nowhere, the absence giving him no face and no voice in return.

**THOOM.**

Kurdag's staff came across in front of him before his foot reached the last stone.

"That's close enough."

Alaric stopped. The pain crowded behind his eyes, but his legs held.

"What happens further in?" he asked. "Nearer the middle?"

"There's a crater big enough to swallow several districts. The land round it changes while you watch. Paths don't always come back to the same place."

"Is there a way round?"

"Not one I'd call safe. Going round the western edge would add days, and the patrols have started going places they didn't go last season."

"We haven't got days to add."

"Being in a hurry won't make that pain any safer."

Alaric stepped back. The next beat of his heart came without filling his skull.

"Favale's past the Expanse."

"You still have to reach it alive."

"The royal road's got soldiers searching it for us. Silas says Inrandeel would kill us." He waited until the divided table held one shape again. "That leaves this."

Kurdag's ears flattened back. He watched Alaric steady himself against the wall.

"Then stay away from the middle," he said. "If the pain changes, tell your friends before it puts you on the ground."

Alaric nodded.

"Has it put you on the ground before?"

Alaric glanced back at the distant fires.

"Yes. I'm trying not to make a habit of it."

"Good. Start by walking back before dark."

Kurdag lifted the staff out of his way.

They left the vanished city behind them.

---

Foramen's lights had multiplied while they were gone.

Small flames sat in windows cut into the brown walls. Wind users guided the smoke up through the open roofs in pale streams. At the cistern, the women had finished and covered the channel. Someone was playing a three-stringed instrument beside the biggest cooking pit, repeating the same unfinished tune while a knot of children argued about the missing note.

Alaric and Kurdag came into the main street together.

Silas was waiting outside the council building. "You took your time."

"He asked too many questions," Kurdag said.

"That sounds like him." Silas studied Alaric's face. "Are you all right?"

"Another headache. Near the missing ground."

"A memory?"

"No. Only pain."

Silas glanced at Kurdag.

"It eased when we came back," Kurdag said. "I wouldn't take him near that edge again tonight."

"I'm thinking about tying him to the next camp."

"Use a good knot," Redd called from the fire. "He heals quickly."

Wena lay beside him with Orieth's daughter asleep against her side. Redd had folded his cloak under the child's head. Orieth sat close by, mending the frayed edge of a water skin with one of the needles he'd given away.

Redd saw Alaric looking and pointed at the girl. "I tried moving her. Wena objected."

Orieth drew the thread through the leather. "The dog opened one eye."

"She was very clear about it."

The tune reached its missing note and fell apart again. A dry wind came in off the western street, carrying dust across the fire pits.

Alaric started towards them.

Wena woke before he got there. Her head came up from beside the child. Her nose turned towards the western ruins.

The musician played the first two notes again.

Wena was on her feet so fast that the girl rolled against Redd's cloak.

Redd caught the child before her head hit the ground. "Easy."

Wena pulled away from him. The fur along her spine rose. A low sound started in her chest.

Alaric stopped.

He'd heard that growl before. Under the roots by the river, with Wena pressed against him and the elven riders searching the bank above their heads.

"Redd," he said. "Take her inside."

Wena barked.

It cracked across the square. The girl woke up crying. Orieth dropped the water skin and snatched her daughter away from Wena's rope.

Wena barked again, fighting Redd's grip on her collar.

Silas was already moving towards the western street.

A whistle came from the outer ruins. One high note. Two lower ones answered it from opposite roofs.

The people round the fires began getting to their feet.

A lookout came running under the broken channel. He reached Kurdag breathing hard.

"Riders. Five of them."

"How far?"

"Past the western arch. They came through the dead street without lamps."

Silas drew his sword. "They followed us."

"Can you be sure?" Kurdag asked.

Alaric watched Wena straining towards the dark.

"She knows someone out there."

Another whistle, closer than the first.

Kurdag struck the end of his staff once against the road.

"Every light," he called. "Put them out."

Foramen moved.

Metal covers dropped over the cooking pits. Water hissed on coals. Wind folded the smoke down under the roofs and let it out again through hidden vents. One by one, the lights that had given Foramen away began to vanish from its windows.

Silas caught Alaric by the shoulder on his way past. "Stay with Redd."

Alaric turned to the council building instead.

The old woman had left his sword on the low table.

He crossed the room, buckled the belt round his waist and closed his hand on the worn grip. Outside, Redd had drawn his father's blade and was keeping Wena behind him. Orieth carried her daughter towards the inner rooms. Kurdag walked west with his staff.

Between two dying fires, five mounted shapes came into the far end of the street.

The last light in Foramen went out before Alaric's blade cleared its sheath.
