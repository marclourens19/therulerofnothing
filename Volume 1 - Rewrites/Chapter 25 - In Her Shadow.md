# In Her Shadow

The woman tipped the whole bucket over Redd's head before he'd finished asking her for a cup of it.

He stood there with water running off his nose and his chin and the ends of his hair, his mouth open. Then he started to laugh. It came out cracked and hoarse, and it didn't stop. He sat down in the mud beside the goats' trough and laughed with his face turned up and his eyes shut, while the woman let the bucket back down the well and wound it up again.

She was small and old and quick, and she had a milking stool tucked under one arm the whole time, as though she'd forgotten she was carrying it. She'd been watching them since noon. Alaric had seen her from a long way off, a dark shape in a doorway at the top of a slope of grass, not moving, while they came up out of the last of the sand towards her.

The sand had given out slowly. It thinned until there was hard ground under it. Then came scrub, grey and spiked, the first living thing in days that wasn't trying to eat them. Then grass. Alaric had knelt in the first of it without deciding to, and put his hands flat on it. It was cool underneath. It smelled green, which he hadn't known a smell could be. He'd stayed there until Redd came back and hauled him up by the collar.

He hadn't looked north once since the rock. He'd decided that at dawn, with the voice still in his ears, and he'd kept to it all day.

The woman handed him the second bucket, and he drank from the side of it until she took it away from him.

"Slow," she said. "Or you'll have it all back up."

Wena didn't listen to her. She drank from the trough until she was sick in the grass, then went back and drank again.

Silas had rinsed his mouth and spat, and now he drank slowly, in small swallows, with his eyes closed. When he'd finished, he held out a coin. The woman looked at it, and then at the three of them, and took it, because she wasn't a fool.

"Nobody comes out of there," she said. "Not walking."

"We were lucky," said Silas.

She looked past them at the pale line of the Expanse, and back at Redd laughing in her mud, and didn't argue.

She fed them goat's cheese and flatbread hot off a stone, and told them what the road was like, since they hadn't thought to ask. Pilgrim season. The Favale road would be thick with them by the last shrines, and the gates worse. "The saint's been on the steps every dawn, they say. They lay the sick where her shadow falls." She rubbed her knee. "I'd go, if these would take me."

"What saint?" Alaric asked.

She looked at him as if he'd asked her what water was. "The saint."

She let them sleep in the goat shed. Redd sat up with Alaric again without being asked, his back to the wall and Wena across his feet, and fell asleep sitting up long before Alaric did.

Alaric lay in the straw and listened north.

The goats shifted and breathed. Outside, the grass moved in a wind that didn't carry sand. The voice didn't come. Once a goat's bell clinked in the dark, and he was half sitting up before he knew what it was.

---

They saw Favale late the next afternoon, from the top of the last rise before the plain.

Alaric stopped walking. Pilgrims parted round him on both sides and closed again in front of him, and he didn't notice them.

The city filled the plain from one side to the other. A river ran through it, broad and brown and slow, with barges on it. Walls ran round it, and beyond the walls the houses went on out into the fields, because nobody had told them where the city was meant to stop. Long lines of arches came striding in from the hills to the west, high over the roofs, carrying water into it.

In the middle of it all, on a hill that must once have been the only hill for miles, stood the temple.

It was gold. Its roofs were gold, stacked one above another up the hill, and the late sun came off them so hard that it hurt to look. A stair ran down the western face of the hill, white, wider than any street, all the way to the roofs below. There were people on it. From here they were grains of something spilled.

Lower down, by the river, stood another building with towers. It was older and darker, and its tallest tower didn't reach the temple's lowest roof.

"The king's got the palace," Silas said, before Alaric could ask.

"And up there?"

"Everything else."

Redd had come back for him. He studied the temple with his hands on his hips, squinting.

"Somebody's very pleased with themselves," he said.

Alaric looked for the Library, because Darcy had said the word and he'd carried it all the way across the Expanse. Silas saw where he was looking and nodded at a grey mass on the far side of the river, square and heavy, with no gold on it anywhere.

"That one."

It looked like a place built to keep things, and not to give them back.

The road went down into the plain and filled up. By the last shrines it was a crowd, and by the gate it was a crush. The sick came on doors and hurdles and on the backs of their sons. People wore sun tokens on strings round their necks and sang in patches, one group taking up a song as another let it drop. At the back of every group walked the ones carrying the most, and no one offered to take a load from them. Alaric had seen people walk like that in Kelmend's Faint quarter.

"Pilgrims' Gate," Silas said, when the walls were close enough to show the arch, choked with people. "Heads down."

Alaric kept one hand on the satchel and Wena's rope wrapped round the other. Silas walked in front with his shoulders loose and his eyes everywhere. Redd stayed close beside Alaric.

At the gate, the watch were stopping women.

They wore white and gold and stood in pairs under the arch, and they weren't looking at the men. They were pulling back hoods. They tugged scarves down off the ears of every woman who came through, young or old, looked, and let her go. A girl of about twelve started to cry when one of them took hold of her scarf. Her mother held her still by the shoulders and looked at the ground while he did it.

"Ears," someone said behind Alaric. "They're looking at ears."

"For the witch." A man's voice, low and eager. "On the pilgrim road, yesterday morning. Two days out, it was. An elf. Burned a thatcher from the inside, right in front of his little girl. Fire coming out of his eyes, they said."

"Two of them," someone else said. "Two elves. One with her face burned black."

"The other one talked like a lady."

"The Last Dark take them both."

Redd turned his head and spat into the dust.

Three filthy men and a dog walked under the arch between two of the watch, and neither of them looked round.

On the far side, Silas didn't slow, but Alaric saw him check the arch behind them twice.

"What?"

"Nobody looked at us."

"Isn't that good?"

"I've come in through this gate a dozen times," said Silas. "It's never been this easy."

Inside the gate, the temple was giving water away. A line of servants in grey aprons, each with a gold sun stitched on the bib, stood behind a row of buckets and ladled water into whatever the pilgrims held out: cups, bowls, cupped hands. Silas and Redd pushed on through the crowd, watching the watch. Alaric stopped and held out the tin cup from his pack.

The woman who filled it was an elf.

At first he only saw a thin woman in a grey apron, with a scarf tied close round her head and an old burn down one side of her face, shiny and pulled tight at the corner of her mouth. She filled his cup without looking at him. Then she looked down, saw Wena at his knee with her tongue out, and crouched.

She didn't use the ladle. She poured the water into her own cupped hands and held them out.

Wena drank. It was what Alaric had done for her in the heat, under the leaning column, and she drank from this stranger's hands exactly as she'd drunk from his: four laps, then she licked the palms and between the fingers until there was nothing left. The corner of the woman's mouth went up, on the side the burn didn't reach. She caught it and put it away.

When she straightened, her scarf slipped.

Her ear was long, and came to a point. He'd seen an ear like that before, under a woman's hair, in the one room he remembered from before the wheat field.

She saw him see it. Her hand went up and pulled the scarf back into place, and she looked at him, and she was afraid of him, and she couldn't hide it quickly enough.

He didn't say anything. He drank the water, and she turned to the next pilgrim in the line, and he went on after the others.

"Al!" Redd was waiting with Silas where the street opened out. "Keep up. You'll get trodden on."

Alaric caught them up. He didn't look back at the buckets. He'd done her a small kindness, he thought, keeping quiet. It was the last time she crossed his mind until the evening.

---

Silas's place was still there.

It had no sign. It was a green door in a row of doors behind the western market, down three steps, and the green had faded to the colour of old moss. Silas stood in front of it longer than he needed to. Then he pushed it open.

Inside it was low and warm and full of steam, with a lamp on every table and a smell that turned Alaric's stomach over. There were six tables. Round every one of them stood chairs.

Redd stopped in the doorway. He laid one hand on the back of the nearest chair, gently, as you'd greet a horse you'd heard good things about.

A woman came out of the back with a tray. She was nearly as wide as the doorway, with cropped grey hair and forearms mottled red from years of steam. She saw Silas and stopped.

"You owe me for a chair," she said.

"Hild."

"Twenty years. And you walk in like you'd stepped out for bread." She put the tray down without looking at it.

Redd had already sat down. He looked from her to Silas. "What happened to the chair?"

"Somebody went through it," said Hild.

"Somebody was rude," said Silas.

"He threw a grain merchant through my good chair because the man said something about his girl." Hild looked at Silas, and then at the door behind him, as though someone else might still come in. "Where is she? The one with the freckles."

The pots went on steaming in the back. Redd studied the grain of the table.

"Kelmend," Silas said.

"You didn't bring her."

"No."

Hild nodded slowly. Then she slapped the table so the tray jumped. "Well. You've brought me three mouths and a dog that look like they've been dug up. Sit down. What'll it be?"

"Pies," said Redd, before anyone else could answer. "Meat ones. With the edges folded over."

"I've mutton and onion."

"Is it really mutton?"

Hild stared at him. "What else would it be?"

"I've been lied to about mutton before," said Redd, and Silas made a sound that in anyone else would have been a laugh.

The pies came on plates. They were real plates, thick and white and chipped round the rims, and Hild set Redd's down on the side of his good hand without saying anything about the other one. Redd held his plate up to the lamp before he'd let anyone touch the food.

"A plate," he said. "Al. Look. A plate."

"I see it."

"Bite a hole first. Let the steam out. I mean it."

Alaric bit a hole. Steam came out of it in a thin white thread. He waited as long as he could bear to, which wasn't long enough, and burned the roof of his mouth on the first bite, and sat there with his eyes streaming while Redd laughed so hard he had to hold on to the table.

It was mutton. It was salt and fat and pepper, and onion gone soft and sweet in the gravy, and pastry that broke into flakes on his tongue and fell into his lap. He ate the whole thing without stopping. He'd eaten a lot of food since the wheat field and been grateful for every mouthful of it: Gerolt's stew, Marta's bread, Foramen's roots, the dry crusts of the Expanse shared out in halves. He had never once, eating any of it, thought *again*.

He reached for a second pie before he'd decided to.

Redd saw. He put his cup down and pointed across the table.

"There it is."

"What?"

"You want another one." Redd sat back, enormously pleased, as though he'd baked it himself. "Told you. You can have more than one thing."

Alaric looked at the pie in his hand. He found he was smiling, and that he couldn't stop.

Silas paid. He paid for the second round of pies and the third, and for the ale Hild brought without being asked, and for the bowl of scraps she put down under the table for Wena. He didn't smile. But he sat with his back to the wall and his legs stretched out in front of him, and once Alaric caught him looking round at them, at Redd with gravy on his chin and Wena asleep with her head on Alaric's boot, and he looked like a man who'd come in out of the cold.

Redd stood up with his cup.

"To chairs," he said.

Silas looked at him.

"To chairs," Redd said again, louder, so the two tradesmen at the next table turned round. "And whoever thought of them. And to Hild, who had the sense to buy more than one, in case somebody gets thrown through it."

Hild, passing, cuffed him round the back of the head, not hard.

"To chairs," said Alaric, and lifted his cup. After a moment, so did Silas.

It hadn't been a long life, so far, the one Alaric could remember. But it was the best night of it. He knew that while it was happening, and he thought that must be rare.

It was late when Hild came to clear the plates. She stacked them along her arm, talking over her shoulder to the tradesmen.

"Temple watch pulled an elf out of their own kitchens not an hour since. Scrubbed their floors eleven years, she did. Took them till now to remember they'd got one." She said it as she might have said the price of flour had gone up. "They'll have her off an arch by the morning bell. They don't waste time in pilgrim season."

Alaric's hand stopped on his cup.

He thought of a ladle, and water poured into cupped hands, and a scarf slipping.

There'd be more than one elf in a city this size. There must be. He didn't know how many, and he could have asked Hild, and she'd have told him.

Redd was laughing at something Silas had said, or at something Silas hadn't said. The lamp was warm. Wena was asleep on his foot, and his mouth still tasted of onion.

He didn't ask.

Redd reached across him for the last pie. "You don't mind," he said, already eating it.

"No," said Alaric. "Go on."

---

Hild had rooms over the kitchen. Theirs had two beds and a window that looked north over the roofs, and Silas took the bed nearest it without discussion.

Redd lay down on the floor across the doorway, with his blanket and his father's sword and the dog.

"There's a bed," said Alaric.

"I know. I've been dreaming about it for a week." Redd shifted his shoulders against the boards. "But if you walk tonight, you'll have to walk over me, and I'm very hard to walk over. Silas offered me the cord. I said I'd sooner lie on you."

The lamp went out. Across the room, Silas's breathing slowed and deepened. The noise of the market went on under the window, then thinned, then stopped.

"Al," said Redd, out of the dark.

"Mm."

"Heard her again? Your voice?"

Alaric looked up at the paler square the window made on the ceiling. A long way north, past the goat farm and the grass and the last of the scrub, the sand was still falling into the crater and never landing.

*"——Alaric."*

"No," he said.

"Good." Redd was already slurring towards sleep. "Good. Night, Al."

"Night."

It was the first time he'd lied to Redd. Afterwards he lay awake and listened north. Nothing came. He listened anyway, until he couldn't tell whether he was awake or only dreaming that he was listening.

---

Silas woke them in the dark.

"Helmi," he said. "Before the streets fill."

Hild was already up and doing something to a fire. She gave them bread with dripping on it, and told Silas the way to the old river road although he said he knew it, and let them out into a morning that hadn't started yet.

The streets weren't empty. They were full of people going the other way.

They came out of every door and alley in twos and threes and whole families, carrying lamps, carrying their sick, and all of them went uphill, east, towards the temple. Alaric and the others went downhill against them.

The whole western city lay in shadow. The sky behind the temple hill had gone the colour of a pearl, and the hill and its roofs stood black against it, and the shadow they threw lay over every street between here and the walls. Alaric's breath smoked in it.

At the bottom of the street, where it opened onto a square, he looked back.

From here he could see all the way up to the western stair. It was covered in people. They lay on it in rows, on doors and blankets and straw, from the foot of it to the top, with lamps set down between them. The square below was full of people standing, and none of them made a sound.

Something white came out at the top of the stair.

It was a person, very small with the distance. The sun was coming up directly behind the temple, and the first of it caught round her edges, so she was lit from behind, thin and burning at the rim. She came to the top step and stopped.

Nobody in the square moved. Alaric had stopped too. So had Silas, he realised, and Redd.

She stood there and looked out over the western city: towards them, towards all of them, towards nothing in particular, the way anyone might look at a morning. Then she turned and went back in.

That was all.

On the stair, a man began to weep, loudly. In the square, people let out their breath and started talking all at once.

"Atera," whispered a woman near Alaric, with her hands pressed together against her mouth.

The old man beside her hushed her. "The Ruler," he said. "You say the Ruler."

Silas had already started walking again. Alaric turned and followed him down into the shadow, and behind them, up on the hill, a bell began to ring. One deep note, and then again.

---

The western aqueduct came down out of the hills on legs of pale stone and crossed the old river road on a single tall arch, higher than the houses on either side. Darcy had said her father's house stood where they met.

They couldn't see it for the crowd.

The bell was still ringing. The road was packed from wall to wall, and more people were pushing in behind them all the time, and all of it lay in the temple's shadow. The watch had cleared a space under the arch with the butts of their spears and were holding it. Along the top of the arch, beside the water channel, ran a narrow walkway. A rope hung down from it into the cleared space. It swung a little, and settled.

Redd went up on his toes. "What is it?"

Alaric knew. He'd known since the bell.

They brought her up the stair that climbed the nearest pier. It was built into the stone, a steep flight of steps with the middle of each one worn hollow by feet, and she came up it between two of the watch with her hands tied in front of her.

She was in a grey shift. Her feet were bare. Someone had taken her scarf. Her hair was tied back with a strip of kitchen cloth, and her ears were uncovered for the whole road to see. The burn ran down one side of her face, shiny and pulled tight at the corner of her mouth.

**THOOM.**

His heartbeat slammed inside his skull.

It was her.

Wena knew her too. She pulled at the rope and whined, high and thin, and kept pulling. Redd hauled her back against his legs without looking down. "Stop it. What's the matter with you?"

The watch walked her out along the walkway to the middle of the arch. Her shift was torn at the back of the neck, and when she turned, Alaric saw the scars across her shoulders, pale and thick and crossing each other. He'd seen scars like that on Orieth's arms at Foramen. Redd had been standing next to him.

A priest came out onto the walkway after them, in white, with a gold sun worked on his breast. He held a folded paper in one hand and a chain in the other, and he waited for the bell to stop before he spoke. When it stopped, the whole road went quiet.

He had a pleasant voice. It carried without effort.

"Hear the judgement of the temple," he said. "The elf called Nell in the lower kitchens of this house, her own name not known, who fled her lawful masters in Natharul and was sheltered eleven years by the charity of the temple, did on the pilgrim road, two days since, work witchcraft upon a man of the Four, and burned him from within before the eyes of his child."

A sound went through the crowd, low and long, like wind going through a field.

Alaric heard it, and did the sum.

The witch had been two days up the pilgrim road on the morning before yesterday. Yesterday afternoon, this woman had stood inside the Pilgrims' Gate with a bucket and a ladle, pouring water into her own hands for a dog. She'd have had to cover two days of road in a day and a half, then walk back in through a gate where the watch were pulling back every hood in Favale looking for her, and pick up a bucket.

She couldn't have. Anyone who'd spent as long on it as he had would know it. They'd said two women, too, and there was one woman on the arch.

No one on the road was thinking about it.

He could say so. He could push to the front and shout it up at the priest. Say what? That he'd seen her at the gate. That a dog knew her. Then the priest would look down at him, and the watch would look at him, and someone would ask who he was, and where he'd come from, and whose dog that was. And he would be the survivor of the field, standing in the middle of the Holy Capital with a war core in his satchel.

Silas's voice came back to him from the grey light under the colossi. *That's who it works on. Someone who doesn't want to hurt you.*

Everyone on this road wanted to. There was nothing here to turn.

He stayed where he was.

"By the Light the Ruler carries," said the priest, "the Light is withdrawn from her."

He lifted the chain. A sun disc hung from it, bigger than the tokens the pilgrims wore, and its face had been scorched black. He put it over her head so it lay against the grey shift. One of the watch fitted the noose. He did it carefully, settling the knot behind her ear, the way a man might fasten a child's cloak against the cold.

She was searching the crowd, face by face, for someone who ought to have been there. Someone from the kitchens, perhaps. Her eyes passed over him and didn't stop.

Wena whined again.

The woman's eyes came back. They found the dog, straining at the end of Redd's rope, and her face changed. It might have been the first thing she'd recognised all morning.

They pushed her off the walkway.

She didn't jump. They didn't wait for her to.

The rope snapped straight, and she didn't go still. Her tied hands came up to the rope at her throat. Her feet found the stone of the pier, and scraped on it, and lost it, and found it again.

Somewhere near the front, a woman began to sing.

The crowd took it up. It was a hymn, slow and simple, one that everybody seemed to know without having learned it, about the Light keeping the faithful through the dark. They sang it in four verses with their faces turned up to the arch. The woman on the rope was still moving through the first verse, and through the second.

On the walkway, the watchman who'd fitted the noose leaned his elbows on the parapet and watched the rope, with the patience of a man watching a line in a river.

Alaric didn't look away. It was the least he owed her. It wasn't enough, and he knew that too, and he kept looking.

In the third verse, the sun cleared the temple roofs. The light came down through the western city street by street, catching the chimneys and then the walls and then the road, until it reached the arch. The singing swelled. They thought it had come to watch.

By the last verse, her feet had stopped looking for the stone.

The hymn ended. In the quiet after it there was only the creak of the rope, and the water running on in the channel above her head, which had never stopped.

"Good," Redd said.

He said it quietly, looking straight up at her, and he meant it.

A man beside him nodded.

Alaric turned and looked at Redd. Redd didn't look back.

---

The crowd came loose slowly, from its edges. People went back to their mornings. Some of them touched their sun tokens as they passed under the arch. Some didn't look up.

Alaric pushed through them. He didn't wait to see whether the others were behind him. He heard Wena's claws on the stones and knew they were.

They were almost clear before Redd spoke.

"Don't."

Alaric hadn't said anything.

"Don't look at me like that." Redd's voice was low and rough. "You don't know what they are, Al. You don't even know where you come from. I was eight. They came through our village with torches and I watched from a ditch while they killed my mam and da in the yard, and nobody hanged anybody for it. Nobody even wrote it down."

"She was at the gate yesterday," Alaric said.

Redd stopped walking.

"With a bucket. Giving out water. She gave Wena some, out of her own hands." His voice didn't sound like his. "The witch was two days up the road the morning before. They said so at the gate. You heard them. She couldn't have been both."

Redd looked down at Wena. Wena looked back up at him and wagged, once, uncertainly.

Redd's face didn't know what to do. Then it closed.

"Then why didn't you say?"

Alaric didn't answer.

"You knew." Redd's voice dropped. "You knew she hadn't done it, and you stood there, and you didn't say a word. At least I meant what I said."

He walked on. Wena went with him, because she always did.

Silas had stopped beside Alaric. He hadn't said anything the whole time. He'd been watching the crowd, Alaric realised, not the arch: the watch, the priest, the faces that turned.

"Keep your voices down," he said. Then: "They've got their witch."

"She wasn't."

"She is now." Silas watched the watchmen at the foot of the pier, coiling up the spare rope. "They've got their witch. They'll stop pulling back hoods."

He said it as though it were good news. For the three of them, it was.

---

Helmi's house stood at the foot of the aqueduct, where it met the old river road. It was tall and narrow, three storeys of old brick squeezed in between a chandler's and a wall. Its shutters were closed. Its step had been scrubbed white.

From that step, if you lifted your head, you could see the middle of the arch.

Alaric didn't lift his head.

He knocked. Once, twice, three times.

The fourth wouldn't come.

He stood with his knuckles an inch from the wood. Four knocks had come to the door of Gerolt's cabin, slow and deliberate as a funeral bell, and after them, the arrows. He put his hand down.

Redd stood a pace behind him, looking anywhere else. Silas stood at the corner, watching the street.

The door opened.

The man in the doorway was tall and thin, with the stoop of someone who'd spent his life under low ceilings. He might have been sixty-five. He was clean-shaven with great care, and there was a tiny nick on his jaw where the razor had caught him that morning. His clothes were old, and very clean. He looked at the three of them and the dog as though he hadn't been expecting anyone, and wasn't expecting anyone now.

Behind him, the room was small and perfectly kept. Alaric could see a table, scrubbed pale, laid for breakfast. There were two cups on it. One of them was smaller than the other.

By the door, on a peg at the height of Alaric's chest, hung a coat.

It was a child's coat, blue once, faded almost to grey. It had been brushed. Darcy was a grown woman. Alaric had sat across from her in a cell and watched her fold the edge of a paper under one finger. The coat on the peg would have fitted a girl of about ten.

The man followed his eyes to it, and back.

"If it's about the woman on the aqueduct," he said, "I didn't see anything."
