# Where Sand Falls

Alaric landed on his back for the third time, and the sky above the colossi still hadn't decided to be morning.

"Again," Silas said.

He stayed down a breath longer than he needed to. The sand was cold under his shoulders, the last cold they'd feel until nightfall. High above him, the broken cheek of the west-facing figure had caught the first grey light, as if the stone had woken before any of them.

"You stepped back," Silas said.

"You swung at my head."

"That's when you step in." Silas held his sword out, still sheathed, with a strip of leather tied over the guard so nobody lost a finger before breakfast. "Step back and you meet the end of the swing, where it's fastest. Step in and you meet the part that's slowest. Close to the hilt, there's no edge that can reach you."

"There's the hilt."

"The hilt hurts. It doesn't open you." Silas touched the inside of his own arm, above the elbow. "Left hand here. Trap it against you. Right hand on the wrist. Turn it out, the way it doesn't want to go, and keep turning till the fingers open. Don't pull. Pulling's a fight. Turning's a door."

Alaric got up and brushed the sand off his sleeves.

He'd asked the night before, beside the channel, while the pies and the chairs were hanging in the air between the three of them. *Can you teach me how to stop someone? Without killing them.* Silas had looked at him without answering. Then he'd said there was rarely time for that sort of thing, and after a while he'd added that it would have to be before sunrise, because he wasn't wasting water on it in the heat.

He hadn't asked Alaric why. Alaric hadn't needed him to.

Silas swung. Alaric stepped in, and he was late, and the sheathed blade cracked across his ribs hard enough to empty him of air.

"Late," Silas said. "You waited to see if I meant it."

"Did you?"

"Assume I always mean it."

Redd was sitting on the rock where they'd slept, boots still off, his bandaged arm held against his chest. "I'd like it noted that I've been saying that about him for weeks."

"Noted. Again."

Alaric went in again and caught the arm, and got his right hand onto the wrist, and turned. Silas turned with him, so easily that Alaric's own wrist ended up somewhere it didn't belong. He went down on one knee to keep his arm.

"You held the wrist," Silas said. "Good. You forgot the elbow. If you don't trap the arm, it's my wrist and your whole body, and I win that every time."

Redd stood up. "Let me be the villain."

"You've got one arm."

"Villains always have something wrong with them. It's how you know." Redd found a length of dry root in the sand, weighed it in his good hand, and pointed it at Alaric. "Prepare yourself."

He swung, wide and slow, like a man beating a rug. Alaric stepped in, trapped the arm at the elbow, found the wrist and turned it. The root fell into the sand.

Redd staggered back clutching his chest. "Oh. Oh, he's killed me."

"You let go," Alaric said.

"I'm a very cooperative villain."

"That's who it works on," said Silas. "Someone who doesn't want to hurt you."

He said it flatly, as he said everything that mattered, and raised the sheathed sword one more time.

This time Alaric didn't wait to see whether he meant it. He went in close, inside the arc of the blade, and got his left arm over Silas's elbow and clamped it, and his right hand closed on the wrist, and he turned. Silas's fingers loosened. The hilt slid halfway out of his grip. Then Silas stepped round the turn, his wrist came free, Alaric's balance went, and Alaric was sitting in the sand again.

But the sword had moved in Silas's hand. Alaric had felt it go.

"Half," Silas said. He retied the leather over the guard, which didn't need retying. "That's more than most people get the first morning."

The sun came up over the eastern figure's shoulder. The cold went out of the sand as if someone had pulled a cloth off it.

Silas slung the heaviest skins across his back and looked south, along the line Kurdag had drawn for them in the dust of a council table. Then he looked at Alaric.

"You're in front today."

Alaric thought he'd misheard. "Me?"

"You kept your head out here better than either of us." Silas settled the straps. "Keep it today."

---

It was a strange thing, to be trusted.

For the first hour Alaric walked as if the ground might notice he didn't belong in front and throw him out of the line. Then the heat came up and he stopped thinking about it. He watched the dark strip, and named each wall before he walked towards it, a habit from the broken streets, and checked behind him often enough that Silas stopped needing to.

The ruins changed as they went. The buildings grew lower and further apart, and then they weren't buildings any more, only the outlines of them: foundations drawn in the sand like plans nobody had got round to building. Twice the wind seemed to blow from two directions at once. Once Redd stopped and turned round to answer someone, caught himself, and walked on with his jaw set.

"Captain Al," he said, the next time they rested. "Has a ring to it."

Silas gave him a look.

"I'm only saying. If he's leading, he should have a title."

"If he's leading, you can carry his water, Lieutenant."

Redd considered it. "I'd rather stay a villain."

The dark sand forked in the early afternoon. One branch ran straight on, south. The other leaned away to the left, east of south, between two long mounds that might once have been walls.

"Which one?" Silas asked.

Alaric looked at them both. The left-hand branch was no darker than the other. The ground didn't slope, but it leaned that way under his feet, like the floor of a house you've lived in all your life.

"That one," he said, and pointed left.

Silas looked at it, then at him, and nodded.

The strip forked again an hour later, and again before evening, and each time the leaning branch was the one that felt right. Each time Silas asked, and each time Alaric pointed, and each time Silas nodded. Alaric found he liked that nod more than he ought to.

They camped on a raised platform of fitted stone that stood out of the sand like a table. There was no fire, and the water came in three mouthfuls each and a dish for Wena. Redd was asleep before the light went, talking in his sleep to someone who wasn't there. Silas took the first watch, sitting with his back to them and his face to the dark.

The creatures came out after sunset, far off. Alaric heard them like weather in another valley: a scraping, a dragging, something that might have been a voice and wasn't.

Then, under all of it, the woman.

It was clearer than it had been in the shelter with the planks. There were no words. It had the shape of words, like a tune whose lines you can't remember. It was warm, and it was close, and it didn't come from behind them, or from either side.

It came from ahead. From exactly where he'd been leading them all day.

Alaric lay on the stone and listened.

*Tell me before you answer*, Silas had said. He wasn't answering. So he wasn't breaking anything, he told himself. And the dark sand ran that way anyway. He'd followed the sand, not her. He could have shown anyone the forks.

Under that, where he didn't have to look at it, sat something plainer. He wanted to know who she was. He wanted it more than he'd wanted anything since the wheat field, more than water, and he couldn't have told anyone why.

He decided to keep the same line in the morning.

He fell asleep with her still speaking.

---

Warmth came first. It was the kind a room holds when a lamp has been lit in it for a long time.

Then a fist closed in the back of his collar and hauled, and the warmth was gone, and the world was cold and roaring and ended a pace in front of his boots.

**THOOM.**

His heartbeat slammed inside his skull.

The ground in front of him simply stopped. Past the lip there was nothing: grey air going down, and down, and further down than air should go. The far side of the nothing was a line so distant it could have been cloud. And all along the lip, as far as he could see in either direction, sand was pouring over the edge. It poured in long pale sheets, in threads and ropes and sudden slumping falls, and its noise came up out of the emptiness a heartbeat behind it. It ran past his boots. He could feel it tugging round his ankles like a current, sliding over the edge and away and never landing anywhere, because there was nowhere down there for it to land.

The sound was like rain that had never stopped since the day it started.

"Don't move," said Silas, behind him.

He didn't. Silas dragged him back one pace, then another, then a third, and kept hold of his collar when they stopped.

Alaric looked down at himself. He was wearing his boots. The satchel was across his body. His right knee was wet and dark through the cloth, and when he touched it his fingers came away red. He didn't remember hurting it. He didn't remember anything.

"Where —" he said.

"Don't," said Silas.

Wena reached them first. She came up the slope behind at a scrambling run and pushed herself hard against Alaric's legs, between him and the edge. Redd came after her, bent almost double, his good hand pressed to his side and the packs dragging off both shoulders. He stopped a few paces short and stood there, getting his breath, and didn't come any closer to the edge.

"You walked," he said, when he could. "That's how you got here. You walked."

"I don't —"

"I know you don't. You were asleep. You were asleep the whole way." Redd wiped his face with his sleeve. "She woke me. Wena. She was whining at your blanket, and you weren't in it."

Silas held up his left hand without being asked. The palm was blistered white across the centre, the skin raised and weeping at the edges.

"We needed to see your tracks," he said. That was all he said about it.

"Your tracks went straight," said Redd. "Dead straight. Didn't curve once. And theirs were all over them." He wasn't looking at Alaric now. He was looking at the edge. "The creatures'. Crossing yours. Going round you, some of them. We saw where one had stood right beside your prints for a while. Right beside. And not one of them came near you."

"Stop," Silas said.

Redd stopped.

The sand went on pouring over the lip. The sun was coming up behind them. Its light went out across the emptiness and found nothing to land on, any more than the sand did.

This was what Kurdag had meant by the central crater, Alaric thought. This was what he'd led them towards.

"I heard you go," said Silas.

Alaric turned.

"Last night, on watch. I heard footsteps going away from the rock." Silas's fist was in his collar. He seemed to have forgotten it was there. "I've heard footsteps every night since the planks. I didn't turn round."

It was the closest thing to an apology Alaric had ever heard from him, and it was worse than one.

The voice was quiet now. It had been quiet since he woke. Standing here at the place it had come from, Alaric could hear nothing but the sand.

"I heard her," he said. "Last night, before I slept. The woman. She was ahead of us, the way we'd been walking." He made himself go on. "And all day, at every fork. I didn't hear her then, but I felt which way was right, and it was always the left."

"And you didn't say," said Silas.

"No."

"Why?"

Alaric could have told him the dark sand ran that way. He'd told himself so often enough.

"Because I wanted to know who she was," he said.

Silas let go of his collar.

For a while none of them said anything. The sand fell. Wena leaned on Alaric's legs as if he might go over if she stopped.

Then Silas turned his back on the edge and started down the slope, the way they'd come. "I'll walk in front," he said.

That was all. He didn't say it angrily. Alaric wished he had.

Redd shifted the packs on his shoulders. "Well," he said. "At least we've seen it now. I'd have hated to come all this way and not —"

He didn't finish. He followed Silas down.

At the foot of the slope, Silas knelt and unslung the skins and laid them out in a row in the sand, and weighed each one in his hands. He did it twice. Then he sat back on his heels.

"We've spent a day and a night walking the wrong way," he said. "We had three dry days of water. We've drunk one. And we're no nearer the edge than we were at the channel." He picked up the fullest skin and put it down again. "Half shares, from now. Wena gets a share. If anyone's thinking about arguing with that, don't."

Nobody argued.

Alaric looked back once, up the slope, to the lip and the endless falling sand. He thought she might say something now, to call him back. She didn't.

He turned his back on it and followed Silas.

By noon his knee didn't hurt any more. He noticed it, and didn't say, and nobody asked.

---

They slept that night in the angle of two walls that had fallen against each other, with the sand banked high on the outer side.

Before he lay down, Silas cut a length of cord from his pack, tied one end round Alaric's ankle, and tied the other round his own wrist. He gave no explanation, and Alaric didn't ask for one. He lay down with the cord snug above his boot and thought it was probably the kindest thing Silas knew how to do, and that it would be humiliating for the rest of his life.

Half shares was a different kind of thirst. It didn't come in a rush. It settled in, and made itself a permanent part of him. His tongue felt too big for his mouth. His head ached behind the eyes whether he moved or not. When he swallowed, there was nothing there to swallow.

Redd was hearing his father again. Alaric could tell from the tilt of his head, and from how quiet he'd gone. Near midnight he sat up.

"Silas."

"Mm."

"Talk to me. About Marta's kitchen. Like you did the other night." A pause. "The leak."

Silas was lying on his back with his blistered hand on his chest. For a while he said nothing, and Alaric thought he'd fallen asleep. Then:

"The leak," he said. "Over the cupboard by the back door. She had a man in to fix it —"

He stopped.

Redd waited. "Go on."

Silas didn't go on.

"Silas?"

"I can't, Redd."

Redd was quiet. When he spoke again, his voice had changed.

"What's happened to her?"

The cord round Alaric's ankle tightened, then went slack, as Silas sat up.

He told him plainly. He told him what Liluth had said at Foramen, with her sword out and one eye left: *Your innkeeper didn't make a sound. Not one. I had to ask a peat-cutter instead.* He told him that was all he knew. That Marta had been hurt. That she hadn't talked.

"She held," Silas said. "That's what it means."

For a while Redd didn't move.

"Course she didn't talk," he said at last. His voice cracked in the middle, and he pushed straight on through it. "Course she didn't. She wouldn't tell the soldiers what was in the stew. She told me it was mutton once for a whole winter, and it was never mutton."

Then he went quiet in a different way.

"A peat-cutter," he said. "What would it take? To make a peat-cutter talk." He wasn't asking them. He was working it out. "Nothing you could do to a peat-cutter would make him talk. They've had everything done to them already." A long breath. "Unless they had his children."

Nobody told him he was wrong. Alaric didn't think he was.

Redd turned his head. In the dark, Alaric felt him look.

"You knew?"

"Yes," said Alaric.

"Both of you."

"Yes."

Neither of them tried to explain why. There wasn't an explanation that would have made it better, and Alaric could tell Silas knew that too.

Redd pulled his knees up. "You could have gone back," he said to Silas. "In Kelmend. You heard the soldiers at the inn. You knew that elf was going round to hers. You could have gone back."

"If I'd gone back, they'd have had us both."

"That's not why."

Silas looked at him. It was too dark to see what his face did.

"That's not why," Redd said again, and it wasn't an accusation this time.

The wind went over the walls. Out in the dark, far off, something dragged itself across stone.

"Twenty years ago I had a company," Silas said.

He didn't tell it like a story. He told it like a report he'd rehearsed for twenty years and never delivered, in short, flat pieces, one after another.

"Eleven of us. Good ones. The Shield paid us to watch Natharul raiders inside Mydea and come back and say what we'd seen. Watch, and come back. That was the whole job."

"We found a unit north of the river, burning a mill. They saw us, and they ran. Into a dry gorge. Walls either side you couldn't climb."

"Two of my people said it was too easy. Marta's father was one of them. He said it looked like a door someone had left open on purpose. I said we'd never get a better look at how they fought. I said we'd go in after them."

"They'd put people on both ridges before they ran. They closed the top end. Then the bottom. It took most of an afternoon."

He stopped. Alaric thought that was the end. It wasn't.

"I walked out. Nobody else did. I don't know why they let me. I think they wanted one of us to go home and tell it, and they picked the one who'd given the order, because he'd tell it best. So I went home and told it. I told Marta it was my order. She shut her door. She was right to shut it."

The cord round Alaric's ankle hung slack in the sand between them.

"Everything I've done since," Silas said, "I've done where she could hear about it. And she's never opened the door, and she shouldn't. And now they've hurt her for it, and it's because I carried him out of that fort." He didn't look at Alaric. "That's why I didn't go back."

Redd said nothing for a long time.

"What summer?" he asked at last.

"The last one. Before the Campaign ended."

"They burned us that summer," Redd said.

"I know."

Wena got up from where she'd been lying across Alaric's feet, crossed the hollow, and lay down against Redd instead. After a while his hand found her ear and stayed there.

Silas lay back down. When he turned onto his side, the cord pulled at Alaric's ankle, a small tug in the dark.

Alaric lay awake. Silas didn't ask him to take a watch.

---

In the morning there were tracks across their path.

Horses, a lot of them, shod with Natharul iron. The prints were a day or two old and already softening at the edges. They came from the north-east and crossed the dark strip at an angle, and then they began to bend.

Silas saw the curve at the same moment Alaric did. Neither of them said Kurdag's name, but Alaric heard him anyway. *Don't follow your own tracks if they start curving back towards you.*

They didn't follow them. They climbed a heap of fallen masonry instead, and from the top they could see where the tracks went.

They went round, and round, and round, tightening, like water going down a drain.

At the centre of the spiral, there were horses lying down.

"The forty," Redd said.

Silas read the ground like a street he knew.

"They came straight through the middle," he said. "The fast way. To be waiting at the far edge before we got there." He nodded at the spiral. "Nobody told them about the tracks."

"We should go round," said Alaric.

"They'll have had water."

It was the only reason that mattered, and they all knew it. They went down.

The first rider lay face down near the outermost loop, where the spiral was wide. His armour had the same gold lines Liluth's had. There was a sword wound in his back, and his sword was still in its sheath.

The second had been opened across the throat. The man who'd done it lay a few paces away with a knife in his side, and his hand was reaching towards the knife as if he'd meant to take it back.

After that, Alaric stopped counting the ways they'd died and counted the dead instead, because that was easier.

Two riders lay locked together where they'd fallen, each with a fist in the other's collar. Water skins were scattered everywhere, slashed open, their leather dried hard and curled at the cuts. Someone who knew what he was doing had cut one horse's throat neatly, and there was a dark crust round the wound where men had put their mouths to it. Long furrows led away from the spiral towards the ruins, the sand scraped smooth by something heavy being dragged. None of them went near those.

There weren't forty. Alaric didn't know if that was better.

"They turned on each other," Redd said. His voice had gone small.

Silas crouched by the slashed skins and turned one over with his knife. "Over water," he said. "And over which way to go. You follow your own tracks round long enough, every man starts hearing a different voice telling him the way out." He stood. "Some of them will have been right."

He searched them anyway. He went from body to body, turning out the skins that weren't slashed and shaking the flasks, quickly and without any expression. Every one was empty. Whatever they'd fought over, none of it was left.

Somebody said something in Elvish.

It came from the heart of the spiral, from under a grey horse that lay on its side with its legs stretched out stiff. A rider was trapped beneath it, pinned from the hip down. He was young, as far as Alaric could tell with elves. His lips were split and black. His eyes had gone the colour of milk at the edges. A sword lay in the sand near his hand, and its blade was dark all the way to the guard.

He'd called out in Elvish as if his own people had come back for him. Then his eyes found Alaric, and whatever he'd hoped went out of his face.

"You," he said. It was the human tongue now, and like all of them he spoke it as if it tasted bad. "Three and a dog. You're him. The survivor of the field."

Alaric crouched a few paces away. He didn't know what else to do.

"The survivor of the field is to be taken alive." The rider said it in a flat sing-song, like a child reciting a lesson. "Returned to Kelmend for His Majesty's questioning." He laughed, or tried to, and it turned into something that shook his whole chest. "Alive."

"Which king?" Alaric asked.

The rider stared at him. "There is only one."

"Alive," Redd said behind him.

Alaric turned.

Redd had gone white. "Alive," he said again. "Like they left Marta alive."

He drew his father's sword.

He didn't shout. He walked, straight towards the grey horse and the man pinned under it, with his bad arm hanging and the sword held low in his good hand. Alaric had never seen him look like that. There was no joke left anywhere in him.

Alaric was in front of him before he'd decided to be.

Redd swung round him, not at him, towards the rider. Alaric stepped in. He was inside the arc of the blade before it reached its speed, close enough to smell the sweat and the dust on Redd, and he got his left arm over Redd's elbow and clamped it against his own ribs, and his right hand found the wrist, and he turned it. The way it didn't want to go. The way Silas had shown him in the grey light under the colossi, with a root and a joke.

It half-worked.

Redd's fingers didn't open. Redd didn't want to hurt him, but he wanted to hurt someone so badly that it came out as strength, and he was stronger than Alaric, and he didn't stop being strong because he was grieving. They went over together, hard, into the sand. The sword came loose and lay between them with its edge in the dust.

Redd left it there. He didn't fight Alaric. He lay on his back where he'd fallen, staring at the white sky, and breathed.

Behind them, the rider was saying something.

"Water," he said. Then he said it again in Elvish, and it was a different word, softer, and after that there was nothing more in the human tongue.

Alaric crawled to him. He pulled the stopper from his own skin with his teeth and got an arm under the rider's head. The rider's face was hot and dry as paper, and his head weighed almost nothing.

Silas knelt on the rider's other side.

Alaric had the skin halfway to the split lips when Silas's knife went in under the rider's ear, once, quick and certain, the way you'd put out a lamp.

Alaric's hand jerked. Water ran out of the skin in a bright rope and splashed into the sand beside the dead man's face, and the sand drank it at once, as it drank everything.

Silas reached across and righted the skin before more than a mouthful was lost. He pushed the stopper back in with his thumb.

Alaric looked at the rider. The eyes were still open. They weren't looking at anything.

"What did he say?" he asked. "At the end."

Silas wiped the knife on the dead man's sleeve.

"He was asking for his mother."

On the sand behind them, Redd made a sound. It wasn't a word.

Alaric laid the rider's head down. His hands wanted to shake, so he pressed them flat on his knees until they didn't.

"You didn't have to," he said.

"He'd have lasted till dark." Silas sheathed the knife. "They come at dark. You've heard them. You'd have stayed with him."

"Yes."

"I know," Silas said. "That's why."

"I stopped Redd."

"I know you did." Silas stood up. "I didn't need you to stop me."

He said it without cruelty, in the same voice he'd used at the edge of the crater to say "I'll walk in front". And Alaric, who'd lain awake last night listening to him describe a gorge with both ends closed, understood exactly why this was the only way Silas knew how to keep anyone alive. That didn't make it easier to forgive. It made it harder.

Redd sat up after a while. He found his father's sword in the sand and wiped the grit off the blade against his trouser leg, slowly, the whole length of it, and put it back in its sheath. He didn't look at the rider. He didn't look at either of them.

They left the spiral on their own tracks, which ran straight.

---

The heat that afternoon was the worst yet.

It came up off the ground as well as down from the sky, so there was no direction to turn your face away from. Alaric's thoughts went thin and slow and far apart. Once he found he couldn't remember what Darcy had told him to say to her father. The whole sentence had gone. He walked with the hole where it had been for most of an hour before it came back, and when it did, it was about water, and he nearly laughed.

Wena lay down in the only shade for a mile, the thin blue shadow of a leaning column, and wouldn't get up.

Alaric knelt by her. Her tongue was grey and dry at the edges. Her eyes followed his hands.

He uncorked his skin and poured his whole share into his cupped palms, and held them under her nose. She drank it in four laps and then licked his palms and between his fingers until there wasn't any damp left anywhere.

When he stood up, Redd was beside him, holding out his own skin.

He didn't say anything. He hadn't said anything since the spiral. He pushed the skin into Alaric's hands, closed Alaric's fingers round it with his good hand, and walked on.

Alaric drank one mouthful and gave the rest back to him. Redd took it without a word.

Silas carried Wena for a stretch after that, across his shoulders like a lamb, her legs hanging down either side of his chest. He carried her until his hands gave out, and then he set her down carefully on her feet. He looked at his palms, the blistered one and the other one, and didn't say anything about them either.

She walked. Slowly, with her head down, but she walked.

They sheltered that night on a spur of rock with the sand dropping away steeply on three sides. There was barely any water left, a few swallows in each skin, and none of them drank before dark.

Before they lay down, Alaric crossed to where Redd was sitting.

"Will you sit up with me?" he said. "For a bit."

Redd looked at him. It was the first time he'd looked straight at him since the morning.

"Why?"

"In case I walk."

For a long moment Redd didn't answer.

"All right," he said.

He sat down against the rock beside Alaric, close enough that their shoulders touched, with Wena's head across both their knees. Silas lay a little way off. He didn't bring out the cord.

The creatures moved somewhere far below, dragging.

The voice didn't come.

Alaric stayed awake, waiting for it. The dark went on, and on, and she didn't speak, and he found that he was listening for her the way you listen for the step of someone who's late home. He hated that he was. He listened anyway.

At some point Redd's head came down onto his shoulder, heavy with sleep. Alaric didn't move him.

---

Grey light woke him. He was still sitting against the rock. Redd was still asleep on his shoulder. His own boots were exactly where he'd left them.

He hadn't walked.

He looked south, over Redd's head, and it took him a while to understand what he was seeing.

There was a line along the horizon, darker than the sand and too soft to be a cliff or a ruin. It rolled. When the light grew, it wasn't grey any more.

It was green.

Above it, so thin he thought at first it was a flaw in his eyes, a thread of smoke was going straight up into the windless morning air. Someone's chimney. Someone's fire. Someone who'd woken in a house and was going to make breakfast.

He shook Redd. Redd came awake all at once, reaching for his sword, and Alaric caught his wrist and pointed.

Redd looked. His mouth opened. Something that was nearly a laugh came out of it, cracked and dry, and then he pressed his good hand over his face and sat there.

Silas was already on his feet. He stood on the edge of the spur with the empty skins hanging from his fist and looked at the green line. Then he closed his eyes.

Wena got up and shook herself.

"A day," Silas said, without opening his eyes. "Maybe less."

Nobody asked how he knew.

They picked up their packs. Redd got to his feet, reached down, pulled Alaric up after him by the hand, and let go.

Alaric turned to follow him down the rock.

Behind him, from the north, from the heart of the Expanse, from the place where the sand fell forever and never landed, a woman's voice said one word.

*"——Alaric."*

It wasn't a murmur. It wasn't the shape of words. It was clear, and close, and broken at the front exactly the way it had been on the floor of Gerolt's cabin, with the arrows coming in and the old man's blood warm under his knees.

Nobody else turned round.

He had never told anyone where his name had come from. Not Gerolt, who'd said it back to him and smiled. Not Silas. Not Redd.

Out there in the ruins, someone already knew it.
