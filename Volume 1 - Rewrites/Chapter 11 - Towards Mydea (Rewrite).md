# Towards Mydea

The harbour came closer without getting any easier to reach.

Somewhere on the descent its roofs had come apart from one another. From the crater they'd been a scatter of pale shapes. Now they had chimneys and courtyards, and narrow roads winding down between them to the sea.

Seralune learned the town in the spaces between Nereth's breaths.

Three steps. A pause. Two more while the breath held. Then the broken sound of air forcing its way back in.

Nereth's arm still lay across Seralune's shoulders, but it no longer tightened when the path slid under them. Her head rested against the side of Seralune's neck. In the daylight, the black-purple veins beneath her left eye had darkened. They branched past her hairline now and disappeared under what was left of her collar.

They pulsed.

Seralune stopped.

"Smaller teeth?" she asked.

Nereth didn't open her eye.

Seralune hitched her higher. Pain ran from her injured shoulder down into the hand holding Nereth's waist. Her bruised thigh tightened under both their weights, and the cut across her foot had soaked through the strip Nereth had tied round it beneath the aqueduct.

The path curved through low, wind-bent trees. Their leaves moved in a breeze that smelled of salt instead of ash. Birds called from somewhere beyond them.

Ordinary birds.

No roots opened under their feathers. No dead mouths carried their voices through the earth. They went from branch to branch because they wanted to, and when one flew off, nothing underground followed it.

Seralune was still watching them when Nereth's knees gave.

They both went down. Stone drove into Seralune's bruised thigh. Her injured palm caught them, split open again, and left a smear of blood in the dust.

Nereth lay half across her lap.

"Nereth."

The good eye opened the width of a thread.

"You keep saying my name," Nereth whispered, "as if I've misplaced it."

Relief went through Seralune so hard it weakened her before it helped.

"Can you stand?"

"You didn't like my answer the last time."

"I'm prepared to dislike it again."

Nereth's mouth moved faintly. "No."

The honesty left Seralune nowhere soft to put it.

She pulled Nereth upright against her. Below, the path dropped in long turns towards the first houses, and past them, masts rose over the harbour, their rigging drawn in black lines across the water.

The bells behind them had changed. They no longer rang from every tower at once. One began. Two others answered. Silence held for a few breaths, then the pattern repeated further down the mountain.

Orders, travelling as sound.

Seralune didn't know what they meant. She put Nereth's arm across her shoulders again and stood.

This time Nereth's feet dragged.

---

The first person they passed was running towards the mountain.

He wore a green work coat buttoned wrong on one side and carried a woodcutter's axe, though there wasn't a tree near the road big enough to need it. His eyes went to the blood on Seralune's face, then to Nereth, then up to the black smoke behind them.

"What happened?"

"The southern slope opened," Seralune said, and kept walking.

That stopped him better than an order would have.

"Opened?"

She didn't have the breath to explain a mountain to someone who could see it.

More people came as the path widened into a road. Two harbour workers climbing with coils of rope over their shoulders. An old woman with a kitchen knife in one hand and a frightened child's wrist in the other. Three soldiers in light armour hurrying north on foot, their green cloaks still settling on their shoulders.

Nobody else stopped her. The town was emptying itself towards the bells.

Its streets stepped down to the harbour in narrow levels. Most of the walls were white stone, streaked grey where rain had found the same path for years. Wooden balconies leaned over the road, and nets hung drying between them, heavy with beads of seawater that caught the late-morning light.

None of it belonged to Seralune's memory.

The windows were too wide. The roof tiles overlapped in a pattern she'd never seen. Even the lettering painted over the nearest shop had changed. It was still Elven, but the strokes had thinned, and some of the endings had been dropped altogether.

She had to read the word twice.

**Waluna.**

The name gave the harbour a shape. It didn't tell her where to go inside it.

Almost every face under the balconies was elven. The only human she saw stood beneath a cooper's awning with his hood up, though the sky was clearing, waiting for the road to empty before he crossed it.

A wagon rolled across the street ahead. Two young elves walked beside it, steering the load with little visible turns of Wind beneath the wheels.

Neither of them looked at the two women slumped against the wall.

Seralune drew breath to call after them.

Nereth changed against her.

It wasn't a seizure. It was an absence. What little weight Nereth had still been carrying went out of her legs all at once. Her head rolled against Seralune's shoulder, and her next breath didn't come.

"Nereth?"

Seralune lowered her against the wall. The good eye stayed closed. Beneath the other, black-purple light moved in a slow, thick pulse.

She put two fingers under Nereth's jaw.

There. Too fast. Then nothing she could tell apart from the shaking of her own hand.

"Nereth. Nereth, *please*."

She brushed the singed hair back from the bite. The wound was still open, dark and swollen at the edges. Something clear and thick moved through the blood, too heavy to run.

"Move your hand."

The voice came from behind her.

A woman stood in the middle of the street with a wooden case on one hip. Copper-brown hair had escaped the knot at the back of her head, and her face was flushed from hurrying. A green scarf crossed one shoulder.

She wasn't looking at Seralune. She was looking at Nereth.

Seralune's arm tightened. "Who are you?"

The woman set the case on the road and dropped to one knee. "Someone who knows what she's doing. Move your hand so I can see the wound."

She flipped the case open. Small glass bottles filled one side. Folded cloth filled the other, beside a narrow knife, needles, cord and bundles of dried leaves tied with coloured thread.

Seralune didn't move.

The woman looked up. The warmth in her face stayed. The softness didn't.

"Tell me what you know about the bite," she said. Her voice had gone hard with urgency, but she kept her hands open where Seralune could see them. "Then let me look at it."

Seralune looked at the dark branches moving under Nereth's skin.

"I don't know anything about it."

"Then you'll have to trust me." The woman's gaze dropped to Nereth's still face. "She hasn't got long enough for us to argue."

Seralune took her hand away.

The woman's fingers were there at once. She found the pulse, lifted the good eyelid, and held a polished scrap of metal under Nereth's mouth.

A faint mist gathered on it.

"Breathing. Badly. How long since the bite?"

"Hours."

"What bit her?"

Seralune looked back at the broken mountain.

"Something that shouldn't have been alive."

The woman followed her gaze for a heartbeat. The line between her brows deepened.

"Well, whatever it was, this isn't behaving like any bite I know." She uncorked a dark bottle and held it under Nereth's nose. "Help me lift her."

"Where are you taking her?"

"My treatment room's behind the chandlery. It's close, and I've got what she needs."

Seralune looked down at Nereth. She'd known her little more than a day. She couldn't bear the thought of losing her.

She took Nereth under the shoulders. The stranger took her knees.

"Don't lift till I say three," the woman said. "One. Two. Three."

---

The room behind the chandlery sat so close to the harbour that the tide moved under its floorboards. Every wave came up through the wood as a low complaint.

**Krrrk.**

Shelves covered two walls, crowded with bottles, cloth and bundles of dried plants. It wasn't the dusty clutter of Cyrandor's passages or the careful emptiness of her palace room. Everything had a place, and everything looked used. A narrow cot stood under the only window.

They laid Nereth on it.

The woman pulled the ruined cloak back from Nereth's shoulders and stopped.

Burned cloth was fused to the attendant's back under Seralune's bandage. The black-purple lines ran down her throat and vanished into the collar of her dress. A bruise spread along one side of her ribs, where the corruption had forced every breath wider than it should go.

The woman let out a long breath through her nose.

"You carried her down the mountain like this?"

"There was no one else."

"Nobody stopped to help you?"

"They saw us."

The woman's mouth tightened. Whatever she thought of that, she kept it behind her teeth.

She crossed to a small iron stove, opened the vent and touched two fingers to the cold coals. Wind left her hand with a low whistle, stirred under the ash, and found a buried ember. Orange light opened.

A small Wind. Controlled. Useful.

She set a kettle on it.

"What's your name?" she asked without turning round.

Seralune's hand went to the chain beneath her grey overdress. The closed lily pressed against her skin.

Nobody in Waluna had recognised her face. That didn't mean they wouldn't recognise her name.

"Sera."

The rest of it stayed behind her teeth.

The woman glanced back. If she'd heard the hesitation, she let it go.

"And your friend?"

"Nereth."

The woman repeated it once, as if putting it somewhere safe. "I'm Nyana. Stay close to her. If she wakes up, don't let her touch the wound."

"Are you a physician?"

"A medic." Nyana was already washing her hands. "Ship's medic, when my brother remembers he needs one."

"Can you help her?"

"I can clean the bite, bring the fever down and keep her breathing while I work out what this poison's doing." She dried her hands on a folded cloth. "That's what she needs right now."

It wasn't a promise. That steadied Seralune more than a kind one would have.

"Her Affinity?" Nyana asked.

"Fire."

"Rank?"

"I don't know."

Nyana looked at her.

"We only met yesterday," Seralune said. "Before we went under the mountain."

Nyana's glance went to the window. Her mouth tightened, but she didn't ask what had happened down there. She laid two fingers against Nereth's wrist.

"There's almost no mana left in her."

"Almost none?"

"I can barely feel it."

Nyana soaked a cloth in warm water and began to ease the dried blood from the bite. Nereth's back came up off the cot. A sound caught behind her teeth.

Seralune reached for her. Nyana's forearm blocked her.

"Her shoulders. Hold her still."

"You're *hurting* her."

"I know. I'm sorry." Nyana didn't look up from the wound. "There's dirt caught under the blood. It has to come out."

"Can't it wait?"

"She's already burning up, Sera. I won't leave it."

Seralune heard Nereth beneath the aqueduct. *So he had to stay behind.*

She moved to the head of the cot and put both hands on Nereth's shoulders.

"Do it."

Nyana poured warm water into the bite.

Nereth screamed without waking.

It went through Seralune's hands and into her arms. Her own bad shoulder locked until she couldn't tell whose pain was whose.

Nyana worked. She cleaned the wound, cut away burned cloth and packed crushed leaves into the torn skin. No light came from her hands. Nothing glowed. Her skill was in the order she reached for things, and how fast she threw away anything that was no longer clean.

Slowly, Nereth's breathing settled into something shallow and regular. Seralune loosened her grip enough for Nyana to tie off the bandage. Past its edge, the black-purple veins went on pulsing.

"Can you take it out?" Seralune asked.

Nyana washed her hands. The water turned pink, then grey with ash.

"I don't recognise it. I can treat the fever and the wound, but I haven't got anything for what's in her veins." She looked back at the cot. "She's breathing easier. That buys time. It isn't a cure."

"What do you need?"

"More medicine than I keep here. Time to watch her. Somewhere she can rest without every bell in the kingdom dragging people into the street." Nyana looked towards the harbour window. "My brother's ship carries the stores for a crossing. It sails before midday."

Hope came up in Seralune before caution could stop it.

"Take us to him."

It came out like an order. Nyana studied her.

"He won't want strangers aboard."

"Then ask him to come here." Seralune looked at Nereth. "Please. Let him see her before he decides."

Something settled in Nyana's face. She stepped out into the lane and called to someone Seralune couldn't see.

"Pell! Find Neldor. Tell him I need him in the treatment room. Now."

Running footsteps. Nyana came back to Nereth without waiting to hear them go.

---

She treated Seralune while they waited, pulling a stool close enough to the cot that Seralune could still reach it.

"Sit."

"I should stay beside her."

"You'll be beside her. You'll just be sitting while I stop that hand bleeding all over my floor."

Seralune sat.

Nyana cleaned the cut on her brow, rewrapped her palm and replaced the filthy strip under her foot. When she reached for the shoulder, Seralune pulled away.

"We haven't got time."

Nyana kept a hand round her arm without forcing it. "If that shoulder gives out while you're carrying her, you both fall. Let me look."

Every moment sitting down felt like another moment for the bells to find them. But Nyana had already been right once when Seralune's fear had argued with her.

"Be quick."

Nyana pressed along the joint until Seralune's breathing changed.

"Bruised. Maybe cracked near the joint. Nothing out of place." She nodded at the thigh. "And that?"

"A piece of the mountain."

"Can you put your full weight on it?"

"Enough to walk."

"That's not quite what I asked." Nyana waited while Seralune tested it, then guided her back down onto the stool. "Tell me if it gets worse."

She loosened the grey overdress to tie a support under it, and her hands slowed when they found the gown beneath.

The pale cloth had been cut for Seralune a thousand years ago. Its hidden fastenings belonged to a court whose clothes were history now. Nyana ran her thumb along one seam, following stitching she'd clearly never seen.

"Where did you get this?"

"My mother."

It was true enough to hurt.

Nyana looked at her face. Whatever she saw there warned her off the next question. She let the overdress fall back over the gown.

"Keep that shoulder still."

The door opened before Seralune could decide whether Nyana believed her.

The man who came in brought the harbour with him. Salt had dried white across his boots. A coil of rope hung from one shoulder, and his dark green coat hung open over a linen shirt gone grey at the collar. The wind had pushed his hair back so many times it seemed to have given up falling forward.

His eyes found Nyana first. Then Nereth. Then Seralune.

The order told Seralune exactly who mattered to him.

"No, Nyana."

He sounded tired, not angry. His attention stayed on his sister.

Nyana didn't move from the cot. "You haven't heard her breathe."

"Pell said you wanted room on my ship for two strangers. I told him you'd never be so foolish." He looked at the cot. "I owe the boy a copper."

"I need the medical stores, and I need somewhere I can watch her through the crossing." Nyana pulled the blanket down to show the dark veins at Nereth's throat. "Look at her before you say no."

He looked. His jaw tightened.

"Moving her could kill her."

"So could leaving her." Fear got into Nyana's voice before she shut it out. "I can do more for her aboard."

Seralune stood. The room tilted. She waited until it came back.

"We need passage to Mydea," she said. "We can work for it."

Neldor watched her steady herself on the stool. "You can barely stand, and your friend can't wake up. You're not offering me workers. You're asking me to hide you."

He didn't wait for her to deny it.

"My ship sails under a royal contract. Every crate is counted. Every soul aboard is named in the book. If the quartermaster finds two passengers who aren't in it, I lose the cargo and the ship, and my crew might go with them."

"No one is searching for us."

The lie came more easily than her name had.

Up the mountain, another bell answered it.

Neldor looked towards the sound, then back at her.

"Don't lie to me while you're asking me to risk my crew." His voice didn't rise. Each word only got more careful. "The southern slope has torn open. Every bell between here and the palace is ringing a different warning, and you walked in covered in its ash."

"They were under it when it broke," Nyana said.

"That explains the blood." He didn't take his eyes off Seralune. "It doesn't explain why they need to leave Natharul before the harbour gets its orders."

Seralune met his eyes.

"We have family near the northern harbour."

"Names?"

She had none to give him.

"People who will know ours."

Neldor set the coil of rope down by the door. "You want me to risk everyone aboard for people you won't name."

Seralune reached under the overdress. The chain caught on the support Nyana had tied, then came free. Silver showed between her fingers.

The signet stayed hidden in her fist.

Her mother had worn it by the south-garden fountain. Cyrandor's family had kept it for ten centuries because a queen had ordered them to believe her daughter might one day need it.

Seralune could buy a place on the ship with it. She didn't know if she could ever buy back the only proof that her mother had waited for her.

Her hand wouldn't open.

Neldor watched the closed fingers.

"If you can't let go of it, don't offer it."

Seralune lowered her hand.

"I'll repay you in Mydea."

"With what?" There was no cruelty in it. He was weighing an offer from someone who had almost nothing.

"I can read and write and keep accounts. Whatever else the ship needs, I can learn."

"You've never set foot on a ship."

"No." She kept her back straight while the edges of the room swam. "But if someone shows me, I'll learn."

His gaze went over her again. Not the injuries this time. The way she stood. The words she'd chosen.

"Who are you?"

"Sera."

"That's your name. It's not what I asked."

The signet pressed into her palm. "It's all I can safely give you."

The truth inside the refusal sat between them. Neldor looked away first, but only at his sister.

"No."

Nyana's shoulders came down. It wasn't surrender. It was a decision. "Then I'll stay with them."

He stared at her.

"The ship sails before midday. You'd let the crew sail without its medic?"

"I know what I'm doing, Neldor." She laid two fingers on Nereth's wrist again. "She's my patient. She stopped breathing in the street, and she might do it again. I won't leave her alone when she does."

He stepped closer. "Nyana."

Only her name, low, with years in it that Seralune hadn't been there for.

Nyana didn't look away. "I know what I'm asking you to risk. I know what happens to her if I leave. I need my brother to understand both."

"You decided before you sent for me."

"I knew I couldn't leave her." Nyana looked at him. "I was hoping you'd help."

Neldor shut his eyes.

For a few breaths the harbour went on without him. Wheels crossed the boards outside. Somebody shouted for another coil. Rigging knocked gently against a mast.

**Tak. Tak-tak.**

He opened his eyes.

"The medical store. Both of you stay below until we've cleared the southern headland. Your names don't go in the crew book, and nobody aboard hears one word more than they need to keep the ship safe."

Nyana breathed out. She didn't make it a victory.

He turned to Seralune.

"At the Mydean harbour, you leave my ship. Until then you stay in the store unless Nyana says otherwise."

"I'll repay what we owe."

Neldor lifted the rope back onto his shoulder. "Keep your friend alive through the crossing. If I've still got a ship when we reach Mydea, we'll talk about what you owe me." He opened the door. "We sail within the hour."

---

They carried Nereth through the harbour under a sheet of canvas, on a narrow litter Neldor used for injured sailors. Nyana took the front. Seralune reached for the back, and Neldor looked at her shoulder and took it himself.

So she walked beside the litter with one hand under the canvas and two fingers on the pulse in Nereth's wrist.

Still there. Weak. Too quick.

Waluna was already going back to work. The bells still called overhead, but grain couldn't sit out in the rain because a mountain had broken. Barrels rolled towards open holds. Nets came up dripping from the small boats. Sailors lifted their heads whenever the highest bell rang, and went back to their ropes when no order followed.

Neldor's ship waited near the end of the second pier. It was bigger than the fishing boats round it, its dark hull built broad for cargo rather than speed, with three masts and green canvas still furled along the yards. Workers were loading crates through a hatch near the stern.

A royal mark had been burned into every crate.

It was her house's sigil. The lines had changed around it. The crown had gained another point, and the old leaves beneath it had turned into spears.

Seralune looked away before recognition could reach her face.

A porter stood by the next stack with one hand tucked under his arm. Grey cloth hung loose from his shoulders. With his other hand, he held a wooden tally out to a soldier sitting behind a narrow desk.

"The quarterman sent me. He said you'd mark me finished."

The soldier looked at the tally. "Six loads left."

The porter drew his hurt hand out from under his arm. The fingers had swollen until they couldn't close. A strip of cloth crossed the palm, dark and wet under the thumb.

"It went under a barrel. I can't hold a rope."

"Then carry crates."

"Could I have it bound first?"

Seralune stopped beside the litter.

The soldier dipped his quill. "When the stores are aboard. Your shift doesn't end because your hand hurts."

"Sir, please —"

"I can enter a refusal, if you'd prefer." The soldier looked up. "Then your custodian can explain why a Faint in his charge held up the king's supplies."

The porter took the tally back.

Seralune took a step towards them. He needed a bandage and a place to sit, and the man behind the desk had made both sound like things he'd failed to earn.

"Sera." Nyana had stopped at the foot of the gangplank. "Her shoulder. Keep her from rolling while we lift."

Neldor was turning the litter to clear the mooring lines. Under the canvas, Nereth's head had tipped onto her bad side. Seralune went back and steadied her. A small sound escaped Nereth as the litter rose.

Behind them, the porter bent to the crate.

He got his good hand under one corner and tried to lift it alone. It didn't come. The soldier watched him try twice, then said, "Both hands," in the same voice he'd used to count the loads.

The porter slid the swollen hand under the wood.

Seralune heard it go. A small wet sound, like green wood breaking. He didn't cry out. His whole body shook once, from the shoulders down, and then he lifted. The worker at the crate's other end shifted closer to take more of the weight, and together they carried it towards the hold while blood ran out of the cloth and down his wrist.

The soldier's quill scratched across the page.

Seralune could still turn round. She could make every guard on this pier look at her, and at Nyana, and at the two women Neldor had agreed to hide. Her hand tightened on the litter until Nereth stirred under it.

"Gently," Nyana said.

Seralune loosened her grip and walked beside them up the plank.

She had said nothing to the soldier. She would remember the sound the porter's hand made for a very long time.

---

The medical store had no window. Crates made a narrow wall round the cot where the crew set Nereth down. It smelled of dried mint, sealed oil and the damp wood of the hull. A single lantern hung from a hook overhead, and its flame swung whenever feet crossed the deck above.

Nyana packed bottles into padded compartments. Seralune sat beside Nereth with her knees almost touching the opposite wall.

The ship told her every step of its leaving through its own body.

A rope drew tight. **Krrrk.** A block struck the mast. **KLAK.** Men called to one another. Boots crossed from stern to bow. Beyond the hull, the water kept folding against the same boards.

Then Neldor's voice cut through all of it.

"Loose forward!"

The ship moved. Not much. Enough for Seralune's stomach to understand before her eyes did.

The floor shifted under the cot. Glass touched glass in Nyana's case. Nereth rolled towards the edge, and Seralune caught her before the bandage could pull.

Wind filled the sails.

**WHUMPF.**

The hull swung away from Waluna. Seralune felt the turn through the wall at her back. The sea stopped striking them from the side. Now it passed beneath them, lifted, and let go.

Nyana listened to the deck until some of the tension left her shoulders.

"He rushed it," she said, grinding herbs into a shallow cup.

"Because of us?"

"Partly. Those bells will turn into orders once the right man reaches Waluna." Nyana glanced at Nereth. "We want to be past the headland before they decide which ships to stop."

"How long is the crossing?"

"A week, if the wind holds."

Seralune looked at the wall as if Mydea might be visible through it. Cyrandor had given her a city to search on the other side of this sea.

"And from the northern harbour to Favale?"

Nyana's hands stopped.

Seralune heard her mistake after it had already left her mouth.

Nyana set down the pestle. "You told Neldor your family lived near the harbour."

"They don't."

The easy warmth went out of Nyana's face. "My brother's risked his ship and every soul aboard for you."

"He'd have refused if I'd told him where we were going." Seralune heard her voice go hard, and hated that she still believed it.

"You don't know that. You never gave him the chance." Nyana put the pestle down. "And when the harbour officials ask where his passengers are headed? What's he meant to tell them?"

"I was trying to protect her."

"So was he, Sera. There are people on this ship who depend on him."

Seralune's first answer came up out of pride. She let it die before it reached her mouth.

"You're right."

Nyana watched her a moment longer. Then she picked the pestle up again. "So tell me the truth now. Where are you going?"

"Favale."

Nyana snorted softly. "In pilgrim season, with no shoes."

"Pilgrim season?"

"Half of Mydea walks to Favale at this time of year. The roads'll be full of them." Nyana ground the herbs in slow circles. "They say the church has a living saint now. That she walks the temple steps at dawn, and the sick get carried up just to lie in her shadow."

"A saint?"

"That's what the pilgrims call her. The priests have a longer word." Nyana shrugged. "I've never seen her. I've seen plenty of people come home from Favale no better than they left."

She tapped the pestle against the cup's rim.

"It's another week overland from the northern harbour, if you've got coin, shoes, and a road without soldiers on it. Longer if you haven't got any of those."

Seralune looked down at her bare feet.

Nyana followed her gaze. "You're not crossing Mydea barefoot. We'll find you shoes before you leave the ship."

"Why did you help us?" Seralune asked. "You didn't know either of us. You argued with your brother. You risked your place on his ship."

Nyana kept her eyes on the herbs.

"I didn't decide all that when I saw you. She stopped breathing, and everyone else kept walking. So I stopped."

"And everything after?"

"I didn't think as far as the ship." She looked at her patient. "First she needed to breathe. Then she needed a room, and medicine. By the time Neldor walked in, leaving her felt too much like choosing what happened to her."

She mixed the herbs with water and covered the cup for when Nereth woke. As the ship left the shelter of the harbour, she checked the bandage and wedged a folded coat between Nereth and the hard edge of the cot.

The hull rose on a longer wave. Seralune steadied Nereth with one hand and waited for the next breath.

It came on its own.

Above them, the crew settled into Neldor's orders. Nobody called for the ship to turn back.

---

The physician had said Cyrandor would live.

Thaeroval had made him say it twice.

The old man lay under guard in a locked infirmary, the stump below his elbow bound tight in clean linen. No one would question him until he woke. No one would move him without Thaeroval's order.

It wasn't forgiveness. The difference mattered to Thaeroval.

It wouldn't matter to Cyrandor.

He went down into the southern aqueduct alone.

The stair his sister had taken no longer reached it. The eruption had torn the lower passages apart and folded three levels of stone into the route. Thaeroval went down through the gap it had left.

Dust settled on his armour. The sword stayed in its sheath. He didn't need to draw it. Where fallen stone blocked his way, he laid a hand against it and found the fractures already running through its weight. Where the path ended, he stepped through the chambers the mountain had opened for him.

The first corpse waited under an arch.

It wore a worker's tunic so old the cloth had grown into the pale roots running through it. The growth round its throat had burned away. Its mouth hung open over a tongue blackened by fire.

Beyond it lay another. Then dozens.

Some had dropped where the roots let them go. Others were still fixed in the walls, their limbs showing through the ash. None of them were soldiers sent down after Seralune. Whatever metal they wore had lost every mark that might have named a house, a trade or a century.

They had been under Natharul longer than anyone had remembered to look.

The bells reached him through the broken earth. Muffled. Accusing.

He went on.

The remains of the rooted things covered the channel ahead. White fire had opened them from the inside. Their hollow limbs had collapsed into rings of ash. Around some, a black-purple residue still clung to the stone, pulsing faintly, and drawing back into the cracks as he came near.

He had never seen it before.

That frightened him less than knowing it had grown beneath his sister's prison.

The lower wards had carried away whatever the seal couldn't hold. He'd known that much. He had never asked where it went.

The heat grew as he entered the ruined basin. Four channels had once divided here. Three lay open to the sky now. The fourth was packed with roots burned white.

At the centre, the stone was green.

Thaeroval stopped at its edge.

A perfect circle sat untouched inside the destruction. Moss covered its floor. A single white flower stood near the boundary, its petals folded against the late-morning sun.

Everything around it had burned. Nothing inside it had.

He knew that shape. Not the aqueduct, and not the flower.

The refusal.

Power reached Seralune and became something else before it could touch her.

He had believed that ended.

The thought didn't hold a face. It held the place where a face should have been.

He stepped into the circle. No memory answered him. His hand found the sword at his side, and for the first time since the mountain broke, the bells overhead seemed very far away.

Past the circle, blood marked the stone. Not much. A thin line dried beside a scrap of burned grey cloth. Further up the slope, two sets of tracks climbed through the fallen earth. One was deep and uneven. The other kept disappearing, and wherever it did, the deeper set carried on alone, as if one body had borne the weight of both.

Seralune had climbed out. Someone had climbed with her.

Thaeroval followed.

The crater opened over Waluna. Sunlight struck his armour as he reached the torn lip of the mountain. Below, the harbour ran out into a sea crossed by white sails. Fishing boats crowded the water near the piers. Merchant ships moved further out, some turning east, some south, before the coast hid them.

He couldn't see her.

The broken wards had kept the mark of her passage through the mountain like heat held in stone. At the crater's edge, it turned towards the harbour and scattered across the water. Her impossible mana had left the land. That was all the trail could tell him. Past the coast, wind and salt had thinned it until every ship crossed it.

He counted the sails.

Nine. Four fishing boats, close enough to make out figures on their decks. Two merchantmen already past the southern headland. Three more heading east, one of them under green canvas full of wind.

Any of them might be carrying her.

His hand closed on the sword.

He could cut the harbour. The piers would open. Masts would fall. Every vessel still inside the mouth would become wood on the water.

He could cut the wind across the headland. Ships would lose their sails against the rocks, and sailors who had never heard Seralune's name would drown beside whichever hull held her.

He could send the edge further. Far enough to reach the green canvas.

Far enough to reach his sister.

His thumb pressed against the guard.

The sword stayed in its sheath.

He had spent a thousand years believing the choice he'd made for her had been necessary. He wouldn't defend it now by cutting through her.

The green sail slid behind the headland.

Thaeroval said nothing.

Below him, Waluna's harbour bell began to ring.

Nereth woke when Natharul had become a dark shape above the water.

Her good eye opened first. It found the ceiling, the swinging lantern, and Nyana asleep on a folded coat beside the medicine case. Then it found the rough blanket over her, and Seralune sitting beside the cot with a cup she'd forgotten to drink from.

The eye narrowed.

"What did you promise?"

It barely disturbed the air.

Seralune leaned in. Relief made her useless. "You're awake."

"That wasn't my question," Nereth whispered. Even half-conscious, impatience came easier to her than fear.

"Work. Repayment. That we'd leave the ship at the northern harbour." Seralune watched her face for any sign the price frightened her. "Nothing else was decided."

Nereth considered that.

"You promised before you knew what the work was."

"You were unconscious."

"That explains why nobody stopped you. It doesn't make the bargain any better."

Nyana stirred by the case. Nereth's eye slid towards her.

"Nyana," Seralune said quietly. "The ship's medic. She treated the poison."

"Did she get it out?"

The black-purple marks answered with one slow pulse. Seralune didn't lie.

"No."

Nereth closed her eye. Her fingers crept across the blanket until they found the edge of the bandage on her cheek.

"What happened after the fire?"

"It destroyed the aqueduct. Part of the mountain with it."

The fingers stopped. "Did it reach anyone else?"

"I didn't see anyone but those creatures." Seralune couldn't give her more certainty than that. "Waluna was still standing when we got there."

Nereth let go of the breath she'd been holding. "I couldn't have made that fire."

"I know."

Nereth opened her eye again. It went to the bruises on Seralune's throat, and her hand drew back under the blanket.

Neither of them looked away.

"We're going to Mydea," Seralune said. "Cyrandor told me to start looking for my mother in Favale. I don't know what we'll find there."

Nereth swallowed, and it hurt her.

"You intend to reach Favale alone, Your Highness?"

"Nyana calls me Sera," Seralune whispered, with a glance at the sleeping medic.

"You intend to reach Favale alone, Sera?"

The correction came without any effort at all.

Seralune looked at her.

"You don't owe me anything," she said. "You nearly died because you followed me."

Nereth's eye held hers. "Do you want me to leave?"

The question found the wish Seralune had been hiding under concern.

"I want you to live."

"That wasn't my question either."

"No," Seralune admitted. "I don't want you to leave. I'm frightened of what'll happen to you if you stay."

Nereth sank deeper into the pillow. Some of the strain went out of the corner of her mouth.

"I'm frightened too," she whispered. "And I'd rather not wake up in Mydea and find you've gone without me."

The ship lifted under them. Water rushed the length of the hull. Nereth's fingers tightened weakly in the blanket.

"I'll come with you to Favale. We can work out the rest when we get there."

"Why?"

Nereth studied her with the one eye the poison had left clear.

"You walked towards a monster because you thought it was suffering. Then you carried me down a mountain on a bleeding foot. Someone ought to stay close enough to tell you when compassion's turned into stupidity."

Seralune heard something gentler under the bluntness, whether Nereth meant her to or not.

"Then you may tell me. I can't promise I'll enjoy hearing it."

"You rarely do."

Nyana opened one eye. When she saw Nereth watching her, relief softened her sleep-creased face.

"Good." She pushed herself upright and reached for the covered cup. "If you can argue, you can swallow. Drink this."

Nereth looked at the cup. "What is it?"

"For the fever and the pain. It tastes worse than it smells, and I won't lie to you about that."

Seralune helped her lift her head. The medicine smelled bitter before it even reached her. Nereth swallowed once, stopped, and fixed Nyana with the full force of her one clear eye.

Nyana held the cup steady rather than pushing it closer. "All of it, if you can."

Nereth looked at Seralune. "You trust her?"

Seralune looked at the woman who had stopped when everyone else kept walking.

"Yes."

Nereth drank the rest with the expression of someone recording a debt.

When the cup was empty, exhaustion took her eye again. Her breathing stayed shallow, but each breath came without being called.

Seralune waited until sleep had her properly.

"I'll sit with her," Nyana said, drawing her stool up to the cot. "Go up and get some air."

Seralune hesitated, watching Nereth breathe. Then she left her in Nyana's hands and climbed to the deck.

The sun stood high over the sea. Waluna had gone behind the headland, but Natharul hadn't. The kingdom rose from the water in far layers of mountain and white stone, its highest towers too distant to tell apart from the light round them.

Seralune rested both hands on the rail.

Her brother was somewhere inside that distance. Beside Cyrandor, maybe. Or under the mountain, looking at what had grown there. Or still standing where she'd left him, one hand held out to a sister who had chosen not to take it.

She had escaped him.

There was no victory in it. Only the knowledge that he'd loved her enough to call a locked room protection, and not enough to let the life outside it belong to her.

Wind crossed the deck. Above her, the green canvas drew tight between the masts. Neldor stood near the stern with one hand raised, Wind running from his fingers into the sail in thin, careful streams.

The ship turned further from Natharul.

Seralune touched the signet through her clothes. Her mother's lily was still there.

Behind her, the kingdom grew smaller without ever quite disappearing. Ahead, Mydea waited somewhere past the horizon.

This morning she had looked at this sea from a mountainside and seen only how far it went.

Now she was on it. And nobody but her was deciding how far she went.
