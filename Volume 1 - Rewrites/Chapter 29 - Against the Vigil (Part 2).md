# Against the Vigil (Part 2)

He looked exactly as he had in the passage, the night she'd run.

Dark hair, fallen across his brow. His eyes, that were her eyes. The pale scar at the base of his right thumb, which she'd never seen before she woke and which she'd never asked him about. The dust of the road on him, and none of that seeming to touch him.

"Thaer," she said.

It came out as it had come out when she was six and had broken something. She heard it and hated it.

"I wasn't coming here." It was the first thing she found to say, and it tumbled out of her. "I didn't — I wasn't coming *here*. I was only running."

"I know," said Thaeroval.

She stared at him.

"How long have you been standing here?"

"Long enough."

"How did you —"

"You told a soldier at Meren that you were looking for family." His voice was quiet and level. He spoke their own tongue, and it sounded like home, and that was the worst of it. "I am your family, Seralune."

The name went into her.

Every voice since the ship had called her Sera. Nyana. Neldor. Nereth, a hundred times a day, cross and fond and frightened. Agna. The girls. It had become her name, the one she'd chosen for herself, a small, plain name that nobody had given her.

He must have read it in the harbour book at Meren. He knew it. And he wouldn't say it.

"Is that what this is?" She made herself look away from him, at the ruin: the stumps, the elder in the hearth, the painted sun on the shrine, the lily in the wall. "Family?"

He didn't answer.

"Did you do this?"

"No."

He let the word stand on its own for a breath. Then:

"But I knew."

She'd heard him say it before. In the passage, with his hand held out and Cyrandor at her back and the hidden door grinding open. *Did you order them to lock that door? No. But I knew they had.*

The same answer. He hadn't even changed the shape of it.

"They took the children," she said. "The smallest one was carried out in her mother's arms. A priest told me. He said it as if it were a story for a feast day."

"I know."

"Father sent them."

"Father sent riders. I did not ride with them." He said it without any weight, the way he'd have told her the hour. "But I knew where they went, and I did not stop them."

"It was a house. There were seven people in it."

"Yes."

"You let them burn it."

"I let a great many things burn." He looked round the ruin once, briefly, like a man checking a council chamber for who was missing. "This was one of the smaller ones."

The field came.

Only a breath of it. Smoke. The sound of running feet, her own. A hand —

She held her face still. She held it so still that the pain behind her eyes had nowhere to go, and it went into her teeth instead, and she let it.

He was watching her. She didn't know how much he saw. He'd always seen more than she wanted him to.

"Where is your attendant?" he said.

"Why?"

"There was a medic on the ship that brought you to Meren. She told me what is under the scarf." His voice didn't change. "She told me your attendant would need a healer. Not a jar."

So he'd sat with Nyana, too. He'd sat with all of them. Seralune thought of Nyana telling him, warm and bossy and frightened, because she'd have thought it might help.

"She has one," Seralune said. "Tomorrow."

"The saint."

"Yes."

He said nothing to that. He only watched her a while longer.

"Come home," he said.

"No."

"Seralune." He took one step towards her across the ash. Only one. "I can protect you."

"From what?"

He looked past her, up at the hill, where the lamps went up the stair in a river of light and the hymn came down over the roofs.

"The thatcher on the road," he said. "The woman on the arch."

"She never touched anyone. She scrubbed pots."

"No," said Thaeroval, quite gently. "She never touched anyone. You did."

Seralune couldn't breathe.

"The pilgrims called her a witch because of you. They hanged her because of you. The thatcher on the road burned from the inside, in front of his little girl." His voice didn't change at all. "How many more, Seralune?"

She'd told herself all of it. Every night for three days she'd told herself exactly that, in those words, lying in the loft with her hand on Nereth's shoulder. It was different in his voice. In his voice it was simply true.

It rose in her, under the quiet, under the stillness she was holding so hard in her face: Wren standing where her father had put her, with her token on its too-long string. The grey shift on the arch. It came up, and the field came with it, running, running, blood and smoke —

She shut it down. Somehow she shut it down.

"I can protect you," he said. "And I can protect them from you. That is what I am for."

"Protect me how?" Her voice came out thin. "In the room?"

"Yes."

"With the lock on the outside."

"Yes."

"Until when?"

He didn't answer.

"Until when, Thaer?"

"Until it is safe."

"And when will that be?"

His eyes held hers across the ash. She waited for him to tell her. She'd waited in the room, too, all through that first night, for him to come back at first light and tell her.

He didn't tell her now either. She watched him not answer, and understood that he didn't know.

"I don't want to be protected."

It came out of her too loud. It rang off the black stumps of the walls.

"I've had enough, Thaer." Her voice was shaking now, and she let it. "Of rooms. Of doors with the lock on the outside. Of waking up and being told a thousand years have gone, and not being told what happened in them. Of being kept safe by people who won't say what they're keeping me safe from."

He said nothing.

"I want to live." She could hear how it sounded. She said it anyway. "My own life. On my own terms. A loft, and barley every night, and a roof to sit on, and nobody deciding for me when I'm allowed down off it. I don't care how small it is. I want to choose it."

"It will not stay small," said Thaeroval. "Nothing is small, with you. You have seen that for yourself."

"Then I'll learn."

"On whom?"

She had no answer to that.

"Where is Mother, Thaer?"

She hadn't meant to ask it. It came out of the place the running came from.

"I do not know."

"You said that before. In my room, the first night. You said you wished you knew."

"I still wish it."

"She sent me here." Seralune put her hand flat over the signet under her dress. "She left her mark on that wall for me. For whoever woke. And you knew about this house for twenty years, and you let them burn it, and you're standing in it telling me you don't know where she is."

"Yes," said Thaeroval.

It was the most terrible thing he'd said yet, and it was only the truth.

The field came again, and this time it didn't ask. The smoke tore open in front of her. Someone was there, just ahead, so close she could have touched them, and she was reaching out —

She turned and ran.

Not up the lane. Down. The Lily Steps went away from her into the dark towards the river, pale and worn hollow in the middle, and she took them two at a time. One. Two. Three. The river smell came up to meet her. Below, the washing rocks lay flat and grey at the water's edge.

"Sera!"

It came from above her. From the top of the steps. From the lane.

Seralune stopped and turned.

Nereth was there.

She stood at the mouth of the lane with one hand flat against the corner of the house beside it, holding herself up.

She'd lost her scarf. Her hair was coming down, and the dark lines stood out on her throat even from here, and her breath was tearing in and out of her. She'd run all the way from the laundry through the vigil crowd, on legs that couldn't stand.

"Sera," she said again. It was barely a voice.

In the ruin, Thaeroval had not moved.

He was looking at Nereth, who had called her Sera.

"So you have chosen force, then," he said.

He said it hardly louder than a breath. Seralune heard every word. She'd always heard him, across any room, however quietly he spoke.

Then he wasn't in the ruin.

She never saw him cross it. There was the ash, and the black stumps, and the place where he had been standing. There was Nereth at the corner of the lane. And then he was in front of Nereth, and his arm was already coming back, and Seralune saw his face.

For one breath it wasn't his.

It was something she'd never seen on him in her life. Something wide awake and ugly, that had been lying under all that stillness a very long time, and had got up.

He hit her.

It made a sound like a door kicked in. Nereth left the ground.

She went sideways into the wall of the leaning house she'd been holding herself up against, and the wall didn't stop her. It burst inward. Plaster and lath and old timber went in with her, and a beam came down inside with a crack like a tree going over, and then a long, sliding, settling roar of falling things.

Then quiet.

Up on the hill, the hymn went on.

When Seralune looked at her brother again, his face was his own. Flat and still and patient. He stood in the lane with his hand hanging at his side, and dust drifting down round him in the lamplight from the hill, and considered the hole in the wall.

---

She didn't remember climbing the steps.

She was at the hole. It was taller than she was and ragged at the edges, and the dust was still coming out of it, pale in the faint light. She went through it without stopping.

Inside, it was nearly dark.

It was a small room. There was a table on its side and a chair in pieces. The ceiling had come down at one end, and the beam that had held it lay slanting across the far corner, over a bed.

Nereth was on the floor in the rubble.

She lay on her side in a drift of broken plaster, with one arm under her at an angle that arms didn't go. Her eyes were half open. Every breath she took rattled, wet and short, and there was blood on her mouth and on her chin, black in that light.

"Nereth."

Seralune went down on her knees beside her. She didn't know where to touch. There was nowhere that wasn't broken.

"Nereth. Nereth, look at me —"

Nereth's good hand came up and closed on her sleeve, and held on.

On the bed, under the beam, there was a hand.

It lay palm up among the fallen plaster, on the blanket, at the edge of the bed. It was a small hand, thin, with swollen knuckles and loose skin, an old woman's hand. The beam lay across the bed above it, and above the beam the ceiling hung down, and there was nothing else to see.

The hand didn't move.

Seralune looked at it.

She didn't know whose it was. She would never know.

The light from the hole in the wall went out.

He was standing in it. A black shape against the faint gold of the hill, blocking it. Then he stepped through, over the broken lath, into the room.

He didn't hurry. He crossed the rubble slowly and evenly, the way he crossed his own hall at home, where nothing was ever in his way. His knuckles were dark. His face was flat.

He looked at the bed, and the hand on it. Nothing in his face changed.

He looked at Nereth.

Then he looked at Seralune, and waited.

She could see he thought he'd won. He had always been so sure of everything, and he looked sure of this.

Under Seralune's hands, Nereth's whole body arched.

The lines on her throat were moving. They crawled under her skin, alive, and the heat came off them in a wave, and Nereth made the sound again, the low one, the one that didn't stop. Her fingers tore at Seralune's sleeve.

It was Seralune. It was what was in Seralune, coming up. It was her grief and her horror and her rage, going into Nereth and out along the lines and burning her from the inside, like the thatcher, and she couldn't stop it, she couldn't —

The field rose through the floor. The red mud and the smoke and the dying, and the blood on the plaster was the blood on the field, and she was running and kneeling at once, and she was screaming.

"CAN'T YOU SEE SHE'S SUFFERING!?"

It tore her throat. It filled the little room and went out through the hole in the wall into the empty quarter, and there was no one to hear it but him.

She had never screamed at him. Not once, in twenty years of being his sister.

He didn't move. He stood in the rubble with the dust settling on his shoulders, and he looked down at Nereth, at the lines crawling on her throat and the blood on her chin, and he didn't answer.

"Please."

She'd never said that to him either. Not like this.

"Please, Thaer." Her voice was in pieces. "The stair. Tomorrow. They lay them where her shadow falls. Take her there. Let the saint see her. She'll die, Thaer, she's dying, she's been dying since the tunnels and it's me, it's me doing it —"

She couldn't finish. She bent over Nereth and held on.

"And then I'll come," she said into Nereth's hair. "After. I'll come home with you. I won't run again. I'll go back to the room. I'll let them put the chain on." She lifted her head. "Anything. Only take her to the stair."

Her brother looked at her for a long time.

Then he went down on one knee in the plaster, across Nereth from her, and studied Nereth's face.

"I'll carry her," he said.

---

The watch came while he was still kneeling.

Seralune heard the boots first, two pairs, coming fast down the lane. Then a lantern swung past the hole in the wall, came back, and stopped.

"Who's there?" A man's voice, young, trying to be loud. "Temple watch. What's all this? What's —"

Thaeroval stood up.

He didn't hurry. He went to the hole and stood in it, and the lantern light fell on him: the dark armour, the grey cloak, the sword, the dust on his shoulders. His hands at his sides.

He didn't say anything. He only looked at them.

There were two of them, in white and gold, with spears. One was young, and one was older, with a grey moustache. Seralune could see them past her brother's arm. The young one had his spear half lowered. His mouth was open.

The older one looked at Thaeroval's face for a long moment. Then at the sword, and at the ears that showed through the dark hair, and at the broken wall round him.

He put out his hand and took the young one by the sleeve.

"Come on, Wil," he said, very quietly.

"But there's —"

"Come *on*."

They went. They went backwards for the first few steps, with the lantern held up in front of them, and then they turned and walked, fast, and then the lane took them round the corner and there was only the hymn again.

Thaeroval stood in the hole a while longer. Then he came back in.

---

They carried Nereth out on the old woman's table.

He found it on its side in the rubble and knocked its legs off, one after another, with the flat of his hand.

"Take her shoulders," he said.

"Her arm —"

"I know. Take her shoulders. Keep her head still."

They laid her on the top of it, the two of them, one at her shoulders and one at her feet. Nereth screamed once when they moved her arm. Then she stopped making any sound at all.

"Is she —"

"She is breathing." He didn't look up. "Lift when I lift."

It was only a few steps. Out through the hole, across the end of the lane, over the black stump of a wall and into the ruin.

Seralune had the head end. She walked backwards, with the table's edge biting into her fingers, and her brother at the other end, and between them Nereth lying the way the sick were lying on the doors and the hurdles all the way up the hill. Carried, like them. On a stranger's furniture, like them. Going nowhere tonight.

They set her down by the wall beside the shrine, out of sight of the lane, and lifted her off the board into the ash as gently as they could. Above them, at the height of a man's shoulder, the lily was cut into the stone.

Seralune sat down with her back to the wall and took Nereth's head into her lap.

Her brother knelt at Nereth's other side and studied the arm.

Then he went back into the broken house without a word, and came out with two lengths of lath, and a strip torn from a curtain or a blanket. He laid the lath along the arm and bound it, quickly and firmly, as he'd have bound a soldier's on a field. His hands didn't shake. Nereth didn't wake.

When he'd finished, he unfastened his cloak and laid it over her, up to her chin.

"Thank you," said Seralune.

His eyes came to her. For a breath she thought he'd speak. Then he got up and went to stand at the gap where the lane came in, with his back to them.

---

The watch came back an hour later.

There were six this time, with a sergeant, and two lanterns, and a sheet folded over one man's arm. They came down the lane quietly, not like men who'd been sent to arrest anyone. They didn't look at the ruin.

They went in through the hole in the wall. For a while there was the sound of timber being shifted, and men grunting, and one of them swearing, softly, over and over, until the sergeant told him to stop.

Then they came out.

They had her between them, under the sheet. Four men carried her. She made so little shape under it that she might have been a bundle of washing.

Through the gap, Seralune watched them come along the lane past the place where Thaeroval stood. None of them looked at him. They looked at the cobbles, or at the sheet, or straight ahead into the dark.

The sergeant looked. He was the last of them, and he stopped in the lane with his lantern and looked at the man in the gap, and Thaeroval looked back.

The sergeant went on.

The lanterns went away up the lane. The singing came back.

Somewhere in the quarter, Seralune thought, some laundry had given that sheet.

Her brother stood in the gap after the lanterns had gone. Then he turned and crossed the ruin to the shrine.

The stone basin on its plinth still held water. The old priest must have filled it that morning. The painted sun on the wall above it was almost invisible in the dark, a pale round shape. The flowers in their jar had closed for the night.

He put his hands in the basin.

He washed his knuckles. He did it carefully, one hand and then the other, rubbing each finger in turn with the thumb of the other hand. The scar at the base of his right thumb showed pale when he turned it. The water darkened.

He went on washing.

He went on long after there could have been anything left on his hands to wash off. Seralune watched him from the ash, with Nereth's head in her lap. He washed his hands in the dark in the temple's little basin, in the burned house that had kept her mother's secret, under a Mydean sun, and he didn't stop.

When he did stop, he stood with his wet hands resting on the rim of the basin and his head bowed, the way he'd once bowed it over her hand by a fire, on the first night after she woke.

Then he straightened, and dried his hands on his sleeves, and went back to the gap. His face was exactly as it had been.

---

He said almost nothing all night.

Once, when she shivered, he said, "Sleep, if you can." She didn't answer, and he didn't say it again.

Much later she said, to his back, "Will they let you on the stair?"

"Yes."

"You're an elf. In armour. They hang elves here."

"No one will stop me."

He said it as simply as he'd said *yes*. She didn't ask how he knew. She'd seen the watch go backwards up the lane with their lantern held out in front of them, and she knew.

The vigil went on up on the hill. The lamps burned on the stair in their thousands, all night, a long slope of gold going up into the dark, and the hymn came and went with the wind.

Sometimes it stopped for a while, and in the quiet she could hear the river going past the foot of the Lily Steps, and Nereth breathing.

Nereth breathed badly. Each breath caught on something and dragged past it. Under the cloak, the lines on her throat lay dark and swollen, and every time the field stirred in Seralune, they moved.

The jar was in Agna's loft. It hung in Nereth's pack on its nail by the hatch, with the last five drops in it, the ones Seralune should have been counting into the cup about now. She'd run without it. There was nothing between Nereth and the stair any more.

The field came up all night.

She'd thought, in the broken house, that it had come as far as it could come. It hadn't.

It came in pieces, and each piece was sharper than the last: the red mud, the walking silver things, the man with his hands over his face. Her own feet. The smoke tearing open. Someone ahead of her. So close.

And each time, under her hands, Nereth's breath hitched, and the heat came up off the lines on her throat.

It was her. She'd known it in the laundry, and she knew it now. Every time she felt anything, it went into Nereth and burned.

She looked up at her brother.

He was standing in the gap with his back half turned to her, so that she could see him from the side against the gold of the hill. He didn't move. He hadn't moved in an hour. The lamplight lay along his cheek and his jaw, and his face was perfectly still.

Not the stillness of someone holding something down. She knew that stillness. She'd been doing it for three days, and it cost, every moment of it cost. This was different. It was the stillness of a room nobody lived in any more. The face he'd worn at council, and for servants, and for strangers. *Yes. No. Just like that.*

She looked at it for a long time.

Then she made her own face the same.

She did it the way she'd have learned a dance, watching his feet. The mouth first: she let it go the way his was, not closed hard, only closed. Then the jaw.

Then the eyes, and that was the hardest part. She let them go flat and quiet, the way his were, as though whatever they looked at were a long way off and none of her business.

The field came.

It came right up to the back of her eyes, all of it, the smoke and the running and the someone just ahead of her, and it pressed there. And it stayed there. It stayed behind her face the way weather stays behind a window, beating on the glass, and it didn't come through.

Under her hands, Nereth's breath eased.

Seralune felt the heat go down out of the lines on her throat. She felt it drain away, slowly, like the ache after a flash, until the skin under her fingers was only skin, cool and damp.

It worked.

She sat in the ash with Nereth's head in her lap and her brother's face on, and it worked.

It wasn't easy, the way it had been easy at the lily stone three days ago. It was work, all night, like holding a door shut against a wind.

But she had his face to copy now. She didn't have to find the quiet place on her own any more. She only had to look at him, and do what he did.

Once, very late, she nearly asked him.

The words were there. *Thaer. When I was running. There was someone.* She had her mouth open to say it.

She shut it.

It was hers. Whatever was at the end of the field, whoever was in the smoke, it was the only thing in the world tonight that was hers and not his, and she kept it.

She kept it there with the rest.

---

The sky went grey over the river.

On the hill, the lamps on the stair went pale, one by one, as the light came up behind the temple. The hymn had changed. It was slower now, and there were fewer voices in it, the voices of people who'd been singing all night and were nearly done.

Then the bell.

One deep note from the hill. Then another. Then, from all over the city, from every tower and temple and shrine in Favale, the other bells took it up, one after another, until the air over the roofs was full of bells, ringing and ringing for the Longest Light.

Her brother turned from the gap.

He crossed the ruin to where she sat. He looked down at her, and at Nereth, and at the lily in the wall above them, with the soot still in its lines.

Then he knelt.

He slid one arm under Nereth's shoulders and the other under her knees, and gathered her up off Seralune's lap, cloak and splint and all. He did it carefully, as if she weighed nothing, and as if she might break.

Nereth's head fell against his armour. She didn't wake.

He stood up with her in his arms, in the ruin of their mother's secret, with the bells going over his head. He looked at Seralune once.

Then he carried Nereth out through the gap, towards the river road and the hill and the white stair, and Seralune got up out of the ash and followed him.
