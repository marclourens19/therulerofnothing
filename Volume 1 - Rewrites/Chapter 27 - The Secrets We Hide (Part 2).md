# The Secrets We Hide (Part 2)

Nereth had all three coppers rolling before the sky was light.

"Well?" she said, when Seralune came down the ladder. She was flushed with steam. For the first time since the tunnels, she looked pleased with herself.

"Three," said Seralune.

"Before the bell," said Nereth.

At the tubs, Agna grunted. "Not bad," she said.

"From Mam," Tilde told them, "that's a song."

The news came in with the first basket.

It was carried by a stout woman from the hostel on the corner, who had plainly run all the way. She dropped the basket on the table so hard that half the sheets slid off it.

"Four of the watch," she said. "Dead. Last night."

Tilde stopped wringing. Bett let go of the sheet she was folding. At the coppers, Nereth didn't turn round, but Seralune saw her hands go still on the rim.

"Where?" said Agna.

"The old archivist's. Down by the hanging arch." The woman pressed a hand to her chest. "They went in after the bell, four of them, and never came out. Found at the change of watch. All four on the floor, and the supper still on the table. Bowls and bread and all."

"By the Four, Maud," said Agna.

"One of them was only a lad. First year in the white." Maud shook her head. "His mam's been on the watch-house step since they brought him in. You can hear her from the market."

"Who did it?" said Bett. Her eyes had gone round.

"The witch's people."

Agna made a noise in her throat. "The witch is dead. They hanged her yesterday."

"Her people, I said." Maud leaned over the sheets. "There were two, weren't there? Two witches on the pilgrim road. One with her face burned, and one who talked like a lady. And it was the dog the watch went to that house for."

"What dog?" said Tilde.

"The witch's dog. At the hanging it pulled at its rope to get to her, like it knew her. One of the watch saw it. He found out where it was lodging and went back after the bell with three of his mates, and now look."

"Could be anybody's dog," said Tilde.

"And the floor?" said Maud. "The flagstones were torn up. Standing on end like teeth, right across the kitchen. Same as the pilgrim road. The road split open under a man's feet there, didn't it? You tell me whose dog that is."

Seralune stood at the tub with her hands in the grey water.

The road had split open under a man's feet. She'd felt it go. She'd never been near any archivist's kitchen, and she'd never had a dog, and the city was telling her story anyway, with other people's dead.

"And the archivist's gone," Maud said. "Him and three men and the dog. Out into the dark, and not a soul saw which way. The watch'll turn over every house this side of the river by tonight. You mark me."

Agna and Tilde looked at each other.

It was quick. Seralune only caught it because she happened to be facing Tilde. Then Agna said, "Well, they'll find no archivists in my coppers," and went back to her paddle.

"Did you see her?" said Bett.

It took Seralune a breath to understand that the girl meant her.

"See who?"

"The witch! Coming in last night. You'd have come down the river road. She's on the arch." Bett was alight. "They're leaving her up till the Longest Light, so all the pilgrims see."

"She's not a witch," said Tilde. "She's a pot-scrubber."

"She's an elf," said Bett.

"She's an elf who scrubbed pots. In the temple kitchens. For eleven years."

"Eleven years," Maud agreed. "Ran off from her masters up north and hid in the temple kitchens eleven years, and never a soul knew. Right under the saint's feet. They only found her out by the burn."

Seralune heard herself ask, "What burn?"

"Down her face." Maud drew a finger down one side of her own. "Right down, old and shiny. The pilgrims said the witch's face was burned black. Well, there you are."

"What was her name?" said Seralune.

Maud blinked at her. "Nell, the kitchens called her. Her own name, nobody ever asked."

The water in the tub had gone cold round Seralune's wrists.

*A thief,* Nereth had said. On the arch, in the lantern light, turning a little on its rope.

The grief came up under Seralune's ribs, and the rage came up behind it, and the heat came off Nereth before Seralune had even turned round.

Nereth made no sound. She only took her hands off the copper and pressed the heel of one to her collarbone, hard, over her dress. Her face had gone the colour of the lye.

*Count.*

One. The grey water. Two. The drip from the sheets. Three. Bett's voice, still going, about the black sun they'd hung round the witch's neck. Four. Five.

It went down. It didn't go all the way. It went far enough.

"Your friend's gone grey," said Tilde.

"It's the steam," said Nereth.

Agna looked at her for a moment. Then she said, to no one in particular, "Four days. Four days till the Longest Light, and then they all go home and take their sheets with them, and I can hear myself think."

---

Seralune found Nereth in the yard behind the laundry, at the pump.

She was working the handle for no reason. The bucket under the spout had filled and spilled over, and she went on pumping.

"You knew," said Seralune.

Nereth didn't pretend. "I saw her ears."

"You said a thief."

"I know what I said."

"I sat on that roof and laughed." Seralune's voice came out too high. She brought it down. "I laughed, Nereth. And she was —"

"Yes."

"Why?"

Nereth let go of the handle. "Because you'd have gone and stood under her," she said. "All night, in the curfew, looking up at her. I know you. You'd have made yourself look until the watch came for you."

She wiped her wet hands down her skirt.

"And I wanted one night. That's all. I wanted you to have one."

The heat off her scars hit Seralune again, as sudden as a slap.

Seralune shut her eyes and counted it down. She got to seven before Nereth's breathing eased.

When she opened them, Nereth was staring at the yard wall.

"She ran," Nereth said. "Eleven years ago she ran from somebody's house. Somebody's kitchen. And she got all this way."

Seralune had nothing to say to that. She didn't try.

"They've hanged their witch now," Nereth said. Her voice had gone flat and careful, the voice she'd used in the palace, before. "Nobody's going to look twice at my face. Not this week."

"Don't."

"It's true. She bought us that."

"Nereth. Don't."

Nereth picked up the bucket. Water slopped over the rim onto her boots, and she didn't look down.

"The jar's nearly empty," she said. "Did you know?"

Seralune had counted every drop into that cup since the fold. She knew what was left, to the drop. "Four days. If you only take it at night."

"Then I'll only take it at night."

"You'll take it when it hurts."

"It always hurts, Sera." Nereth said it gently, which was worse. "If I took it every time it hurt, it'd have been gone before we left Meren."

Four days left in the jar. Four days to the Longest Light.

Seralune looked up over the yard wall. From here she couldn't see the temple, only chimney smoke going up into a clean blue sky.

She saw it anyway: the white stair, the rows of the sick, the small burning figure at the top.

"Then you're going on that stair," she said.

Nereth laughed, short. "They hang elves here."

"They lay the sick where her shadow falls. You're sick."

"I'm an elf."

"You're a woman with a burn on her face, in a city full of pilgrims with worse," said Seralune. "On the Longest Light, nobody will be looking at anyone but her."

Nereth held her gaze. She didn't say yes. She didn't say no either.

"Four days," she said, and carried the bucket in.

Inside, Bett was sorting the clean sheets into bundles for the hostels. She chanted the names over them as she tied each one off.

"The Sun and Stair. The Two Wells. Widow Ashby's. The Lily Steps. The Lily Steps. The Lily —"

Seralune stopped in the doorway.

"What did you say?"

Bett looked up. "The Lily Steps. It's three hostels. They're all at the Lily Steps, so I call them that."

"Why is it called that?"

"Dunno. It just is." Bett shrugged. "It's steps. Down to the river, in the old quarter, past the palace. Tilde, why's it called the Lily Steps?"

"Why would I know?"

Agna didn't turn round from the coppers.

"There was a house," she said.

Seralune waited.

"Up at the top of the steps. A long time ago. Before you were born, Bett." Agna pushed the sheets down into the boiling water with her paddle. "It burned. There's nothing there now but a priest and his little shrine. Don't go poking round it."

She said it flatly, as she'd said everything else. But she said it to Seralune.

Seralune put her hand to her chest. Under her dress, on its chain, the signet lay against her skin.

Through the cloth she could feel the stone, and the shape cut into it: a single closed lily, the petals folded so tightly that the centre almost vanished.

Her mother had sealed her own letters with it. Not the court's letters. Her own.

"I'm going," she told Nereth, up in the loft, when Agna had gone out with the bundles.

"With the watch on every corner."

"They're looking for three men and a dog."

"They're looking for witches." Nereth was winding her scarf, tight, round and round. "You heard her. The other one talks like a lady."

"Then I won't talk."

"Sera."

"Cyrandor said there was someone in Favale," Seralune said, low. "Someone who could help us find her. If my mother trusted them, they'll know people. A healer. Someone who doesn't need a saint to do it. The jar, Nereth. Four days."

Nereth finished the scarf. She pulled the end through and tucked it under, and faced Seralune in the thin light between the sheets.

"Then I'm coming," she said. "And you're not talking."

---

They went in the middle of the morning, when the streets were fullest.

Nereth had been right about the watch. They stood on the corners in fours and walked the lanes in fours, and every group of men that passed them was looked at twice.

Not one of them looked twice at two women in scarves carrying an empty laundry basket between them.

The basket had been Nereth's idea. "Nobody stops the washing," she'd said, and Agna had handed it over without asking what they wanted it for.

"Hold it like you've carried one before," Nereth said, as they set off.

"I haven't."

"I know. Hold it like you have."

By daylight the river road was a roar. Men were still up ladders hanging lanterns, and a boy went along behind them with a bucket, washing the glass.

Ahead of them, the aqueduct crossed the road.

Seralune looked.

She'd told herself she wouldn't. She looked anyway, because Nereth had been right about that too, and because she owed it.

The woman hung from the middle of the arch, high above the banners, in a grey shift. Her feet were bare.

A sun disc hung round her neck on a chain, scorched black. Her hair had been tied back with a strip of cloth, so that the whole road could see her ears.

The burn ran down the right side of her face.

The right side. Nereth's marks were on the left.

It was on the wrong side of her face, and nobody had cared.

The pilgrims walked under her. A man touched his sun token without looking up. Two children sat on the bottom step of the pier with a heel of bread between them, swinging their legs.

Seralune stood in the road with the basket, and couldn't make her feet move.

The basket tugged. "Sera."

Heat was already coming off Nereth. Seralune could feel it on her cheek.

*One.* She took her eyes off the woman on the arch. *Two.* The banners. *Three.* The road. She walked.

A few doors past the arch, a crowd had gathered outside a tall, narrow house of old brick, squeezed between a chandler's and a blank wall. Four of the watch stood across its door.

Someone had scrubbed the step white once. There were brown marks on it now, dragged across the white, where things had been carried out.

"That's it," said a woman at the back of the crowd. "The archivist's."

"It was the lady one," said the man beside her. "The other witch. Her and her men."

"I heard she wasn't even there," said the woman. "I heard she did it from outside. Through the wall."

"Don't be soft."

"They sent to the laundries for sheets to carry them out in," said a boy at the front, with relish. "Four sheets."

Seralune kept her head down and her mouth shut, as she'd promised, and walked past the house where four men had died beside somebody's supper. She didn't look at it. She'd already looked at the arch.

On the next corner, the watch were taking a dog.

It was a small brindled thing, a ratter, fighting its rope with all four legs braced. A man in a leather apron had hold of the watchman's sleeve.

"She's never been near a witch in her life! She's a ratter! She works the chandler's cellars —"

"Orders," said the watchman. "Every dog over knee height, this side of the river. Take it up with the temple."

"She's not over knee height!"

"She is when she stands up."

Behind the man, a little girl was crying without making a sound, her fists jammed against her mouth.

Seralune stepped towards them.

She didn't think about it. She'd never been able to watch someone dragged where they didn't want to go. She was halfway to the watchman's elbow, with her mouth already open, when Nereth's scars flared beside her, hard.

She stopped.

The watchman walked away with the dog. The man in the apron went after him, still arguing. The little girl stood where she was.

So did Seralune. Then she shut her mouth.

"I know," Nereth said quietly. "I know. Walk."

The midday bell rang as they came in sight of the old palace.

The whole street stopped under it. All round them, people touched two fingers to their foreheads, waited, and went on.

Seralune was a beat late. Nereth wasn't. Nereth had picked it up on the pilgrim road, and Seralune had never noticed her learning it.

The old palace sat low and dark by the river. Two banners hung over its gate. One was Mydea's, white with a gold sun.

The other was white, with a gold tree.

It hung a hand's breadth higher than the sun.

"That's your father's," Nereth said, barely moving her lips.

"I know whose it is."

At a fruit stall on the corner across from the gate, two people were buying cherries.

They wore grey and green.

Seralune knew the colours before she knew anything else. Grey and green, cut close and plain: the dress of a royal attendant. The dress Nereth had been wearing the first morning Seralune opened her eyes.

The two of them stood straight-backed, a man and a girl, with their eyes on the fruit and their ears bare to the whole street. Two of the temple watch stood behind them with their spears grounded, guarding them from the street.

Nereth's end of the basket went rigid.

"Turn round," she breathed.

"What —"

"Turn round. The girl. That's Yseth, from the east kitchens."

Seralune turned round. She stood with her back to the palace and her face in the shadow of her hood, and stared at a stall of onions, and listened.

"Four baskets," the fruit woman was saying behind her. "Every day. Paid in silver, mind."

The man in grey and green answered too low to hear. He spoke the human tongue carefully, and badly.

"Cherries, yes. All the cherries. First of the year!"

When Seralune dared to glance back, the servants were crossing to the gate with their baskets, the watch behind them.

A boy trailing after his mother had stopped dead to stare at their ears. His mother took him by the collar and hauled him on.

No one touched them. No one spat, or said *witch*.

As soon as they'd gone, the talk started.

"Raw fruit," said the fruit woman to the onion man. "Nothing cooked. Won't have anything that's been near a fire. And they send half of it back."

"Come for the Longest Light," said the onion man. "To stand up on the stair beside her, so the pilgrims can see who's friends with who."

"You don't know that," said the fruit woman.

"Everyone knows that. Why else would you come in pilgrim season?"

"War," said a woman buying onions. "They want the frontier roads for their war on the beastfolk. That's what my husband says. They'll have our roads and our grain and our boys, and the king'll bow and say thank you."

"He bowed first," said the fruit woman. "In his own hall. Did you hear that?"

"Everyone's heard that," said the woman with the onions.

"My cousin's on the palace gate." The onion man dropped his voice. "He says they've lost something up north. Somebody they want back. Some lad who walked off a battlefield."

The woman laughed at him. "Walked off a battlefield. You'll believe anything."

Seralune didn't turn round. Her father's tree hung over that gate. Somebody had ridden to Favale under it, in a white carriage, through the sick on the pilgrim road.

She thought of Leorin, who would come to a human festival if he were told to, and smile through every hour of it.

She thought of her father, and couldn't make the picture fit.

She thought of Thaer, and put that down quickly, before it could turn into anything.

"Would she know you?" she whispered. "The girl."

"She slept in the bed next to mine for four years." Nereth was already moving. "The Lily Steps are that way. Past the palace. Don't look at the gate."

Seralune didn't look at the gate.

The old quarter was older than anything they'd walked through yet.

The houses leaned together over the lanes until the sky between the eaves was a ribbon. There were no banners here, and no lanterns.

The Lily Steps went down to the river between two of those leaning houses: forty or fifty steps of pale stone, worn hollow in the middle. At the bottom, women were beating washing on the flat rocks, and the slap of wet linen came up the steps with their voices.

At the top, where a house should have stood, there was a gap.

The walls were black stumps, none of them higher than Seralune's shoulder. A doorway still stood, with its burned lintel across it and nothing on either side. An elder sapling had rooted in the old hearth.

In one corner, built into what was left of two walls, stood a little shrine: a stone basin on a plinth, a sun painted above it, a jar of fresh flowers. Its stones were clean. It was newer than everything round it.

In the wall beside it, at the height of a man's shoulder, one stone had been carved.

It was a closed lily, the petals folded so tightly that the centre almost vanished.

Soot had got into the lines and never come out.

Seralune put down her end of the basket.

She went to the stone and put her fingers on it. It was warm from the sun, and rough, and the lines were shallow with weather, and it was her mother's. It was exactly her mother's.

"Is it hers?" Nereth whispered.

"It's hers."

With her other hand she pressed the signet flat through her dress. The same lily lay there, small and cold, with the nick in the band from when she was seven.

Her mother had been here. Or her mother had sent someone here, before anyone in this city was born. Someone had cut this into the wall for her, or for whoever came after her.

*For me,* Seralune thought. *For whoever woke.*

She searched. She couldn't help it. She went along every wall and felt every stone she could reach. She knelt in the weeds where the hearth had been. She pushed at the blackened stones round the lily in case one of them moved.

None of them moved. There was nothing behind the lily but wall, and nothing under the weeds but ash and broken slate.

"Sera," said Nereth.

"There'll be something."

"Sera. Someone's coming."

An old man was climbing the steps from the river with a broom over his shoulder and a bucket in his other hand. His white and gold had gone soft and yellow with washing.

He saw two women in his ruin and didn't seem surprised. He nodded to them, set his bucket down by the shrine, and began to sweep.

"You found the lily," he said.

Seralune stood up. "Yes."

"Everybody does." He swept round the plinth. "It's a pretty thing. People stop. That's why they left it."

"Who left it?"

"The temple." He glanced up at her. He had a creased, kind face and very clear eyes. "When they cleaned the ground. They could have had that stone out, but they left it, so people would ask. And then I tell them."

"Tell them what?"

He leaned on his broom. He looked, Seralune thought, like a man who'd been waiting for the question and was glad of it, and wanted to do it justice.

"You're not from here," he said.

"The coast."

It was the answer she'd rehearsed for the gate. The gate hadn't asked.

"Then you won't have heard it." He settled himself. "There was a family lived here. Humans. Good people, by all accounts. Kept themselves to themselves. They'd been in this house longer than anyone could remember, mother to daughter and father to son, and every one of them kept an elf's secret."

"An elf's secret," said Seralune.

"Generations, they say. Something left with them a long time ago, by one of that lot up north. And they kept it. They put that lily on their wall for it, so whoever it was would know the house." He bent and picked a leaf out of the basin. "Elf-friends, people called them afterwards."

"After what?"

"In the last summer of the war," the priest said, "riders came. Twenty years ago, near enough. Pale armour, every one of them, and a warrant with a tree on it. A tree with stars. They came in under a white flag, right through the city, and rode up those steps on their horses. I was younger then. I saw it."

Seralune didn't move.

"The temple read the warrant on the steps. Out loud, so everyone heard. And then the temple stood aside." He said it without any bitterness. "There was peace being talked about, you see, and there'd been enough burning. What's one house against a whole kingdom?"

"And the family?"

"They came out on their own feet. All of them. Seven. The grandmother first, and she told the rest to."

"Were there children?" said Nereth.

"Three. The smallest was carried out in her mother's arms."

Nereth made a sound. It was very small. She didn't ask anything else.

"Nobody lifted a hand," the priest said. "You don't, against a warrant like that. They took them alive. North, I suppose. Then they set the house alight behind them, and sat their horses in the street till the roof fell in, so nobody could carry anything out."

He surveyed the ruin with a kind of sad contentment.

"That autumn, the war ended. Some said that was the price of the peace. I don't know about that. I know the temple built this after, to make the ground clean. I've kept it nineteen years."

Seralune found she was holding on to the stone.

Pale armour. A tree with stars. She'd seen that armour on the pilgrim road, riding through the sick without slowing, round a carriage with her father's tree on the door.

Twenty years ago, her father's riders had come to this house for her mother's friends, and for whatever her mother had left with them. They'd taken the children too.

Cyrandor's voice came back to her, from her room in the palace with the fire nearly dead. *I can't promise that person is still waiting, Your Highness.*

Nobody was waiting. No one had been, for twenty years.

"Do you know what the secret was?" she asked. Her voice was steady. She didn't know how.

The priest smiled. "That's the thing about secrets, isn't it? Nobody ever does." He took up his broom again. "But there's a lesson in it, and that's why I tell it. Secrets don't keep in the Light. Whatever you hide, it comes out on the steps in the end, where everyone can see."

"What were they called? The family."

He shook his head. "The temple struck their name off the rolls. I don't say it." He said it gently, as though he were sorry to refuse her. "It'd be a poor sort of cleaning if I went about saying it."

She felt it coming.

The grief first. Then the rage behind it. And behind both of them, bigger than either, a thing she had no name for: seven people walking out of their own door; a child carried in her mother's arms; her father's riders sitting their horses in the street while the roof came down.

Beside her, Nereth drew a breath through her teeth. Her scars were burning. Seralune could feel it from a pace away.

The priest looked round. "Is your friend unwell?"

"She's tired," said Seralune.

"Bring her to the stair on the Longest Light." He was kind. He was entirely kind. "Lay her where the Ruler's shadow falls. She'll be seen to."

Seralune reached for the count, and didn't need it.

It went out of her.

It went all at once, like water through a crack in the bottom of a jar. The grief, the rage, the thing behind them. It simply went, and left her standing in a burned house in the sun with her hand on a carved stone, feeling nothing.

It was very quiet. It was almost restful.

She could see everything clearly: the priest's kind face, the soot in the lily's lines, the broom, the flowers in the jar. She could see all of it, and none of it could reach her.

"Thank you," she said to the priest. "You've been very kind."

He blessed her, two fingers to his own forehead and then to hers, and went back to his sweeping.

Nereth caught her sleeve.

"They've stopped," she whispered.

Seralune turned to her.

"The marks." Nereth's hand was pressed flat to her collarbone. "They were burning. They've stopped. Just now. All at once." She searched Seralune's face. "Sera. What did you do?"

"Nothing."

"You did something."

"I didn't do anything." It was true. She hadn't done anything. She'd only let it go, and it had gone, and nothing at the top of the Lily Steps had burned. "It stopped hurting you. Isn't that what we wanted?"

Nereth stared at her.

Seralune picked up her end of the basket. After a while, Nereth picked up hers.

They went back the way they'd come.

"Sera," Nereth said, on the river road. "Say something."

"What would you like me to say?"

Nereth didn't answer that.

At the arch, Seralune stopped under the woman on the rope and made herself look up, on purpose, for as long as she could bear it.

She could bear it for as long as she liked. She felt nothing.

Beside her, Nereth's marks stayed quiet all the way home.

---

They'd been back an hour when the watch came into the lane.

Agna came in from the yard at a run. "Up," she said. "Now. Not a sound." She shoved them towards the ladder. "If they come up, you're my cousin's girls from the coast, and you're simple, the pair of you. Don't open your mouths."

They went up the ladder into the loft. Agna took her place at its foot with an armful of wet sheets.

Between two of the sheets at the loft's edge, Seralune could see down into the laundry: the coppers, the tubs, the table, the door. Nereth crouched beside her. Her breathing was loud in the cramped space. Seralune's wasn't.

"If they come up —" said Nereth.

"They won't."

They heard the watch before they saw them. Boots came down the lane, and fists hit doors, and a voice said the same words at every house, a little more tired each time.

Then the voice was at Agna's door.

"Afternoon, mother. Temple watch."

There were four of them, and a sergeant with a knot of cord on his shoulder and a book under his arm. They came in wiping their boots on the wet floor, which made no difference to anything.

"Three men and a dog," the sergeant said. "And an old man with them. Tall, thin. An archivist. Have you seen them?"

"There's no archivists in my coppers," said Agna.

"Then you won't mind us looking."

They looked. They weren't rough about it. They were thorough.

"Smells better than the tannery," one of them said, lifting a sheet with his spear.

"Everything smells better than the tannery," said another.

They prodded the hanging sheets with their spear butts and checked behind each one. They opened the cupboards. Tilde stood by the table with her arms folded. Bett stood beside her with her hands knotted in her apron.

One of the watchmen came to the foot of the ladder and peered up.

Seralune looked straight down at him through the gap between the sheets. He couldn't see her. She watched his hand go to the ladder and close on a rung, and she felt nothing about it.

"Sergeant," said a voice from the coppers.

The youngest of them had knelt in front of the little iron door at the end of the coppers, with a lantern.

"Well, now," he said. "Who's this?"

Kit came out.

He came out slowly, on his hands and knees, with soot on his face and cobwebs in his hair, and stood up in front of the watch. He didn't grin.

"My sister's boy," said Agna. "Visiting. He's nine."

The sergeant studied Kit. "He's twelve if he's a day."

"He's small for it."

"He's big for nine." The sergeant opened his book. "Tested?"

"Next testing day."

"Where's his paper?"

Agna didn't answer.

"What's your name, son?" said the sergeant.

Kit looked at his mother. Then, because he'd been brought up to answer when he was spoken to, he said, "Kit."

The sergeant wrote it down. He wrote for some time. Seralune watched the pen move across the page and felt nothing.

"Untested," the sergeant said as he wrote. "Male child, about twelve. Found concealed in a fuel hole." He looked up. "He'll go to the cup with the next lot, mother. If he's Faint, he goes where he's placed. That's the law."

"I'll pay the bond," said Agna.

"With what?" He didn't say it unkindly. He took in the laundry: the tubs, the wet floor, the sheets. "The bond's more than this house is worth. Take it up with the stewards. They sit on the fourth day."

"Please." Agna's voice broke. "Please. He can't warm his own hands. Three drops, on a good day. What use is he to anybody?"

"That's what the placing's for, mother," said the sergeant.

He closed the book on his thumb.

"Anyone else in the house?"

"My girls," said Agna.

The sergeant turned to Kit. "That right, son? Anyone else?"

Kit looked at the floor. "No, sir."

Up in the loft, Nereth's hand closed on Seralune's wrist.

It tried to come up in Seralune then, from a long way down, under the quiet. It had Kit's grin in it, and the roof, and the lanterns, and a sooty finger tracing the way the watch walked. She felt it start.

She put it back. It was easy. It was the easiest thing she'd ever done.

The young watchman took Kit by the shoulder. He wasn't rough either.

"Mam?" said Kit.

"Go with them," said Agna. "Be good. Don't give them any trouble. Go with them."

Then she said nothing else at all.

Bett had started to cry. Tilde had both arms round her and was holding her so hard that neither of them could move. The watchman at the ladder still had one hand on the rung.

"Sergeant," he said. "The loft?"

The sergeant looked up at the sheets. Then at his book, and the door, and the boy.

"It's washing," he said. "We've forty houses to do before the bell, and I'm not climbing into every loft in the quarter after three grown men. They'd have broken the ladder."

The hand came off the rung.

They went. The boots went up the lane. A fist hit the next door, and the tired voice said, "Afternoon, mother. Temple watch."

Kit didn't look back. He walked out between them with his head up and his shoulders square. He'd never been past the end of the lane.

In the loft, Nereth was crying without a sound. Her face was wet, her mouth was shut tight, and her hand was still round Seralune's wrist. Under her collar, the marks lay quiet. Nothing in the loft burned.

If they hadn't found him, they'd have climbed the ladder.

Seralune knew it the same way she knew how many drops were left in the jar. As a number, with nothing attached.

Below them, Agna stood in the middle of the wet floor. She stood there a long time.

"Mam," said Tilde.

Agna picked up her paddle. "The Two Wells want their sheets before the bell," she said, and went back to the coppers.

---

Agna brought their supper up the ladder after dark.

She came up far enough to set the two bowls on the boards, and no further.

"You did the coppers," she said. "That's supper. Coppers at first light, same as today."

"Thank you," said Nereth.

"Thank you," said Seralune.

Agna's eyes rested on her. Whatever she heard in it, she didn't say. She went back down the ladder, and they heard her below in the dark, putting things where they went.

They ate. Afterwards Nereth unwound her scarf for the night.

In the light from the hatch, Seralune saw that the marks had crept further. They'd come down past her collarbone now, fine as hairs, and spread under the edge of her dress towards her breastbone.

Seralune looked at them. Then she took the jar and the cup out of Nereth's pack, and counted five drops into the cup.

One. Two. Three. Four. Five.

She added the water and gave it to Nereth.

Nereth held the cup without drinking.

"You didn't lose count," she said.

"No."

"In the fold, you lost count at three. Your hands were shaking so much you tipped it out and started again."

"I remember."

Nereth drank. She set the cup down carefully.

"Are you all right?" she said.

"Yes."

Nereth watched her in the dark.

"You sound like your brother," she said.

Seralune didn't answer.

"When he talks to us. To servants. Yes. No. Just like that." Nereth's voice shook. "As if there's nobody in there at all."

Seralune opened her mouth. She didn't know what was going to come out of it.

Two knocks came from below.

She was on her feet before she knew she'd moved. Her heart was going. The quiet split right down the middle, and the palace came up through it all at once: the pale room, the lock on the outside of the door, footsteps stopping, and her mother's voice.

*Two knocks are a warning. They are not permission.*

She was at the hatch. She looked down.

In the dark laundry, at the end of the coppers, Agna was on her knees on the wet floor in front of the little iron door. Her knuckles were against it.

As Seralune watched, she knocked again. Twice.

Nobody came out.

Agna stayed where she was, on her knees, with her hand against the iron.

Seralune sat down on the boards among the sheets.

Thaer was across the sea, she told herself. He didn't know where she was. In all the world, only Nereth knew where she was, and Nereth was right here.

Below her, Agna knocked twice more.

Seralune counted.
