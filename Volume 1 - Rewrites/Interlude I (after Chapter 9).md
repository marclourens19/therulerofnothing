# Interlude: I

Tobin brought her water at first light.

He came down the row slower than usual, one hand on the wall, and when he reached her door she saw why. His lower lip had split and swollen to twice its size. A bruise had spread from his temple up into his thin grey hair, dark as spilled ink.

"Brought you the good cup," he said, and passed it through the bars. It was the only one on the row without a chip. "Don't tell the others."

"Tobin. Your face."

"Came at me through the bars while I was sleeping." He touched the lip and winced. "Didn't even have the decency to wake me first."

He tried to smile at his own joke. It opened the split again.

Darcy held the cup in both hands. The water was cold. He'd drawn it fresh from the well in the yard, which meant he'd climbed two flights on those knees to fetch it.

"When they ask," she said carefully, "tell them you were struck from behind."

"I was struck from the front, girl. Bars and all." He shrugged, and that hurt him too. "Doesn't much matter, does it? I was asleep."

His eyes went to the lamp on her desk. It had burned down to a blue bead, and the ash of Alaric's drawing still lay curled in the bottom of its cage.

"That wants trimming."

"Leave it." Too quick. She made herself soften it. "I like it low."

He looked at her a little longer than he needed to. Then he nodded, the same way he'd nodded the winter her fever broke, when he'd left a candle burning past curfew so she wouldn't wake alone in the dark. He had never once mentioned it since.

"I'll leave it, then."

He went back up the row with his hand on the wall. She listened to his uneven steps all the way to the gate. She knew exactly what was coming for him, and she couldn't find one word that would save him without setting the quarter alight.

---

Gilmot came an hour later. His were the only steps on that stair that never hurried.

He stopped outside her door and looked at the lock before he looked at her. He tried it. It held. The candle in the sconce beside him leaned towards his hand and stayed there.

"Good morning, Darcy."

"Captain."

"Our visitors spent a long time on this row for men who only wanted a grate." His coat was buttoned to the collar and the gold on it was bright, as if he'd slept well. Perhaps he had. "Help me understand that."

It was how he'd always begun. Help me understand the eastern bank. Help me understand why the bridge will fail. As if they were two scholars bent over the same problem, and the collar had never been heated at all.

"The grate's the only way out of this fort that doesn't pass a gate," she said. "It's old, and the lock's older. Somebody had to find the right key on a ring of forty, in the dark, with the yard waking up over their heads."

"And how did they know about a grate that isn't on my plans?"

"Because the men who built this fort left it off theirs, and I corrected what I could see from this desk." She let some of her tiredness into it. She didn't have to pretend very hard. "The sewer men who clear it every spring know it's there. So does anyone who's ever bought one of them a drink."

Gilmot considered that. The candle flame bent lower.

"You'll add it to the plans."

"Yes, Captain."

She had kept that throat of stone off every sheet she'd ever drawn of this fort. She gave it up in two words, and he never knew there had been anything to give.

"Your neighbours say they walked past every cell without stopping." He sounded almost disappointed in them. "Not one door opened."

Darcy kept her hand flat on the map.

The woman with the sleeping child had watched them come. So had the old man who broke his bread into portions for people he couldn't see. Every one of them had seen Silas stand at this door with the keys in his hand.

Not one of them had said so.

---

Lighter steps followed. The Natharul officer came along the row as if the fort were a room it had been asked to wait in. Gold lines moved over the dark armour. In the grey light from the high grate, the elf's face was pale, fine-boned and impossible to read.

"Captain." Each word came out cut clean from the next. "One of our hunters saw a human boy leave the drain by the eastern wall before dawn. Bloodied hands. He ran into the settlement outside it." A pause. "Your Faint quarter."

Gilmot didn't turn. "Then the provision stands."

The candle flame climbed his fingers.

Darcy had known this was coming from the moment she told Alaric where the water came out. She'd had the whole of the dark to prepare for it. Her heart went anyway, hard and fast, and she made her voice go the other way.

"The drain empties where the fort's water goes. They built the quarter there because nobody else wanted the ground. Anyone coming out of that throat would run through it. There's nowhere else to run."

"That would be true," Gilmot said, "of a quarter that helped him."

"A quarter that helped him wouldn't have let your hunters see him." She turned the map slightly, as if the answer lay somewhere on it. "And the provision is for my removal. I'm still here."

Overhead, the Wind core pulsed. Blue-green light ran along the copper line above her door and was gone.

"Burn it, and you burn the hands that charge that core. Your ship leaves this morning with half a charge. The next one waits until the roads dry, and the frontier waits with it."

She hadn't asked him for anything. She'd only shown him the ground. He had always liked that best.

Gilmot smiled, the small way he did when a pupil surprised him.

"Precisely argued. The provision is for your removal, and you haven't been removed." He lowered his hand, and the flame settled. "The quarter stays as it is."

Darcy let her shoulders come down by the width of a hair.

"You've burned something," Gilmot said.

He was looking at the lamp.

She hadn't prepared for this. She'd spent the whole night on the grate and the drain and the quarter, and she had left the ash exactly where it lay, because ash in a lamp was nothing and ash on the floor was a question. She'd been sure of it.

Her mind, which had never once failed her in front of him, went blank as a fresh sheet.

"The third frontier revision," she heard herself say. "I'm not going to sign it. I didn't see why it should exist."

Gilmot looked at her for a long time.

Then he laughed, very softly, the way a man laughs at a child who has hidden her supper under the table.

"Then you'll draw it again on the ship. And a fourth, for the trouble."

"Yes, Captain."

The elf hadn't moved. Its gaze had gone to the lamp as well, to the one corner of paper the flame hadn't reached. A curve of ink showed on it. Part of a circle, drawn inside another.

Nobody drew frontier revisions in circles.

The elf's eyes stayed on the ash. Then they moved to the ink on Darcy's forefinger, and to the tremor she hadn't managed to hide.

The elf said nothing at all.

---

"There is still the matter of the gate," Gilmot said. "Bring him down."

Two soldiers brought Tobin down between them. He tried to walk on his own and couldn't; his knees had given out completely on the stair. When he saw Gilmot, he pulled himself as straight as his back allowed.

"Captain. I'm sorry, Captain. I was —"

"A man slept at his post while thieves took his keys." Gilmot said it gently, and to Darcy, as though Tobin weren't there. "Negligence needs correcting. What would you recommend?"

Tobin's eyes found hers through the bars.

If she told the truth, the quarter would burn before noon.

"Dismissal," she said. "He's old. Send him home."

"You were always merciful."

Gilmot drew off his gloves one finger at a time and handed them to the nearest soldier. Then he hit Tobin in the face.

The old man fell back against the bars of the cell opposite. Gilmot followed him down without hurrying. He struck him again, and again, with the same measured care he used to straighten a transfer order, while the candle flame in the sconce stretched towards his bare knuckles and the prisoners on the row turned their faces to the wall. Tobin stopped trying to cover his head. Then he stopped making any sound at all.

Darcy didn't look away. She had learned long ago that he noticed when she did.

Gilmot straightened. His breathing hadn't changed.

"Tie it," he said.

The soldiers bound Tobin's wrists and ankles with harness strap while he lay on the stone. One of them hesitated, looking at the old man's face, and Gilmot waited until he'd finished.

"It won't guard a gate again. It won't keep a ledger." Gilmot took a handkerchief from his sleeve and wiped each knuckle clean. "But there's mana in it. Put it on the end bench and run it until we reach the frontier. If it runs dry before then, drop it over the plain."

They carried Tobin up the stair between them like a sack of grain.

Gilmot pulled his gloves back on and turned to her. His voice was exactly as it had been.

"We sail within the hour. I'll be beside you the whole way."

---

They brought her up through the fort in iron cuffs lined with felt, so they wouldn't mark her wrists. That had been Gilmot's idea. He'd never liked her hands damaged.

The airship hung over the yard on four chains, its hull dark with oil, its belly glowing blue-green where the core sat. Halfway up the gangway, the wind off the plain found her and pulled her hair loose, and for three steps she was only cold. It was almost a relief.

Through an open hatch below the deck, she saw the harness benches. Tobin lay strapped along the last one where they'd dropped him, copper rings clamped round both wrists. Blue-green light crawled up his arms under the skin and drained away into the core's housing. His eyes were open. They didn't follow anything.

The chains came free one at a time.

**KRRRNNNK.**

The deck tilted, steadied, and rose. Every pulse that lifted them was partly him.

Gilmot stood at her shoulder by the rail. He didn't touch her. He'd never needed to.

Kelmend fell away beneath them.

She found the southern gate first, and the crooked roofline beside it that was Marta's inn. A thread of smoke rose from its chimney. Then came the walls, the fort, and the long grey line of the eastern wall.

Outside it lay the Faint quarter.

Darcy counted the smoke.

Eleven chimneys. Twelve. Thin grey threads going straight up in the still air, the way smoke rises from bread ovens and cookfires. No black. No roofs open to the sky. A woman was pegging washing between two sheds, and she didn't look up at the ship at all.

Darcy's cuffed hands stopped shaking on the rail.

Not one house.

She'd been ten the last time she looked down on water from this high. Her father had carried her on his shoulders up the aqueduct steps in Favale the night before her examination, and they'd watched the lamps of the river barges drift away into the dark. She'd asked him where the water went. The sea, he said. She'd asked if it ever got lost on the way. He said water couldn't get lost. It went round things, and under them, and sometimes it waited a very long time, but it always got there in the end.

"So will we," he'd told her. "If they take you, we'll follow it. All the way to the sea."

The next morning she'd let three drops fall into a silver cup, and they had taken her.

Somewhere down there, a boy who couldn't remember his own life was walking towards Favale with her words in his head. He'd come into a fort full of soldiers for a woman he'd never met, and then he'd let her say no.

*Water always finds its way to the ocean.*

She shaped the words without a sound, facing into the wind so Gilmot wouldn't see her mouth move.

Beside her, he lifted a hand to shelter the candle he'd brought up from below. The flame leaned into his palm the way it always did, patient and bright, and the ship turned its nose towards the frontier.

Darcy watched Kelmend until the smoke was too small to count.

Then she kept watching the place where it had been.
