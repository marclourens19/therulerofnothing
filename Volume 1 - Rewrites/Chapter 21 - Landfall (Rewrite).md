# Landfall

Mydea came out of the morning haze as a darker line along the water.

Seralune watched it from beside the forward mast while the line grew roofs, then chimneys, then a wall built right down to the sea. Smoke rose inland in thin grey columns. Fishing boats were crossing the harbour mouth, their brown sails turning for shore one after another.

None of it looked like an enemy country.

A bell rang from the harbour. Three slow strokes came over the water before the wind broke them up.

"Meren!" Neldor called from the stern.

Sailors answered him from both sides of the deck. A rope came loose above Seralune and ran hissing through its wooden ring. She was out of its way before anyone had to tell her. A week aboard had taught her that much: when the crew needed the deck, it wasn't hers.

Nereth came up through the forward hatch with both packs hanging off one shoulder.

She'd wound a plain grey scarf over her hair and round the points of her ears, and a worn brown cloak covered the clothes Neldor had found for her in Waluna. The scarf shadowed the black-purple marks below her left eye without hiding them. Against her pale skin they looked almost painted, until you followed them branching up into her hairline.

Seralune crossed the deck and took the larger pack off her.

"I was carrying that."

"You were carrying both."

"Only until I found you." Nereth settled the lighter pack before Seralune could swap them back. "The medicine's in this one. If you drop the food, we go hungry. If I drop the medicine, Nyana swims after us to complain about it."

"She'd reach the harbour before we did."

"Then let's spare Neldor the embarrassment."

Nereth's humour lasted from the hatch to the mast. Her strength didn't. By the time she got there, the colour had gone out of her face, and one foot had started coming down more carefully than the other.

Seralune set the food pack by her boots. "How much does it hurt?"

"Enough that I'd rather not talk about it where Nyana can hear."

"Worse than last night?"

"No. I'm tired, and the whole ship has learned to listen when you ask me that."

Nereth reached up and tugged at the edge of Seralune's scarf. One ear had pushed its shape through the cloth.

"Turn your head."

Seralune turned it. Nereth loosened the fold and drew it lower along the side of her face.

"There. Remember which name you're using."

"Sera."

"And try to sound less like someone people bow to."

Nyana arrived with a small wooden cup in time to hear the last of it.

"What are you two whispering about?"

"Whether Sera can get through an inspection without giving anybody orders," Nereth said.

"I'm standing right here," Seralune said.

Nyana handed her the cup. "Then drink. You can defend yourself once we're not docking."

The water tasted of the cask. Seralune drank it while Nyana looked Nereth's face over in the open light.

Nyana had stopped asking permission before checking the scars on the fourth day. Nereth had stopped objecting on the sixth. Nyana's thumb rested below the lowest dark branch without quite touching it.

"The colour hasn't spread," she said. "Did the pain wake you?"

"Once."

"Twice," Seralune said.

Nereth turned to her. "You were asleep the first time."

"You knocked the water cup over."

"That could've been the ship."

Nyana took the empty cup back. "If the ship starts swearing under its breath, I'll apologise to you both."

She pressed two fingers under Nereth's jaw and counted quietly, and stepped back before worry could turn into another argument.

"No running," she said. "No carrying the heavy pack because Sera's looking the other way. If the marks go hot, or you feel anything move under the skin, you stop, and you find someone trained to help."

"You've said that every morning."

"And yesterday I caught you carrying a crate. I'll keep saying it till you're off my ship."

Nereth nodded as though she'd heard it for the first time.

Seralune had thought once, on the night of the memory, that she'd seen the dark lines turn under Nereth's fingers. She'd been crying at the time, and the lantern had been poor. Nyana had checked every morning since and found nothing moving. Seralune let the thought go back where it had come from.

The harbour bell rang again.

This time a yellow flag climbed the watchtower at the end of the northern pier. A white one followed it up.

Neldor left the stern faster than he'd moved all voyage. Whatever had been amusing him that morning was gone under the habits of a captain bringing his ship into a foreign port.

"Customs inspection before unloading," he told the crew. "Military cargo stays sealed till they clear it. Deren, cover the aft hold. Pol, find the manifest you swore you put in my desk."

"It is in your desk."

"Then find the part of my desk that stole it."

The crew scattered.

Neldor stopped in front of Seralune and Nereth. He looked at their scarves first, then at the packs.

"The harbour's changed its signal."

"Is that unusual?" Seralune asked.

"It means they're boarding everyone carrying military supplies. You two weren't on the departure record when we left Waluna."

Nyana folded her arms. "They needed treatment."

"Tell them that. Don't expect it to satisfy them."

"I've got a treatment book. They can read it for themselves."

Neldor nodded and turned back to Seralune.

"You can wait below while I talk to them. But if they compare the record with the people aboard, you hiding down there is going to be harder to explain than you standing up here."

The open hatch waited behind Nereth.

Seralune pictured herself under the deck while strangers questioned Neldor about choices she'd asked him to make. He'd already carried them across the sea and fed them from his crew's stores.

She picked the food pack up again.

"We'll stay where they can see us."

Neldor accepted it with a short nod. "Then remember your name."

"Sera."

"And you?" he asked Nereth.

"Nereth. Nobody here has any reason to know it."

"Nereth, then."

Nereth's name still belonged to her. Seralune's could lead a stranger all the way back to a palace room with its door locked from the outside. She said the smaller name over to herself while Meren grew in front of them.

Sera.

It didn't feel false. It felt unfinished.

---

The ship came into Meren behind two fishing boats and a broad barge riding low under a load of timber.

Stone arms closed round the harbour on either side, whitewashed along their upper courses so brightly in the sun that Seralune had to narrow her eyes. Gold circles were set into the walls at regular intervals, each with eight straight rays round an empty centre.

The same mark flew above the watchtower.

Mydea's sun.

She'd known Favale was named for the God of Light. She'd expected temples, perhaps statues. She hadn't expected his sign on warehouse doors and harbour posts, and on the iron brackets that held the signal lamps above the piers.

Meren met the ship in noise. Porters shouted numbers along the quay. Cart wheels rattled in grooves worn deep into the stone. Gulls fought over baskets of silver fish while a woman below them swung a broom and used language that made two sailors laugh out loud.

And there were humans everywhere.

In Waluna, the one human she'd seen had waited under a cooper's awning for the street to empty before he crossed it. Here they filled every street she could see. Men and women moved between ropes and stalls and stacked cargo in clothes made for work, not war. A boy sat on an upturned crate eating a yellow fruit while his father mended a net beside him. Three old women argued over the price of salt. A young porter walked past with a barrel on one shoulder, singing so badly that everyone near him kept supplying the right words.

Nobody looked like the enemy from the histories she'd learned.

The first mooring line went across and tightened round a black post. The hull struck the fenders with a low wooden groan.

The gangplank had barely settled when two officials came aboard.

The first was a narrow human man going grey above both ears, with a leather ledger held against his chest. He wore a white tabard edged in gold over an ordinary brown coat. The woman behind him wore Mydean mail and a short sword, and a sun-shaped clasp pinned her pale cloak at one shoulder.

She left the sword alone.

Neldor met them by the mast and held out the manifest.

"Captain and owner?" the clerk asked.

"Neldor. Registered at Waluna."

The clerk unfolded the sheets and checked the seal pressed into the lower corner. "Waluna registry. Natharul military contract."

"Everything's where the record says it is."

"That would put you ahead of three ships this morning."

He handed one page to the soldier. She went below with a sailor, and he stayed by the mast, reading each line and then looking up to count the people on deck against it.

His count reached Seralune and Nereth.

"Those two aren't crew."

"They worked their passage," Neldor said.

"They're not on your departure record."

"They came aboard before we left Waluna."

The clerk lifted the ledger an inch. "Which is exactly what a departure record is for."

Nyana stepped in before Neldor could answer. "One of them couldn't stay conscious long enough to stand in the harbour office. She needed treatment."

The clerk looked at the scars across Nereth's face. "You're the ship's healer?"

"Medic."

"You keep a treatment log?"

Nyana went below and came back with a book wrapped in waxed cloth. She opened it at a page marked with a strip of blue thread.

The clerk read the entry without taking the book from her. "Found near Waluna. Severe burns. Poisoning suspected. No household entered."

"She was unconscious."

"I was awake for enough of it to wish otherwise," Nereth said.

The clerk looked from the entry to the scars. "And the second woman?"

"She stayed with the patient," Nyana said. "Then they both worked."

"Work doesn't add names to a ship after it's left port."

The soldier came back up from the hold. "Cargo matches the manifest. Grain seals are intact."

"Good. Tell the harbourmaster to hold the unloading permit."

Neldor's voice stayed level. "For how long?"

"Until I know who came into Meren on your ship."

On the quay, men had started fastening a lifting frame over the aft hold. They stopped when the soldier raised a hand. Beyond them another ship was waiting for the berth, its captain already shouting at the watchtower.

Neldor let out a long breath through his nose. "They've got names."

"Then I expect this will be brief."

The clerk opened his ledger to a clean page and started with Nereth.

"Name?"

"Nereth."

"Household or family?"

"I don't have one any more."

Nyana pressed her lips together. Neldor studied the rigging overhead as if it needed urgent prayer.

The clerk dipped his pen in the little inkpot fixed inside the ledger's cover. "Place of residence?"

"Natharul, until a week ago."

"Occupation?"

Nereth hesitated. "Lady's attendant."

The pen stopped above the page.

Seralune saw the mistake before the clerk looked round at her.

"To whom?"

"I'm no longer in her service," Nereth said.

He wrote without showing whether he believed it, and turned to Seralune. "Your name?"

"Sera."

"Household or family?"

"None I can give you."

"That isn't the same as none."

"It is the answer."

It came out the way it would have come out in the palace, flat and final, a door closing. His pen stopped again.

The harbour went on round them. Rope scraped on stone. At the next berth someone dropped a wooden crate and got a burst of abuse from inside a warehouse for it. Seralune became aware of how much attention one quiet official could gather without ever raising his voice.

"You talk like somebody who's used to questions ending when you say so," he said.

She'd spoken to courtiers and physicians and tutors in exactly that voice since she was a child. In the palace, her rank had been enough to end a question.

Here it had given him a reason to ask another one.

*Count. One breath first.*

"I was," she said. "I spoke badly. Ask what you need."

He set the pen back on the page. "Start with Waluna."

"The southern slope broke open. Nereth was hurt, and we got down to the harbour during the alarm."

"And the medic brought you aboard?"

Nyana answered first. "I treated her on the quay. Her fever was climbing, and the medicine I needed was on this ship."

"And the captain agreed to take you both?"

"He did," Seralune said. "We worked the whole crossing."

"Where are you going?"

"Inland."

"There's a lot of Mydea that counts as inland."

"We're looking for family."

She felt the queen's signet under her shirt, held against her skin on its fine chain.

"Do you know where they live?"

"No. I was told to start near the pilgrim roads."

Family didn't have to mean living. Her mother might be dead. The person Cyrandor had promised her in Favale might have stopped waiting centuries ago. She couldn't give the clerk an address because she didn't have one.

"No travel writ," he said. "No passage record. Have you got anything that proves what you've told me?"

The signet seemed to grow heavier.

She could put it in his hand. If he knew the queen's seal, the questions might end there. It might also give Meren her real name before she'd walked off the pier.

She left it where it was.

"Only Nyana's treatment book, the work we did, and the people who watched us do it."

The soldier shifted by the gangplank. Her attention had drifted from Seralune to the sealed hold.

"We could question the crew separately," the clerk said.

"Question them," said Neldor. "Every one of them watched Sera lose a fight with a bucket, and Nereth spent half the crossing correcting my labels. They worked."

Nyana shut the treatment book. "The labels needed correcting."

"That isn't helping."

The soldier turned a laugh into a cough before the clerk could look round.

He asked Seralune, "Do you claim any part of the cargo?"

"No."

"Any employment with the captain or his crew?"

"No. Our passage ends here."

"And the packs? Yours, or the captain's stores?"

"We're leaving the ship at Meren," Seralune said again. "The packs are ours. We claim nothing else."

He went back to writing.

The soldier held up the manifest and tapped one line with her thumb. "Another hold for the northern camp."

"That's the contract," Neldor said.

"They're buying grain by the cup in Dalen."

"I know what the cargo is. I don't choose which stores the quartermaster fills."

"No. You only keep them full."

"Gurdred," the clerk said.

Gurdred handed the manifest back. "Destination matches."

She stepped down off the gangplank. The resentment stayed in the way she looked at the sealed boards, but she didn't aim it at the sailors standing by them.

Seralune had grown up hearing that humans took whatever elven settlements couldn't defend. Now a human town was measuring its grain by the cup while Natharul's soldiers were sent holds full of it. Neldor couldn't break the contract without losing his ship. Gurdred couldn't open the hold without breaking her orders. The grain would leave Meren without ever reaching Dalen, and nobody standing on this deck could stop it.

The clerk blotted the wet ink and read the entry back once, half to himself, the way a man checks a sum.

"Sera. Black hair, very fair, green eyes. Speech of a great household. Nereth. Burn scarring, left side of the face, eye to temple. Taken aboard at Waluna during the southern alarm. Passage worked. Disembarking at Meren for inland travel."

He closed the ledger.

Seralune had watched court records outlive the people who signed them. This one had her eyes in it now. It had the way she talked. If anyone ever came to Meren asking after two women carried out of Waluna, the harbour would be able to tell them exactly who to look for.

The clerk signed the bottom of Neldor's manifest. "You can unload after the military quartermaster's inspected the outer seals. These two can go when the gangway opens."

Neldor took the page. "How long till the quartermaster gets here?"

"Long enough for the next captain to blame you for the berth."

The clerk and the soldier crossed onto the quay. The other ship's captain had started shouting again before their boots reached the end of the pier.

Neldor watched them into the harbour office, then turned round.

"You nearly bought us half a day tied up here."

"I know."

"You got it back."

Nereth shifted the strap on her shoulder. "Don't encourage her. We've still got a whole country to cross."

Seralune lifted the heavier pack. "I'll try to be less memorable."

"Start by leaving before they ask you to show them that hauling knot."

Nyana hit her brother on the arm with the treatment book.

---

The quartermaster arrived before noon.

Cargo came out of the hold in nets and wooden slings, and each load got a chalk mark from him before it went onto the line of military wagons waiting on the quay.

Seralune and Nereth stayed aboard until the lifting frame swung aside and the gangplank opened to foot traffic.

Nyana checked the medicine one last time at the top of it. She lifted the stoppered jar out of Nereth's pack and held it up to the sun. Dark liquid moved under the wax round its neck.

"Five drops in water when the pain won't let you sleep," she said. "No more than twice a day. If the marks go hot, the jar won't help you. You find a healer."

"Five drops," Nereth said.

"Count them into the cup before you add the water. Lose count, start again."

"I can count to five, Nyana."

"You tried to tell me walking counted as rest."

Nereth put the jar back. "Your bottles are labelled now, by the way."

"They were labelled before."

"Numbers aren't labels if nobody else knows what they mean."

Nyana pulled her into a hug before she could say anything else. For a heartbeat Nereth's arms hung uncertain at her sides. Then they came up round the medic's back.

"The moment the scars change," Nyana said into her scarf, "you find somebody better than me."

"That might be difficult."

"Promise me you'll look."

Nereth's answer was lost against Nyana's shoulder. "Thank you."

Nyana held on a little longer before she let go.

Neldor waited at the top of the gangplank. Seralune spread the sailcloth map across a closed crate, and he put his finger on the first mark past Meren.

"The inland gate opens onto the old coast road. The broken-bell shrine marks the fork. If you can't reach it before dark, stop at the carters' shelter here." He touched a square drawn well short of the shrine. "If the light's going when you get there, stay put. That shrine isn't worth finding in the dark."

Seralune glanced at Nereth. "We'll stop when she says we stop."

"Good. Listen to her."

"I've been telling her that for seven days," said Nereth.

Neldor rolled the map and gave it back. At the aft hold, the quartermaster had started arguing about the count of oil jars, and sailors were calling for their captain.

Seralune had rehearsed several ways to thank him. All of them sounded too small next to a sea crossed and a country reached.

"You didn't have to bring us here," she said.

"Nyana made that very clear. Right up until refusing got to be more work than sailing."

His sister pushed past him and started down the gangplank with Nereth.

Neldor waited until they were out of earshot.

"You nearly told that clerk who you were without giving him the name."

"I know."

"Do that in Favale and somebody will notice." He looked towards the harbour office. "Your names are in his book now, next to my ship's. If anyone comes asking, I'll tell them what's written there. It's all I know."

Seralune touched the pack strap across her shoulder instead of the signet under it. "Do you want to know my real name?"

"No." It came easily. "I want to finish unloading before the tide turns. That's already more than I wanted to be responsible for this morning."

He looked down at Nyana, who'd reached the quay and was fussing with Nereth's scarf again.

"Get to the pilgrim road before my sister decides to follow you."

"Will you be safe going back?"

Neldor looked at the military seals, at the harbour office, at the sailors waiting for him.

"We've got the contract and the right seals. That's as much as I can promise before the crossing home. Go."

Seralune went down the gangplank.

The quay didn't move under her.

After seven days at sea, still stone felt untrustworthy. Her body kept waiting for the ground to rise and got no answer. Nereth took three careful steps, stopped, and looked down at her own boots.

"I don't like this."

"The land?"

"Its confidence."

Seralune laughed before she could stop it.

Nyana kissed Nereth's forehead, told Seralune to obey every instruction she'd ever given, and went back up to the ship while she still could without changing her mind. Neldor had already vanished into the argument by the hold.

Seralune shouldered the food pack and turned to face the streets climbing away from the harbour.

---

Meren rose from its harbour in three broad levels.

The lowest belonged to warehouses, rope-walks and fish stalls. The second held inns and workshops and narrow houses whose upper floors leaned out over the street. Above them ran the inland wall, its pale towers flying white banners with the gold sun on them.

Seralune and Nereth followed the military wagons uphill.

Their scarves drew fewer looks than Seralune had feared. Sailors wore cloth over their hair, and half the women under the fish awnings were wrapped against the sun. Nereth's scars drew more. Some people stared. Others saw them and found work somewhere else.

The street narrowed beside a warehouse painted with faded blue waves. A wagon blocked half of it while porters loaded sacks under the eye of two soldiers.

A man hung from the hoist beam above the warehouse doors.

He'd been there a while. His feet were bare and dark at the soles, and they turned a little whenever the wind came up the street. Someone had hung a board round his neck on a length of cord. It said MILITARY STORES in plain black letters, the same hand that had marked the sacks.

The porters worked underneath him. None of them looked up.

"Those are for the northern camp," one of the soldiers called. "Keep the marked grain separate."

A porter dropped his sack on the wagon harder than he needed to. "My street was told there wasn't any marked grain left."

"Take it up with the harbour office."

"They told us to take it to the quartermaster."

The soldier glanced at the line waiting behind the wagon. He sounded tired of the road, the porter and the order all at once.

"You know what they do for an open sack," he said. "Put it on the cart."

The porter's eyes went up to the beam, and came down again.

He put it on the cart.

Seralune had stopped walking. Nereth's hand found her elbow and turned her uphill, gently, the way you'd move someone away from a cliff edge without letting them see you were afraid of it.

They didn't speak until the wagon was well behind them.

"Is that what the palace meant by contracted support?" Seralune asked.

Nereth kept her voice under the market noise. "I thought it meant coin and supplies. Nobody ever said anything about towns measuring grain by the cup."

"I never asked."

"Neither did I," Nereth said.

At the next crossing, a small sun shrine filled the corner between a bakery and a cooper's yard. There was no statue. A polished gold disc caught the light above a stone basin of clear water, and people touched two fingers to the water and then to their foreheads before they went on.

A woman with bread under her arm guided her daughter's hand through it. The girl slapped the water too hard and soaked both her sleeves. Her mother scolded her, dried them with the corner of her apron, and gave her the smaller loaf to carry.

Seralune watched them go.

"You've never seen a sun basin," Nereth said.

"Only drawings in a tutor's book. The people in them were all kneeling."

"Drawings like people who keep still."

Nyana had said the pilgrims walked all the way to Favale to lie in a living saint's shadow. Looking at the basin, Seralune could almost believe it. She couldn't imagine anyone in Natharul walking a week to lie in anyone's shadow.

The road climbed again. Nereth's steps shortened before she'd admit the pain was back. Seralune steered them under an awning where baskets were stacked by a closed dye shop, and put the packs down.

A woman came out of the house next door with a pail, saw Nereth's face, and stopped.

She was older, with forearms stained blue to the elbow from the dye vats. She put the pail down, went back inside, and came out again with a clay cup of water.

"Here, love. Drink that. You've gone grey."

Nereth took it. "Thank you."

The woman looked at the scars without any pretence that she wasn't looking. Then she tipped her head down the hill, towards the harbour and the wagons.

"Was it them?"

Nereth drank before she answered. "It was fire."

"It always is, with that lot." The woman took the empty cup back. "My brother's girl came home from the northern camp with her hands like that. Only they never did go that colour." She squeezed Nereth's shoulder, once, roughly, the way you'd squeeze a neighbour's. "Keep your hood down past the gate. The soldiers'll only want to know where you got it."

She picked up her pail and went off down the hill.

Nereth watched her go.

"You didn't tell her," Seralune said.

"Tell her what?" Nereth pulled her hood lower. "She was kind to me. I didn't want to take it off her."

Seralune thought of white fire in a garden under the palace, and of the cold that had gone into her own palm when she'd held Nereth's wound. She didn't say anything.

"We could stay in Meren tonight," she said instead.

"You want to leave."

"The clerk wrote us in his ledger. He wrote down my eyes."

"He also let us off the ship. Leaving before we can walk properly would waste that."

"Can you make the shelter Neldor marked?"

Nereth looked uphill at the inner gate. "How far is it?"

"Short of the shrine. The map puts it a few hours past the wall."

"A few hours drawn by a man who can cross a deck without falling over buckets."

"The bucket moved."

"Of course it did."

Nereth pressed the heel of her hand below the lowest scar and worked it out where Seralune could see.

"Two more hours," she said. "Then we stop, whether the shelter's in front of us or still hiding in Neldor's imagination."

"Two hours."

"And we rest once before the gate."

Seralune sat down on the step. "We rest now."

They shared some of the dried fruit from Neldor's stores. Around them, Meren carried on without waiting to hear their decision. A cooper rolled an iron hoop across his yard. Two apprentices carried a painted sign up the street and hit the same balcony with it three times. Somewhere behind the dye shop a baby started crying, and a tired voice started singing to it.

Seralune ate slowly.

She'd expected Mydea to feel foreign because it was strange. What unsettled her was how much of it she understood. People worked and argued and prayed, worried about food, carried children through streets built long after her own world had ended. A thousand years had changed the lettering over their doors. It hadn't changed the way a mother dried a wet sleeve.

It hadn't changed what people did to a man who opened the wrong sack, either.

Nereth tied the pack shut. "If we sit here much longer, you're going to start studying the baskets."

"The weave's different from ours."

"You've started studying the baskets."

They went back to the road.

---

Near the inland wall, the soldiers thickened. Horses waited in a stone yard beside covered wagons, and notices had been nailed along a long board under the gatehouse, several stamped with the gold sun.

One said the western road would close to civilian carts at dusk. Another ordered all travellers to yield the centre of the road to military convoys. A third listed villages required to surrender horses, lamp oil and preserved food under emergency requisition.

The names meant nothing to Seralune. The amounts did.

One name near the bottom had a single line struck through it. Beside it, in a different hand, somebody had written one word.

*Refused.*

An older carter stood reading the same board while his mule pulled at an empty feed bag. He took off his cap and knocked the dust out of it against his thigh.

"Is the road to the broken-bell shrine still open?" Seralune asked.

He looked at her scarf, then at Nereth's face. "Open enough, if you're walking. The army took the main road this morning. Stay behind their dust and you'll get to the broken bell."

"Is there shelter before it?"

"Old carters' roof. South side, past the chalk pits. Half the wall came down last winter, but the well still works."

So Neldor's map hadn't lied.

Seralune looked at the struck-out name again. "What does that mean? The line through it."

The carter followed her eyes.

"Harl's Ford," he said. "Refused the elves' collectors in the spring. Said they'd nothing left to give." He put his cap back on. "The collectors came back with their own soldiers."

"And now?"

"Now there's nobody left to ask." He said it the way he'd told her about the well. "By the Four, don't stand gawping at it. They don't like that either."

Seralune made herself look away.

Her family's war was on that board. It had her father's seal somewhere above it, and her brother's sword somewhere behind it, and she couldn't say one word about it to the man in front of her without telling him what she was.

"Thank you," she said.

"If you hear wagons behind you, get off the road before they reach you. Those drivers won't stop. Keep off the centre stones."

He led his mule away towards the harbour.

At the gate, two passenger carts waited under a painted board. Seralune asked the woman taking fares what it cost to reach the shrine.

"Three silver each. Four if the soldiers close the road before we're past the chalk pits."

Seralune had no Mydean coin at all.

The signet touched her skin as she shifted the pack. Her mother's closed lily had opened doors all over Natharul. Here, showing it might buy a seat on a cart. It might equally buy an arrest, or a message sent back across the sea.

"We'll walk," she said.

The woman had already turned to the next traveller.

They went up to the inland gate on foot.

The guards checked they weren't carrying trade goods and waved them through with everyone else leaving behind the convoy. One complained about the heat. The other asked Nereth if she could see well enough to walk the road. She told him she could, and he called the next person forward without looking at her again.

Beyond the wall, the road ran between fields gone gold under the afternoon sun. Dust hung far ahead where the military wagons had passed. A line of low hills softened the horizon, and somewhere past them waited a shrine with a broken bell, the pilgrim road, and Favale.

Seralune stopped under the gate's outer arch.

From there she could see a slice of Meren's harbour between the rooftops. Neldor's ship was still at the pier, shrunk by distance to one mast among dozens. One of its sails had started to rise.

Nereth followed her gaze. "If Nyana catches us looking back, she'll decide we need another examination."

"Then we'd better go while Neldor can still stop her."

They stepped out of the wall's shadow.

The white-and-gold banner opened above them in the sea wind. Seralune settled the heavier pack, made sure her ears were still covered, and matched her pace to Nereth's without taking her arm.

Together, they started down the road to Favale.
