# Volume 1 So Far (Chapters 1–10): An Honest Review

10 October 2026. I read your Word file *The Ruler of Nothing – Chapters 1-10* from start to finish, about 46,600 words, and checked it against the Volume 1 Picture, `Decisions.md` and the outline. It matches `Manuscript/Volume 1/` word for word except in Chapter 9's vision, where the file has your newer line ("Alaric. This is who you are.") and no "*No. I didn't—*". This review is of the file's version.

This is one reader's opinion. None of it changes `Decisions.md` until you answer the questions at the end.

## The verdict

Yes, the story is good. The writing is clean, controlled and often moving. At its best, in Chapters 2, 7 and 10, it would hold its own in a published fantasy novel, and its sentences are cleaner than most web fiction manages. Gerolt, Seralune, Nereth and Marta feel like people rather than parts of a plot. And the shape of the two threads is the strongest idea in the book: Natharul hunts Alaric while Natharul's own princess runs from it, and neither knows the other exists.

It isn't great yet, and almost every reason sits between the chapters rather than inside them:

- Since Chapter 2, Alaric hasn't wanted anything on the page except to reach Marta, and in Chapter 9 he reached her.
- His side moves slowly: six chapters to travel about a day from where he woke.
- The volume's question hasn't been put to him yet. Nobody has told him he's nothing.
- The mysteries are kept mostly by people refusing to answer and by overheard conversations, and by Chapter 9 that's starting to show.

If you want a number, the chapters average about 80/100, and the volume as a whole sits a few points lower, because its weak points are in its shape, not its sentences. Put plainly: the writing is at a publishable level, and the structure isn't there yet. The good news is that every one of the structural problems can be fixed going forward, in Chapters 11–15, without reopening Chapters 1–10. The foundation is good enough that the next five chapters will decide how good this volume is.

## What works

### Gerolt

He's the best character in the volume, and he's alive for two chapters. That isn't a complaint: his death works because Chapter 1 made him a person first. He's funny under strain ("Heavy little shite, and half-starved besides"; "Calling louder is the whole of my strategy"). He's frightened without saying so. And he keeps doing small decent things, like setting his stool where he can see both the bed and the door, or "Take it with both hands, there."

Chapter 2 is the high point of the book: "Suppose that's goodbye, then."; the name arriving, and Gerolt's "There you are."; "That was the worst horse I ever had… Slow as shite."; and "Heh." The borrowing from Cid worked: the humour over the damage, and nothing else.

### Seralune's thread

Right now it's the stronger of the two. Each of her chapters has a clear situation, a discovery she puts together from evidence rather than being told, and a choice she makes herself.

- **Chapter 4.** Her voice arrives whole: "It's hardly dignified, but at least no one can see me."
- **Chapter 5.** The thousand years builds from Leorin's white hair, the doors where there used to be wall, and the tree, until "So it's been…" and she won't finish the thought. Then "You said you'd be back before evening, too."
- **Chapter 7.** She turns back for Nereth when she could have walked on. It's the clearest choice either lead makes in ten chapters.
- **Chapter 10.** She takes the lamp, picks the deeper path and frees the lizard. Each choice is brave or kind, and each makes things worse. That's her flaw (she takes control, and it ends badly) planted exactly as the plan wants, and readers can see her mana behind it before she can.

### The message that changed

Her mother said "I'll never stop looking for you"; it reaches her as "The falls do not stop, and neither will we", and she answers, "She said *I*. Not *we*." This is the most original idea in the volume. One changed word says a thousand years, and it's a small answer that hurts. More of the book should work this way.

### Chapter 10's horror

It's horror made from a person going wrong one habit at a time, which is what you asked for. The best of it:

- "I was waiting to see whether you would start again."
- Nereth holding a hand that isn't there.
- Nereth straightening the dead woman's collar, the way she tucked in the shawl that morning.
- Seralune's own voice coming back out of a dead mouth.

The dry back-and-forth before it ("Is Your Highness decorating?") is what makes the fall land.

### Wena

She's used well every time: silent under the bed, licking Gerolt's knuckles, turning back to him and then running after Alaric, refusing the river, knowing Marta's door. She carries a lot of the book's feeling without ever stopping being a dog.

### The two threads answer each other

- In Chapter 1 the Light searches him "the way a hand searches a dark room for something it already knows the shape of". In Chapter 4 her mana goes out "towards somewhere so far away" and "found something there, and took hold".
- A single white flower stands at the edge of the circle in Chapter 1; white flowers grow from her mana in Chapter 10.
- A hand closes on nothing three times: Alaric's in Chapter 1, Thaer's in Chapter 7 and Nereth's in Chapter 10.
- Doors and knocks end Chapters 1, 5 and 8, and open Chapters 2, 7 and 9.

A careful reader will connect the two leads long before the book says a word about it. That's exactly the "the reader suspects" effect the tear will need.

### Small answers, all the time

Principle 2 is working. Every chapter hands the reader something solid (Mydea, Affinity, the Faint, his name, Silas's name, the thousand years, the seal, Inrandeel, Nereth's Fire, "His wife?" / "Aye. Me.") while keeping the big things back. Readers never feel they've been given nothing.

### The prose

It's plain, specific and strict about point of view. The comparisons are rare and earned, and people talk like people. Three lines show it best:

- Silas: "And you had his sword in your hands, but you stayed where you were and did nothing."
- Marta: "Tell me what happened. Don't just tell me he's dead."
- The neighbour at the fence: "Told the old fool he'd smoke us out one day."

## What holds it back

Some of these come from choices you made on purpose: the slow, lived-in pace, holding back the big answers, and Alaric failing to act at Gerolt's death. The choices are sound. What follows is what they're costing.

### 1. Alaric has nothing driving him yet

He wants to learn who he was; that's his want for the whole volume. The last time he did anything about it was Chapter 2. Since then, everything he's done has been staying alive or following Gerolt's instruction: run, cross the river, walk to Kelmend, find Marta. While he was grieving and hunted, that was right, all the way through Chapter 8. But Chapter 9 got him to Marta. So at the end of Chapter 10, readers know what Seralune will try next (help for Nereth, then her mother's trail), and they don't know what Alaric will try next.

He's likable, and he has a voice ("a headache that's at least probably my own"; "*Nearly.*"). But he's mostly been led, first by Gerolt, then by Silas, then by Marta and Rhose. And he's been rescued every time it mattered: Silas saves him from the riders, from the river and from the ogre. The plan calls him smart, and he hasn't yet solved a problem with his head.

Two things on the page already point the right way:

- In Chapter 6 he goes off alone on a wrong belief ("Whatever broke those trees was hours back") and nearly dies for it. That's a small, good version of how the tear will happen.
- The double in Chapter 9 is the first real push towards his past.

Chapter 11 has to turn that push into something he wants and does.

### 2. His side moves slowly

The ten chapters cover three days. Alaric's six (about 27,100 words) take him from the field to an inn about a day's travel away. Seralune's four (about 18,600 words) get her out of the palace and under a mountain, and they feel faster because more changes in each one.

Your rough outline of 29 September put "Empty" as a verdict in Chapter 8, and Redd and Freya in Chapter 9. Both have slipped past Chapter 10, because the chapters grew longer rather than fuller. Chapter 8 shows it most: 5,300 words, most of them waiting (in the loading yard, then outside the inn), around two strong scenes, the auction and the slammed door. The extra content you asked for on 29 September has partly come as extra length.

The heart of the volume is the group becoming the place he belongs. A quarter of the way in, only half of it is here: Silas and Wena, but not Redd or Freya.

### 3. The volume's question hasn't reached him

"What is your worth when everyone tells you that you're nothing?" In ten chapters, everyone Alaric meets is kind to him: Gerolt, Silas in his way, the woman with the trays ("Thanks, lad."), Marta, Rhose. The only person who has called him nothing is Alaric himself ("Empty?"), in private, in Chapter 1. The world's cruelty is shown to someone else, at the auction (the best scene of its kind in the book), and he walks away from it.

For a setup, that's fine. But the title promises a man the world calls nothing, and readers will be waiting for the world to say it to his face. The planned "Empty" scene in Chapter 11 is where the title starts to pay off, so it should hit hard and cost him something.

### 4. Holding back has become the default

Thaer turns aside Seralune's questions at least five times in Chapter 5. Silas dodges Alaric's through most of Chapters 3, 6 and 8. Marta closes the subject of her marriage with "Leave it at that, Alaric." And five times, the news arrives because someone overhears it:

- Leorin and Thaer through the door (Chapter 5);
- the couple at the fence (Chapter 6);
- the guards in the loading yard (Chapter 8);
- the crowd at the gate (Chapter 8);
- the guards' questions, heard from Rhose's room (Chapter 9).

This comes from two good rules working together: stay in one pair of eyes, and hold back the big answers. If nobody will tell the viewpoint character anything, the only way left is to overhear it. In Chapter 5 the refusal is the point ("Why can't anyone just say what is going on?"), and it works. By Chapter 9 it's a habit.

Chapter 7 shows the way out: Cyrandor answers her, and the answers hurt more than the silence did. The next chapters will be stronger if each gives one real answer that costs someone something, and uses overheard talk only when nothing else will do.

### 5. Familiar pieces

Taken one at a time, a lot of the building blocks are familiar:

- the amnesiac waking on a battlefield;
- the gruff old mentor who dies in Chapter 2;
- the swordsman with a dark past;
- the princess sealed for a thousand years;
- the dream that turns out to be a dream;
- the dark double who says "This is who you are."

You write every one of them well. But it means that what makes this book different is the new things in it: a battle nobody heard; a message worn down by a thousand years of retelling; a princess whose own leaking power is the monster in her horror chapter; and two leads on opposite sides who don't know they're bound together. Lean on those, and lead with them when you describe the book to readers.

The double will carry a lot of the series. Its first line was the right size for a first appearance. Next time, it should say something only this double could say.

### 6. Two misjudged moments in Chapter 9

- **Rhose's room.** In one evening Alaric has watched a naked woman sold on a chain, told Marta her uncle is dead and hidden from the guards. Then the chapter gives a page to Rhose's neckline, his blushes and her offer ("I told him I was finished for tonight. I didn't say anything about you."). Coming straight after the auction, and after Marta's looks have been remarked on twice, it reads as the book enjoying the women's bodies at the moment it should be grieving. The women's freedom to choose, which `Decisions.md` sets out carefully, isn't on the page yet, so readers can't see whatever contrast with the auction you intend. What makes Rhose's death in the dream hurt is her kindness: giving up her room, holding his ankle, moving the candle closer. That's all Chapter 9 needs from her.
- **The false awakening.** The images are strong, and it brings in the double. But "it was only a dream" at the end of a chapter called "Refuge" is a move readers know, and some will feel tricked. It earns its place if the next chapter makes the dream matter: the headache stays, he can't bring himself to tell anyone, and he sees Rhose and Marta differently.

### 7. Chapter 10's length and scale

- **Length.** At 8,700 words it's a fifth of the volume, and it mostly earns that: the circling takes about 2,700 words, and its best beats (the sea, the mark, "You stopped breathing") pay for it. What could be tighter is the walking between the beats. That's where "a long time" or "for a while" marks time, nine times in the narration, because the no-counting rule has pushed time into vague phrases. The chapter already has the better way: "The lamp had been half full when she sat down, and it wasn't now." Mark time by what she can see and feel (the oil, the blisters, her thirst), not by "a long time".
- **Scale.** Nereth can't hold a flame "for hours", yet she opens the side of a mountain. Gerolt's last stand, the biggest Fire the reader had seen before this, burned two riders. Careful readers have enough to guess why (the flowers bend towards Nereth's hand, and Seralune's mana rises every time); others will decide the magic has no rules. You've already noted that later chapters must respect the scale. They also need to show the world noticing, and the bell is a good start.

## What I'd do next

Recommendations, for your yes or no. Answer by number and I'll put what you decide into `Decisions.md`.

2. **By the end of Chapter 11, Alaric wants something and acts on it.** The double gives the push.
