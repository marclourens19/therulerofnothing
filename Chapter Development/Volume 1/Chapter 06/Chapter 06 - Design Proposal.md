# The Ruler of Nothing — Next Alaric Chapter

## Detailed design proposal

Prepared 30 September 2026.

**Status:** A proposed chapter design for discussion. It preserves the author's five principal beats: waking and leaving the cave; looking back towards Gerolt; questioning Silas; separating and encountering an ogre; approaching Kelmend beneath Natharul's royal airship. New dialogue, route details, ogre behaviour and scene arrangements below are recommendations, not approved additions to canon. No manuscript chapters have been changed.

**Numbering:** In the current reading order, Chapter 5 is Seralune's *The Shape of Absence*. This proposal is therefore for Chapter 6, the next Alaric chapter. Its title remains open.

## 1. My recommended chapter

I would write a substantial journey chapter in which Alaric and Silas discover how difficult it is to travel together. The danger grows out of that difficulty, and surviving it leaves them with a different arrangement.

At the beginning, Alaric follows because he has no workable alternative. Silas supplies instructions but gives him little reason to understand or accept them. Alaric wants to reach Marta and honour the last direction Gerolt gave him. He also wants to know why this violent stranger has decided to accompany him.

The questions become personal. Silas's refusal to explain himself makes Alaric wonder how much choice he will have once they reach the town. I recommend a concrete disagreement: Silas intends to take Gerolt's sword to Marta first while Alaric waits outside. To Alaric, the one person Gerolt told him to find, and the object he told him to show her, are now being placed beyond another stranger's permission. He leaves with a plan that seems sufficient to him and is dangerously incomplete.

The ogre encounter exposes the limits of that plan. It also gives Alaric a chance to act while afraid. Silas rescues him, but Alaric contributes to his own survival and protects Wena rather than becoming an inert object in another man's fight.

Afterwards, their anger has to be spoken through. Alaric can accept that he made a dangerous choice without accepting that asking questions was wrong. Silas can remain abrasive while recognising, through a practical concession, that withholding every useful explanation helped produce the separation.

They approach Kelmend with the beginnings of cooperation. Then the airship makes the danger much larger than either of them expected.

**The emotional movement:** Alaric goes from following an unexplained decision to choosing a limited agreement with Silas. Silas goes from moving a boy out of danger to beginning to deal with that boy as someone who can answer back, help, and make choices.

This is an early relationship change. It does not make them friends, settle Silas's history or cure Alaric's grief.

## 2. What the existing chapters require

These details should govern the draft:

- Silas has already saved Alaric and Wena from the river. A second rescue needs a different outcome for their relationship.
- Alaric has seen Silas kill an elf with deliberate brutality. He has reasons to be uneasy around him despite accepting his help.
- Silas has given his name. He has not asked Alaric's.
- Silas already says, “We'll need another way in.” His intention to accompany Alaric has been hinted at before this chapter begins.
- Alaric knows that Kelmend's guards were talking to a Natharul rider at the bridge. Another identical patrol would add little unless it changes the route or reveals something new.
- Gerolt directed Alaric to Marta at the inn by the south gate: “Show her that. Tell her the old fool sent you.” The sword is the proof he is meant to show her. This is the one destination Alaric has received from someone he trusts.
- Silas reacts noticeably to Marta's name. His history with her and Gerolt remains unrevealed.
- Silas hurt his hand while heating his blade. He remains an experienced Eminent Fire user; the injury should not turn him into an incompetent novice.
- Alaric has not told Silas that Gerolt found no readable 『Affinity』 in him. The chapter should not casually assume Silas knows this.
- Gerolt's last stand occurred away from the burning cabin. Smoke indicates the direction of the farm, not the exact location of his body.
- Chapter 3 reaches first light before Silas tells Alaric to sleep.
- Seralune sees an enormous dark, plated ship with blue-green light along its underside departing at night in Chapter 5. Her belief that her father is aboard is still a belief within her viewpoint.

The current handoff and chapter text take priority over superseded material in the old chapters. In particular, the sword remains Gerolt's gift; the old split-shield token should not reappear.

## 3. Length and distribution

I would plan a first draft of approximately **5,500–6,500 words**, with about 6,000 as a working centre. This is a recommendation for the amount of dramatic material below, not a requirement to reach a number.

| Section | Working allowance | What earns the space |
|---|---:|---|
| Waking and preparing | 450 | Physical condition, reluctant care, the unresolved decision to travel together |
| Leaving and looking back | 600 | Gerolt's absence becomes part of a visible morning landscape |
| The road and the questions | 1,200 | Ordinary life, practical information, conversational escalation |
| The disagreement and separation | 650 | A credible motive, an inadequate plan, a choice Alaric owns |
| The ogre | 1,100 | Discovery, pursuit, helplessness, action, rescue |
| The aftermath | 1,000 | Anger, practical care, one concession from each person |
| Kelmend coming into view | 450 | The destination becomes tangible and briefly offers relief |
| The airship | 600 | Scale, recognition, political consequence, a changed immediate problem |
| **Total** | **6,050** | |

The road and aftermath receive nearly as much space together as the attack and escape. That is intentional: the chapter's lasting value should come from the people travelling through it.

If the draft naturally finishes at 5,200 words, keep it there. If the road conversation and aftermath remain active at 6,800, let them breathe. Additional words should introduce a new observation, disagreement, concession or consequence. Repeating Gerolt's death or Silas's refusal will not make the chapter deeper.

## 4. Suggested timing and geography

I recommend leaving **later in the morning**, after several hours of imperfect sleep. This preserves Chapter 3's current ending. Keeping the departure at dawn would require changing that ending or deliberately showing that Silas has woken Alaric almost immediately.

| Time | Proposed event |
|---|---|
| First light | Chapter 3 ends; Alaric finally sleeps |
| Later morning | Silas wakes him, they eat a little and leave |
| Late morning | Smoke, the first stretch of forest and practical questions |
| Early afternoon | The personal questions become an argument; Silas scouts an alternative approach |
| Afternoon | Alaric leaves, encounters the ogre and is rescued |
| Later afternoon | Recovery, a difficult conversation and renewed travel |
| Dusk | Kelmend's lights appear; the airship passes overhead |

The final draft needs a simple route that the reader can hold in mind: the cave; a visible direction back towards the farm; the forest route towards Kelmend; the place where Silas turns aside to find a safer approach; and the rise from which the town becomes visible.

Alaric's separation should be short. He cannot walk for hours into a different forest and still be within convenient hearing distance. Keep him within several minutes of Silas's scouting area, with the exact distance determined by terrain and sound. Wena's barking first alerts Silas; the ogre's noise and disturbed undergrowth make the direction easier to find.

If the ship is the same one Seralune saw, its journey must fit the available overnight-to-dusk travel window. The world map and established ship speed should settle that. The design does not establish a new flight speed, compass direction or border distance.

## 5. Scene-by-scene design

### Scene 1 — Silas wakes him

**Immediate question:** Why is Silas still here, and why does he appear to have decided what Alaric will do next?

Open with a physical interruption rather than a recap. Silas nudges Alaric's boot or leg and tells him they need to move. Alaric wakes badly: his arm is numb beneath Wena, the cave has become bright around its entrance, and his body remembers the river before his thoughts recover the night.

For a moment, he is simply trying to sit up. Then he remembers Gerolt. Give that recognition room without replaying the whole death scene.

Silas is already preparing to leave. He has put out the fire, collected what he needs and dealt with his hand roughly. The boy notices preparations through their immediate effects. The narration does not announce that Silas has stayed awake to protect him unless something Alaric observes establishes it.

Silas offers food again. This time he is less willing to let Alaric refuse because they have a day of walking ahead. Alaric eats enough to move, perhaps irritated by Silas treating him as another thing that must be packed and carried.

Let there be one small, unceremonious exchange about Wena or the food. It gives them something to talk about that is not a mystery. Silas's care is imperfect and irritable; it should not become a performance of Gerolt's warmth.

The question of travelling together follows naturally. Alaric remembers “We'll need another way in,” but daylight has made that promise more real. Silas knows the route; his personal reason for taking it remains concealed.

**Important practical detail:** Establish how Alaric carries Gerolt's sword. Chapter 3 leaves it in his hand. Do not suddenly provide an unexplained scabbard. A makeshift carrying arrangement supplied here is a proposed ordinary addition; alternatively, keep the blade carried and let its weight become part of the journey. Whichever choice the draft uses must hold during the ogre encounter.

**Scene outcome:** Alaric is fed, awake and moving. He has accepted the journey for now, but has not received the explanation he wanted.

### Scene 2 — The smoke behind them

**Immediate question:** Can Alaric leave the direction of Gerolt's farm without feeling that he is abandoning him again?

Outside, the world is clear enough to distinguish things the night concealed: the bank, the forest, the distant smoke. Let the morning remain ordinary. Birds, work in the distance or sunlight on wet leaves can exist without becoming poetic symbols of indifference.

Alaric stops when he identifies the smoke. His reaction should attach to one concrete absence: Gerolt will not be sitting by the hearth, or there is no cabin left for Wena to return to. Choose one. Do not stack several memories simply because each is sad.

Wena lingers, turns back or scents the air. Describe her behaviour. Alaric can interpret it as wanting Gerolt, but the narration cannot enter the dog's memories.

Silas continues for a few strides, then checks where they are. I would change Claude's recommendation that he never looks back. A man who is trying to move a hunted, exhausted boy and a dog safely through the forest should keep track of them. That can be irritating rather than tender: Alaric catches him waiting and feels hurried.

Silas does not need to give a speech about burial. Nor should he dismiss Gerolt's death with a clever line. A restrained prompt to move is sufficient.

Alaric finally turns away because he remembers the destination Gerolt gave him. Following that instruction is something he can still do.

**What changes:** In the cave, grief paralysed him. Here, it supplies a direction. This is fragile and incomplete, but it is movement.

**Transition:** He follows several paces behind Silas. The distance reflects discomfort and hesitation; it should not become so great that Silas inexplicably allows him to disappear immediately.

### Scene 3 — The road, ordinary life and questions

**Immediate question:** What kind of man is leading him, and how much of this world will that man let him understand?

Begin with practical conversation. Alaric asks how far Kelmend is, why they are avoiding the main road, or what Silas is watching for. Silas answers these questions more freely than questions about himself. He can explain a useful point in two or three connected sentences; he does not have to speak only in commands.

Let the conversation take place while they are doing things. Alaric has to catch up after a fallen trunk, Wena stops at a smell, or Silas waits for a clear view. These interruptions should affect what gets said rather than decorate every line with an action tag.

Give the road one brief encounter with ordinary life. My preferred proposal is a small farm or working yard seen from cover. Alaric hears people disagreeing over a task. If a small use of magic belongs naturally in that task, it can appear without anybody stopping to identify a rank. Its exact form must agree with the magic rules.

For Alaric, the significant thing is that these people are continuing their lives. They have names, familiarity and something mundane to finish. He briefly wants to approach them. Then he remembers the guards at the bridge, or notices Silas waiting for him to stay hidden, and does not.

This gives the reader something more than another threat. It also gives Alaric a small present desire: he would like to speak to someone who is not dying, pursuing him or refusing him.

His questions then become personal. The sequence I recommend is:

1. Why are you coming with me?
2. How did you know Gerolt?
3. Why did you react when I said Marta's name?
4. If you know her, why will you not tell me anything about her?

These are connected questions. Silas's answers or evasions produce the next question.

Do not repeat Chapter 3's conversation word for word. Alaric can refer to the previous deflection: Silas told him how he reached the farm, not how he knew Gerolt. That shows he remembers an exchange and is dissatisfied with it.

The early-Alphinaud quality should appear in the way he builds a case. Alaric thinks that, if he explains why the question matters, Silas ought to answer. He underestimates how threatening a personal subject can feel to someone else. He may be wrong about Silas's motives while remaining right that he needs basic information about his own journey.

Silas becomes sharper when Marta is mentioned. Alaric notices the difference. Do not explain its cause from outside his viewpoint.

**Scene outcome:** Alaric has learned several useful things about travelling, but his trust in Silas's intentions has weakened. Silas has learned that the boy will pursue a question after being dismissed.

### Scene 4 — The route changes, and Alaric leaves

**Immediate question:** Is Silas taking him to the destination Gerolt promised, or expecting him to follow somewhere else?

The departure needs a concrete trigger beyond a change of direction. My preferred addition is that Silas tells Alaric he will have to wait outside the town while Silas takes Gerolt's sword to Marta first. The sword can establish why he has come; Silas has a legitimate concern about bringing a hunted boy directly to the inn, and may have private reasons for wanting to manage that meeting. The private reasons stay outside Alaric's knowledge. He has not taken the sword yet; this is his proposed arrangement for arrival.

Alaric objects. Gerolt told him to find Marta and show her the sword himself. He is being asked to hand the next step of that instruction to someone who will not explain either of his connections. He does not need to decide that Silas is certainly a traitor. It is enough that he cannot accept Silas taking the sword ahead and deciding when, or whether, he gets to speak to her.

Silas then turns towards a safer southern approach. Alaric notices the change because Silas has given him a landmark, not because an amnesiac suddenly becomes a navigator. Silas explains that he is going round the gate but, already irritated by the disagreement, cuts short the discussion of what happens afterwards and goes ahead to scout.

He tells Alaric to wait in cover. There is no need to make him inexplicably vanish for a long time.

Alaric puts the morning's evidence together: Silas knew Gerolt, knows Marta, will not explain either connection, and now expects him to wait while taking the meeting and the sword into his own hands. The altered route increases his uncertainty. Being rescued does not tell Alaric what Silas ultimately wants from him. His mistake is treating a lone journey as a workable solution to that uncertainty.

His plan should be specific enough to understand. He intends to regain the earlier direction, stay off the main road and reach the town's southern side. He believes he can find the inn once there. What he fails to account for is the forest, his exhaustion and his lack of local knowledge.

Let him consider waiting. Leaving becomes more convincing when he recognises that it is a choice rather than acting on the first flare of annoyance.

Wena initially resists the unfamiliar direction or stops to scent the route. Alaric interprets her reluctance as something he can manage and urges her on. Do not frame the dog as already understanding that Silas is trustworthy. She is reacting to her surroundings.

**Scene outcome:** Alaric chooses a course of his own. The reader understands his reason and can also see the weaknesses in his plan.

**The distinction to preserve:** He is responsible for leaving; he is not responsible for a predator's decision to attack him. The encounter tests his judgment without becoming punishment delivered by the author.

### Scene 5 — The ogre

**Immediate question:** Can Alaric survive the consequences of being alone long enough to do anything useful?

I would make the ogre a physical, local threat. This chapter already carries Gerolt's death, Silas's hidden past and Natharul's arrival. It does not need to introduce corrupted mana, an ancient experiment and a new displacement mystery through the same creature.

**Proposed creature decision:** This individual understands simple speech and recognises Alaric as prey. It can form crude, purposeful phrases. This does not establish that all ogres behave alike, classify them as beastfolk or settle their place in the world's peoples.

Introduce it through something Alaric misreads: movement beyond a trunk, disturbed ground, or an apparent shape that stands. Use two or three observations he can actually process. A catalogue of its entire anatomy will slow the discovery.

Wena recognises danger first. Her barking alerts the ogre, as in the author's shape, and also alerts Silas. It is a warning under pressure, not foolishness for which the dog should be blamed.

Alaric runs. His fatigue and the difficult ground matter. The chase should not cover enough distance to make Silas's arrival implausible.

The ogre catches his ankle and lifts him. Be exact about the position: which foot is held, where his head ends up, where the sword is, and what he can reach. A boy hanging by an ankle cannot necessarily reach the hand holding his foot. Do not use the suggested wrist cut unless the actual arrangement makes it possible.

I recommend this action sequence:

1. **Capture:** Alaric is lifted before he can organise himself. The change of orientation and the pressure on his ankle make his first response confused and physical.
2. **Attempt:** If the sword remains in his hand, he swings at what he can reach near the ogre's face. The blow is clumsy. Even a shallow cut or a threatened eye makes it flinch and interrupts its attempt to bite him. He has bought time through action, without discovering lost skill.
3. **Intervention:** Silas arrives from the direction already established. He attacks the creature's support or gripping arm to free Alaric. Alaric falls badly enough to be winded, not so badly that the rest of the chapter must ignore a disabling injury.
4. **Continuing danger:** The ogre turns on Silas. The fight has difficult footing and limited room. Silas has to account for where Alaric and Wena have fallen.
5. **Alaric's second choice:** Rather than remaining where he fell, he gets Wena clear. If the ogre reaches for her, he strikes at its reaching arm despite being frightened. His contribution is immediate protection and interference, not a sophisticated manoeuvre.
6. **Finish:** Silas uses the opening to kill it decisively. Any use of Fire is brief and purposeful. His existing injury makes handling the weapon unpleasant; it need not make him nearly lose a routine fight.

The final choreography may combine the two Alaric actions if both feel excessive. The essential progression is that he is afraid, acts without expertise, accepts urgent help and then does something useful after being freed.

Silas should look experienced. Being Eminent does not make every problem easy, but it also does not require every encounter to give him a new serious wound. The difficulty can come from freeing someone, choosing a line of attack and keeping the creature away from the dog.

I would leave Wena frightened and muddy rather than badly injured. The threat of losing her is enough here. A brief examination afterwards can show both Alaric's attachment and Silas's practical concern.

**Scene outcome:** Alaric survives through a mixture of his own imperfect action and Silas's intervention. Silas has seen him move towards danger for Wena, rather than merely freeze. Neither man's earlier account of the other is now complete.

### Scene 6 — The aftermath

**Immediate question:** Can they travel together after this without pretending that nothing happened?

This is the chapter's central relationship scene. Allow the physical recovery to begin before anyone delivers a finished argument. Alaric checks Wena, struggles to breathe normally and retrieves his sword. Silas checks the surroundings before turning on him.

His first reaction can be angry. It may contain fear, but Alaric cannot know that with certainty. Show what Silas actually does: looks for injuries while swearing, handles Alaric's ankle more carefully than his voice suggests, or checks Wena despite calling her a mutt.

Alaric initially apologises for leaving or tries to defend himself. He does not need to apologise for wanting information. Eventually he states the problem in ordinary language: Gerolt told him to find Marta and show her the sword, Silas kept refusing questions about her, and now he was supposed to sit outside while Silas took it to her alone.

Silas counters that he told him to wait. That is true. Alaric counters that an order did not explain the destination. That is also true.

Let neither character produce the report's mature interpretation. They are sore, angry and frightened. Alaric can stumble over his explanation; Silas can interrupt it and then discover he needs to answer the part he avoided.

The useful concession is practical. Silas tells him what route he was checking, why he changed direction and how that route leads towards the south side of Kelmend. He then explains the safety concern about approaching the inn and agrees that, if the approach is clear, Alaric will come with him and show Marta the sword himself. Alaric agrees to wait if there is a specific danger to check. They have made an arrangement with conditions both understand. Silas does not need to say, “I should have explained.”

Alaric then helps with the burned hand or the equipment. He can offer to hold something, pass cleaner cloth or carry a load. Do not give him unexplained medical expertise. Nor should care magically stop Silas being unpleasant.

This is a good place for a first exchange of Alaric's name. My preference is that Alaric supplies it himself: he has noticed that Silas never asked. The observation can come out with hurt and irritation rather than as an accusation that the narration proves Silas deserves.

Silas's response should be ordinary. No recognition, no cryptic pause suggesting he remembers ancient Alaric, and no reverent repetition. He can use the name naturally later. That small change will mean more than a speech about seeing the boy as a person.

Give one limited answer about Gerolt if it belongs here. For example, Silas can acknowledge knowing him without revealing the apprenticeship or Marta's history. The exact amount should preserve the intended later reveal. If he gives no personal answer, his clearer practical explanation must still make this conversation different from the previous ones.

**Avoid the proposed Gerolt reprimand.** “Gerolt told you to run and you stood there…” weaponises the boy's grief. If Silas says it, the scene becomes about cruelty and its consequences. My recommended version does not use it as a witty correction.

**Scene outcome:** Alaric agrees to remain with Silas on an explained route. Silas agrees, through what he says and does, to provide enough information for that cooperation. The tension remains, but it is no longer exactly the same tension.

### Scene 7 — Kelmend becomes a place

**Immediate question:** Has Gerolt's direction finally brought Alaric somewhere he can go?

Resume walking with a visible difference. Alaric stays within speaking distance. Silas occasionally tells him what they are approaching without waiting to be interrogated. The conversation is less defensive, though it can still contain irritation.

Show the consequences of the ogre without repeatedly itemising them: Alaric is slower on one foot; Silas notices and adjusts the pace; Wena startles at a sound and settles when they keep moving. If injuries are worse than this, the distance and arrival time must change accordingly.

Kelmend appears gradually through the landscape. The first lights are attached to houses, walls or a gate. The place has noise, work and people. Give Alaric one specific thought about arriving: finding Marta, water, a place to sit, or the possibility that somebody will finally answer a question. Choose the thought that has been prepared.

If the Faint quarter is visible, show dwellings outside or against the wall without explaining their entire legal status. Its proper introduction and the meeting with Redd and Freya belong to the subsequent chapter work.

Marta's inn remains a destination, not a guarantee of welcome. Silas can indicate which side they need while being noticeably less talkative as the town becomes real.

**Scene outcome:** Arrival becomes credible and briefly desirable. The reader should share that small relief before the sky interrupts it.

### Scene 8 — The airship

**Immediate question:** What has arrived above them, and what does its presence mean for entering Kelmend?

Begin with a sensation that interrupts what they are already doing: a low hum through the ground or air, a change in the light, or Silas stopping mid-instruction. Select the effect that fits the ship as already described.

Alaric looks up and cannot immediately assemble its shape. The town gives it scale. Something he first mistakes for part of the sky resolves into a constructed object with dark plating and blue-green light beneath it.

Retain the visual continuity with Chapter 5. The referenced image's precise colour, spines and escort arrangement have not been independently established in this report; those remain author decisions. One or two distinctive features are enough to connect the two sightings.

Give Alaric a moment of wonder. He does not have to think only about being hunted. This is something extraordinary in a world he is still discovering. Then Silas identifies Natharul, using a recognisable feature or knowledge the draft makes credible, and the wonder changes.

Silas should be shocked for a specific reason. My recommendation is the unexpected presence of such a significant Natharul vessel above this approach to Kelmend, following the scouts' actions the previous night. Do not claim he knows the king is aboard unless the text establishes a basis for that knowledge.

Alaric associates the name with Gerolt's warning and the riders. He may fear the ship has come for him. The narration must leave room for him to be wrong.

Kelmend can respond visibly: movement on the wall, a signal or people coming outside. The exact response depends on its political relationship with Natharul. An alarm bell would establish more than a neutral observation, so it should be a deliberate choice.

For the ending, I favour a **changed immediate plan** over a portentous declaration. Silas had been explaining how they would approach the town. Now he directs Alaric and Wena back into cover or tells him to wait while he studies the vessel. This time Alaric understands why.

If the ship turns towards the farm's direction, Alaric can notice that. He cannot know its exact destination from one overhead passage, and smoke alone should not certify it.

**Scene outcome:** They have reached the edge of their destination, but entering it is no longer the same problem they expected. The relationship has advanced just enough for them to face the new uncertainty together.

## 6. Illustrative dialogue and voice

These examples test the proposed exchanges. They are original working examples, not final chapter text. The surrounding prose would supply movement, interruptions and Alaric's immediate perception. Their purpose is to demonstrate different emotional pressures, not prescribe a catchphrase for either character.

### Waking and food

“Come on, boy. You've had a few hours. Get up before I have to carry you again.”

Alaric tried to sit up. Wena had her head across his arm, and he had to ease it out from under her.

“Are we leaving now?”

“Once you've eaten.” Silas pushed the sack towards him. “And don't give me that look. There's nothing better in it.”

This gives Silas a practical objective, a reference to their shared night and irritation that can coexist with care. Alaric begins with a normal question rather than immediately speaking the chapter's emotional thesis.

### The personal question

“You knew Gerolt before yesterday.”

Silas stepped over a fallen branch. “Watch your feet.”

“I am watching them. You said you heard the fighting, but that doesn't tell me how you knew him.”

“No. It doesn't.”

“Then why won't you tell me? He sent me to Marta, and you know her too. I'm supposed to follow you all the way there without asking?”

“You're supposed to keep your voice down. We've enough people looking for you without you helping them.”

Here Silas's warning can be valid and still be a deflection. Alaric builds an argument rather than repeating “Who is Marta?” in several forms. The longer line belongs to the person trying to get an answer; the shorter line belongs to the person trying to stop the subject.

### The route disagreement

“Why are we turning here? You said Kelmend was over that rise.”

“And it is. I'm finding a way round the gate.”

“To Marta?”

“You'll wait outside while I take the sword to her. I'm not taking you straight to the inn with half the river looking for you.”

“Gerolt told me to show it to her. He told me to find her, Silas.”

“And she'll still be there when I've found out whether it's safe.” Silas glanced down the slope. “Stay here a minute. I need to see what's below us.”

Silas has a reasonable concern but has not resolved Alaric's objection. The previous evasions make the boy unwilling to trust an arrangement he cannot check. The draft should develop that resistance without pretending it proves Silas intends harm.

### After the rescue

“What in the hell did you think you were doing?”

“Trying to get to Kelmend.”

“And what did you think I was doing?”

Alaric looked up at him. “You wouldn't tell me anything about her. Then you said I had to wait outside while you took the sword in. Gerolt gave it to me. He told me what to do with it.”

“I didn't say that. I said I was checking first.”

“Then say that! Tell me what you're checking. You know her, you knew Gerolt, and I can't get a word out of you about either of them. I'm supposed to just sit there and hope you come back?”

Silas looked towards the slope they had come down. After a moment, he pointed beyond it.

“There's a way along the south wall. That's what I was looking for. If it's clear, we can reach the inn without walking up to the guards.”

“Both of us?”

“If it's clear. If there are riders outside, you wait while I find another way. Can you manage that?”

This is the concession. Silas answers the useful question. It does not require a polished apology or a declaration of trust. Alaric explains a concrete misunderstanding instead of diagnosing Silas's behaviour.

The phrase “what in the hell” is only a placeholder for Silas's established vocabulary; do not transfer Gerolt's “Last Dark” oath to him automatically. The final voice pass should choose an oath Silas actually uses or omit it.

### The name

“It's Alaric, by the way.”

Silas looked at him.

“My name. You never asked.”

“All right, Alaric. Hold this while I tie it.”

This is deliberately small. The name enters a shared task. Silas need not transform into a warmly articulate man to begin treating him differently.

### A possible final instruction

“Natharul?” Alaric looked from Silas to the lights beneath the ship. “Those riders—”

“I know.”

“Is it coming here?”

Silas watched it a moment longer. Then he moved back towards the trees.

“Alaric. Get Wena into cover.”

The end would include the ship's actual movement and the town's visible response, rather than depend on this exchange alone. Using Alaric's name follows from the earlier scene; the urgent short instruction is earned by the immediate danger.

## 7. The character design underneath the scenes

### Alaric

His kindness should remain evident without making every action wise. He tends Wena, notices Silas's injured hand and wants to honour Gerolt's direction. He also dislikes being moved through his own life without understanding what is happening.

His error is believing an incomplete explanation leaves him equipped to act independently. He can reason well about a conversation and badly about a forest. That combination gives him personality rather than reducing him to a foolish boy who needs an adult.

He should have several emotional modes across the chapter: unwillingness to wake; grief at the smoke; curiosity about ordinary life; irritation at evasion; brief relief after leaving; terror; shame mixed with defensiveness; concern for someone else; and wonder beneath the ship.

These shifts need transitions. They do not require a face twisting, a fist tightening or a breath catching every time he feels something.

### Silas

His competence should be dependable. His emotional availability should not be.

He can give a clear explanation of a route and then become evasive over a name. He can be cruel in an argument, useful in a crisis, awkward when helped and impatient with thanks. Avoid making every line a threat or a clever maxim.

The reader should see that he has chosen to remain present before Alaric proves useful or important. Food, waiting, tracking him and coming back are already evidence of that choice. Alaric's contribution to the fight changes what Silas understands about him; it does not confer the boy's moral worth.

His reaction to the airship should expose the limits of his control. He can kill a forest predator. He cannot simply cut down the political and military power passing overhead.

### Wena

Wena connects the journey to Gerolt, but she needs to remain an animal with immediate needs. Let her sniff, drink, become tired, bark, refuse a direction and settle near someone who remains with her.

Not every movement should be interpreted as a message about grief. Her ordinary behaviour can make the relationship more affecting than constant symbolic loyalty.

## 8. Worldbuilding and thematic purpose

The chapter should establish the difference between the places Alaric sees and the rules other people already understand. Silas knows which roads are watched, which gate is risky and how to approach a settlement. Alaric does not. Their conflict turns that unequal knowledge into a question of trust and agency.

Ordinary work on the road shows that the world contains lives worth wanting. Kelmend begins turning those lives into an institution: walls, entry, housing and decisions about who may pass. The airship then introduces authority on a much larger scale.

This relates to the series' concern with people claiming the right to decide other people's lives. The local form is simple: when Silas knows more, what does he owe the person following him? When Alaric wants control, what responsibility comes with acting on incomplete knowledge?

Do not state the philosophy in their dialogue. Let the reader experience a disagreement in which safety, knowledge and choice cannot be separated neatly.

Gerolt remains present through his instructions, sword and dog. His absence does not need a fresh sad comparison in every scene. Alaric's decision to keep going towards Marta is already an attempt to preserve something Gerolt gave him.

The chapter should also begin building the later companion dynamic. A little irritation, practical help and imperfect humour belongs beside the danger. These are the early experiences that can make a future group feel like people who have actually travelled together.

## 9. Mysteries: what advances and what remains concealed

| Question | Recommended movement in this chapter |
|---|---|
| Why is Silas helping? | More evidence of a deliberate choice to stay; a limited practical explanation; personal motive remains open |
| How did Silas know Gerolt? | Alaric presses the previous evasion; at most one bounded acknowledgement |
| Who is Marta to Silas? | His changed manner becomes clearer; history remains for its intended later scene |
| Why are Natharul's riders searching? | The airship broadens the question; it does not prove they are searching for Alaric personally |
| What happened at the battlefield? | No new vision or explanation is necessary here |
| Who was Alaric? | No sudden memory, hidden sword skill or recognition from Silas |
| What is Alaric's 『Affinity』? | Remains undisclosed to Silas unless the author deliberately chooses a new reveal |

The chapter must give enough ordinary answers that the withheld personal answers have weight. Practical clarity is not the enemy of mystery.

## 10. Visual page style and prose control

The chapter should use the house style developed through the Chapter 1 tests:

- Close third person through Alaric throughout.
- A new paragraph for each speaker, with clear tags or action beats when needed.
- Connected prose for walking, observation and conversation. Several related sentences can share a paragraph.
- Isolated short paragraphs at genuine pressure points: recognising the smoke, being lifted, identifying the ship.
- Italics for immediate internal thought and precise stress. Avoid repeatedly italicising the same emotional conclusion.
- Ordinary quotation marks, no permanent speaker labels and no `ーー`.
- Established typography such as 『Affinity』 retained when the term appears. Do not bracket every magical noun.
- Few deliberate section breaks. A break after the smoke and another after the ogre may suit the draft; changing location by a few strides does not require one.
- Concrete observation before explanation. Alaric may infer Silas's motives, but the narration must not certify those inferences.
- Figurative language only when it adds something available to Alaric. No ornamental funeral-bell comparisons or grand summaries of how the world feels.

The immediate thoughts should develop. An early *Why is he coming with me?* can become *He knows her. Why won't he say how?* and later *I thought I could get there.* They should not become five versions of *I don't know*.

In the fight, the reader needs a continuous physical picture. Shortening the prose does not excuse losing track of the sword, the held ankle, Wena or Silas's approach.

## 12. Proposed additions that need author decisions

The report makes concrete recommendations without treating them as approved lore. These are the few additions that would need to be settled when drafting:

1. **The safer approach to Kelmend and the disputed inn visit.** A southern service path or route along the wall is a proposed solution. Its geography and connection to the inn must fit the town design. Silas's plan to take Gerolt's sword to Marta alone first is also a new recommendation, not an established event.
2. **The ogre's nature and speech.** I favour a predatory individual with limited understanding. Its classification and wider species culture remain open.
3. **The sword's carrying arrangement.** The draft needs a practical answer before Alaric runs and is lifted.
4. **The limited Gerolt answer.** Decide whether Silas admits knowing him here, or whether the practical concession and name exchange are enough.
5. **The airship's identification and journey.** Set what Silas recognises, whether this is the same vessel from Chapter 5, and how its travel fits the map.
6. **Kelmend's response.** Decide whether the vessel is expected, welcomed, feared or politically ambiguous. Show only what Alaric can observe.

None of these requires changing the central five-beat shape. They determine whether the draft's causes and consequences are convincing.

## 13. How I would draft and review it

Write the journey and aftermath exchanges first, in context, until Alaric's departure and their later agreement feel credible. Those conversations carry the chapter's structure. Then write the ogre to produce the physical and emotional conditions the aftermath needs, rather than adding an arbitrary fight and repairing the relationship around it afterwards.

Draft the opening and smoke scene with the current end of Chapter 3 beside them. Draft the airship with the relevant Chapter 5 passage beside it. This keeps the return to Alaric and the connection to Seralune precise.

On review, check these outcomes:

- A reader can explain why Alaric leaves without calling him simply stupid.
- A reader can explain why Silas withheld the personal answers without being told his entire history.
- Alaric's action during the attack matters, but grants no unexplained skill.
- The two men behave differently after the fight, and both retain their personalities.
- Grief changes its form across the chapter instead of repeating a conclusion.
- Wena remains a dog, not an interpreter of everybody's motives.
- The route, hearing distance, sword and injuries remain consistent.
- The ending identifies a new problem without claiming more than Alaric or Silas can know.

The next drafting step would be the opening through the first road conversation. That will let us test the actual voices and the change in atmosphere before committing to the escape and fight prose.

## 14. What could make this chapter 100/100?

**Author's design requirement, added 30 September 2026:** Ask this question when designing this chapter and all future chapter proposals. Answer it through specific improvements and honest challenges, rather than treating a functional outline as the highest achievable version.

Here, “100/100” is the ambition to realise this particular chapter's fullest potential. An outline cannot guarantee a perfect reading experience, and larger spectacle, more suffering or additional words do not establish quality. The finished voice, choices, pacing and emotional effect will determine how well the design works.

### What still limits this proposal

The broad sequence remains familiar: an inexperienced traveller leaves his protector, encounters a predator and is rescued. The explained disagreement and Alaric's contribution improve it, but they do not automatically make it exceptional. The two principal remaining tests are whether the spoken exchanges feel unmistakably like these people, and whether one ordinary action makes their changed relationship felt without explanation.

### The strongest opportunities

1. **Let the sword dispute resolve through a small act of respect.** After the fight, Silas might offer to carry the sword because Alaric is hurt or exhausted. Alaric refuses. Earlier, Silas wanted to take it ahead to Marta; now he accepts the refusal and adjusts to the slower pace. That behaviour demonstrates a change while preserving the sword as Gerolt's specific gift and instruction. It should replace a redundant explanatory exchange, not add another scene simply for emphasis.
2. **Make the reader's sympathy move during the argument.** Initially, Alaric's questions should sound reasonable. Then the reader understands Silas's concern about bringing pursuit to Marta. Alaric's decision to leave should be understandable while the danger remains visible. The aftermath lets both valid concerns survive. If either person is obviously right throughout, the chapter loses much of its emotional complexity.
3. **Give the ordinary-world scene a life of its own.** The people Alaric observes should be occupied by a task and relationship that would continue without him. Their disagreement, humour or use of magic should supply a particular glimpse of Mydea, not stand there to illustrate a theme. Alaric's interest must arise from what he sees.
4. **Consider different meanings of the airship for different witnesses.** If the political design permits it, people at Kelmend might admire or welcome the same vessel Alaric associates with the riders. That would make the ending reveal something unsettling about the living world, beyond the size of a threat. This is an optional new political proposal; it must agree with Kelmend's established relationship with Natharul.
5. **Make every emotional return change its subject.** The smoke concerns Gerolt's absence; the sword concerns carrying out his last instruction; Wena concerns a life still dependent on Alaric; the aftermath concerns another person choosing to remain. These should not all end with the same statement of guilt. The chapter's strongest emotional effect would be Alaric beginning a present relationship while he still believes his meaningful life is behind him.

### What I would test before claiming the design has reached that level

- Does Alaric's departure remain convincing when read directly after Chapter 3, including all the help Silas has already given him?
- Can the conversation be read aloud without sounding like questions and answers supplied by an outline?
- Does Alaric's contribution to survival remain physically believable and necessary, without becoming a reward for hidden power?
- Is there a small, memorable action that changes how the reader sees Silas?
- Does the airship add a new meaning or immediate consequence beyond repeating the arrival of Natharul's riders?
- Which passage could be removed because another passage already does its work?

The first improvement I would test is the sword refusal and Silas accepting it. It connects the proposed conflict, Gerolt's actual instruction, Alaric's independence and Silas's developing care through conduct. If that action feels contrived in the draft, replace it with a more natural behaviour that accomplishes the same relationship change; keep the aim, not the favourite device.
