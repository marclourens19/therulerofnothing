# Severance

Cyrandor woke while the physician was changing the linen round what was left of his arm.

He tried to sit up. Pain dragged a sound through his teeth, and fresh red darkened the innermost fold of the bandage before the physician could press him flat again.

"You'll open it."

"Then stop touching it," Cyrandor managed.

The physician stared at him. "I'm trying to keep you alive."

"You've managed. Let an old man enjoy the surprise."

It barely carried past the bed.

Four lamps burned round the infirmary, their crystals turned low for the last hours of the night. Clean bowls stood stacked on a side table. In one of them, a curved needle lay beside a length of thread stained red for half its length.

It was the third night since Seralune had gone down the southern stair, and it had nearly ended.

Lord Leorin stood by the locked door. He'd traded his court robes for a dark working coat, though the silver branch at his collar still marked his office. Thaeroval waited on the far side of the bed.

Cyrandor found him last.

"Prince Thaeroval." He let his head settle back into the pillow. "Have you come back for the rest of me?"

"No."

"Then you should let him work. You left him plenty to do."

The physician reached for the bowl. Thaeroval stopped him with a small movement of one hand.

"Can he answer questions?"

"He's woken twice," the physician said. "The first time, he didn't know where he was. The second, he tried to leave."

"I was thirsty."

"You were halfway out of bed."

"The water was across the room."

The physician turned to Thaeroval. "A few questions. No more. If he starts bleeding again, I end this, whether you're the prince or the First Blade."

"Give him something for the pain."

"Anything strong enough to help will cloud him."

"Afterwards, then."

Leorin stepped away from the door. "Your Highness, this man opened a forbidden royal passage, attacked the palace guard and helped the princess flee Natharul. His comfort can wait until he tells us where she is."

"Cousin." Thaeroval turned. "Wait outside."

Leorin didn't move. "You asked me to witness the questioning."

"I no longer require a witness."

The physician dropped the bloodied linen into a bowl. "Nor do I require half the royal court standing over my patient."

Leorin gave him a look that would have emptied most rooms. The physician reached for another bandage without appearing to notice it.

"Five minutes," Leorin said to Thaeroval. "The king returns before dawn. I will not have him learn that we questioned the traitor with no one present to verify what was said."

He opened the door. Two guards straightened in the passage outside.

The physician tied off the fresh linen, checked Cyrandor's pulse, and pointed at the stain already spreading through it.

"If that reaches the outer fold, you call me."

"I will."

Only when the physician had joined Leorin in the passage did Thaeroval close the door. The lock turned.

Cyrandor looked at it. "Of course you locked it."

Thaeroval came back to the bedside. His sword stayed in its sheath. He'd left his cloak somewhere, and pale dust lay along the joints of his dark armour. A dried line of Cyrandor's blood marked one groove of his right vambrace.

"You betrayed the royal family."

"I served its queen."

"You drugged two guards."

"They'll mend."

"You raised an anchored ward against me."

"It almost slowed you down." Cyrandor's mouth bent without quite reaching a smile. "I'd hoped for longer."

Thaeroval drew the chair closer and sat. He didn't unbuckle the sword.

"I am sorry for your arm."

Cyrandor turned his head on the pillow.

"Are you?"

"I chose a wound you could survive. Breaking the anchor any other way would have killed you."

"That's an explanation."

"It is the truth."

"I didn't say it wasn't." His remaining hand moved across the blanket and stopped short of the bound stump. "You looked at her before the blood reached the floor. Not at me."

Thaeroval took a long time to answer.

"She should never have seen it."

"No," Cyrandor said. "She shouldn't have."

One of the lamps gave a low glass hum. The light in its cage thinned, then came back.

Thaeroval leaned in, keeping his voice below the hearing of the guards.

"Where did you mean to take her?"

"Away from that room."

"Do not spend what strength you have avoiding me. Which country?"

Cyrandor shut his eyes. "Mydea."

Thaeroval sat back. "Where in Mydea?"

"I don't know."

"You carried her under the palace with no destination but another continent?"

"I was to get her out of Natharul. Someone else was meant to help after that."

"Who?"

"That name was never passed to me."

"Whose order were you following?"

Cyrandor looked at the covered window. No dawn showed round its edges yet.

"Your mother's."

Thaeroval didn't rise. The fingers resting on his knee went still.

"My mother left Natharul a thousand years ago."

"She planned for more than one lifetime."

"Explain."

Cyrandor shifted against the pillow. The colour drained from his face before he found a way of lying that didn't pull at the bandage.

"The queen gave an order to a family in her service. One keeper passed it to the next. We were to watch the lower seal. If it ever broke, and Princess Seralune woke, one of us was to reach her before the court decided what should be done with her again."

"Again," Thaeroval said.

"That's how she gave the order."

"She built a hidden service round my sister and told no one in her own house."

"She'd already seen what her own house chose."

His shoulder struck the bedframe, and the nearest lamp rattled on its hook. Thaeroval leaned over and set the pillow straight behind him before the old man could drag himself any higher.

Cyrandor didn't thank him.

"My mother abandoned Seralune," Thaeroval said. "Whatever order she left does not change that."

"No. It only proves she knew they might never let her back."

"Where is she?"

"I've never met the queen."

"You said the trail begins in Mydea."

"That's what I inherited."

"And nothing more?"

Cyrandor met the question and didn't answer it.

Thaeroval's eyes went to the open collar of the old man's nightshirt. A pale line crossed the skin at the base of his throat, where a chain had rested for years.

"You wore something under your uniform."

Cyrandor's chin dipped, by the smallest amount.

"Every day I passed you in these halls," Thaeroval said. "The same chain. It is gone."

"It reached the person it was kept for."

"My mother's lily signet."

Cyrandor held out for the space of a breath.

"Yes."

Thaeroval stood. The chair legs made no sound on the stone.

"Seralune has it."

"It belongs to her."

"Someone may have seen it."

"When she woke, she ran into your arms." Cyrandor's voice gave out halfway, and he pushed the rest through anyway. "The next time she saw you, she ran the other way."

Thaeroval went to the foot of the bed.

"She does not remember why the seal was necessary. She does not remember what happened when her mana broke loose. Giving those memories back before I understand what woke her could destroy far more than this palace."

"Did you tell her that?"

"I told her enough to keep her safe."

"You told her to wait in a locked room."

"I was finding out what had broken beneath her."

"And when morning came, she still had no answer."

The stain reached the outer fold of the bandage.

Thaeroval pulled the bell cord beside the bed. Footsteps answered from the passage at once.

"She was safe here," he said.

Cyrandor's hand pressed down on the linen above the wound as the door opened.

"She didn't believe you."

The physician came in with Leorin behind him. Thaeroval stepped out of his way.

"You said five minutes," the physician snapped.

"It has been four."

"Then you've managed a great deal of bleeding in less time than most." He cut through the new linen and reached for the curved needle. "Out."

Leorin waited in the doorway. "Did he give you anything useful?"

"Seralune is looking for the queen in Mydea. She carries the queen's signet."

Leorin glanced past him at the bed. "That is more than he gave me."

Thaeroval looked at the bed one last time.

"Keep him alive."

The physician didn't look up. "That has been my intention, despite everyone's interference."

Cyrandor had turned his face to the wall.

Thaeroval left him there.

---

The medicine bottles began to ring against one another before the infirmary door had closed.

A low vibration came up through the floor, climbed the walls and set every hanging lamp swinging on its chain. The guards looked at the ceiling. Somewhere deeper in the palace, metal doors groaned in their frames.

Leorin went to the nearest window and drew the covering aside.

The night had gone. The royal warship had taken its place.

Its armoured underside reached out past the northern towers, pulling loose leaves up out of the gardens and pressing every banner flat. At the far end of the corridor, servants left a cart against the wall and ran for the landing terraces. Guards went after them, speaking into black crystals as they ran.

"He has returned early," Leorin said.

A royal messenger reached them before Thaeroval could answer. The young man stopped several paces off and bowed too fast to control the fall of his cloak.

"First Blade. His Majesty requires you aboard immediately."

Leorin let the covering drop. "Did he give a reason?"

"Only that the prince was to come alone."

Leorin looked at Thaeroval. "Then you had better not keep him waiting, cousin."

By the time they reached the royal landing terrace, the warship's shadow had swallowed every wall, and the wind between the towers smelled of burnt wheat and engine heat. A boarding bridge came down through the dark and cracked the stone where it landed. Royal guards came down it in pairs, grey ash on their boots and greaves. None of them had stopped to clean it off.

The messenger halted at the foot of the bridge. "His Majesty is in the forward chamber."

Thaeroval went aboard alone.

Inside, there was no welcoming line. No one from the court waited to receive the king. Soldiers hauled crates away from the forward chamber while attendants carried sealed cases the other way, and every new order arrived before the last had finished.

Two guards opened a pair of metal doors as Thaeroval reached them.

The king stood inside with his back to the room.

He still wore the armour he had worn across the battlefield outside Kelmend. A long dark mantle hung from his shoulders, its hem stiff with mud and ash. Pale hair had come loose from under the narrow gold band at his brow. When he turned, a black smear crossed one cheek where a gloved hand had failed to wipe it away.

"Close the doors."

The guards did.

"Lock them."

One guard hesitated. Thaeroval reached behind him and turned the inner wheel himself. Bars drove into the walls with three heavy blows.

The king waited for the last one.

"When did Seralune wake?"

"Three nights ago."

"Before the report from Mydea?"

"Yes."

"How long before?"

"The lower seal broke near midnight. The first message from Kelmend arrived a few hours after."

The king crossed to the black crystal fixed above the chart table. Lines of green writing covered it. He wiped them away with the side of one hand, and new lines began forming under the glass.

"A few hours," he said. "Not days. Not months."

"What did you find?"

The king turned round.

"An army we watched die a thousand years ago."

The ship shifted round them. A metal joint complained somewhere beyond the wall.

Thaeroval said nothing.

"Their standards belonged to houses that have not existed for a thousand years. Their armour carried seals we outlawed after the Remaking. The machines were built before half the foundries in Natharul had names. And they appeared in Mydea without crossing a border, without a supply road, without a single scout seeing them come."

"Could they have been preserved under the field?"

"They were not buried." The king's voice rose, then dropped at once. "They stood in battle lines. Some were still mounted. Thousands died in the same moment, and no one in Kelmend heard them arrive, or fight."

He pressed both palms to the chart table. Ash broke off one glove and marked the map.

"I touched one of the machines."

"And?"

"I remembered the day its line broke against our western host." The king was looking past him now, at the locked doors. "I remembered the captain who led it. He wore a gold plate over the left side of his jaw, because your blade had taken the bone beneath. His body was there. The plate was still in place."

Thaeroval's head dipped once.

"You believe the field and Seralune are connected."

"I believe nothing from that age comes back by accident."

"She remembers none of it."

"Not yet."

The king left the table and came close enough that the dirt on his mantle brushed Thaeroval's boots.

"Where is she?"

"Her trail ended at Waluna. She is no longer in the settlement."

"Then she took a ship."

"Most likely."

"To where?"

"Mydea."

The king turned away. The chart table was only a few steps off, but he went back to it as if the room had pushed him there.

"How do you know?"

"Cyrandor said the route prepared for her began there. Seralune carries Mother's lily signet."

The king stared at the map between the two continents. Shipping lanes crossed the sea in thin inked curves.

"Who prepared it?"

"He gave me no name."

"And you let him live?"

"He survived the wound."

"That was not what I asked."

"He knows no exact place."

The king struck the table once. The crystal jumped in its silver frame.

"I have an ancient army standing round a human farm, a broken seal under my own palace, and a daughter whose power once helped tear countries out of Lazaran. Do not give me measured answers as though any part of this is still ordinary."

Thaeroval didn't give him any answer at all.

The king pointed across the table at Mydea.

"You will follow her."

"Yes."

"You will bring her back to Natharul before whatever began in that field reaches her."

"I will bring her back."

"Anyone who knowingly keeps her from you has chosen what follows."

Thaeroval's face gave him nothing.

"Be precise, Father."

"Kill them."

It went no further than the chart table.

"The servants who took her?" Thaeroval asked.

"Anyone who stands between you and her. I will not lose a kingdom because you mistook accomplices for innocents."

"And Seralune?"

The king looked at the door before he answered, though no sound came from behind it.

"Restrain her. Bring her home. We restore the upper seal before her mana settles."

"If she refuses?"

"You are the First Blade of Natharul. Her refusal is not beyond you."

"That was not my question."

The king crossed the narrow space between them.

"You know what it cost the last time. Better than I do." His voice had gone very low. "If her memories come back, and you cannot contain what follows, you end it. Before another city is gone."

Thaeroval didn't move.

"Can you do that?"

The engines carried their low vibration through the chamber.

"I will find her," Thaeroval said. "I will bring her back, and I will restore the seal."

"Can you kill her?"

Thaeroval reached past him for the wheel.

The king caught it before it could turn.

"Answer me."

"You gave me an order." Thaeroval looked down at the hand on the wheel. "I heard it."

A fist struck the other side of the door.

"Your Majesty!"

The king let go. Thaeroval turned the wheel, the bars drew back, and a soldier staggered over the threshold. Blood covered the front of his armour. More had dried under one ear.

"The southern terraces. Something came up out of the old channels."

The king took in the blood. "How many?"

"We don't know." The soldier swallowed and forced himself straighter. "The south-wall wardwrights heard the old channels ring in the middle watch. Every one of them at once, like something had called down them. Then things started crawling up out of the aqueduct. Small ones. They sent us down to clear vermin." His voice cracked. "The first company stopped answering. The second turned on each other before it reached the lower court."

Another impact shuddered through the ship. This one came from below.

The soldier looked at the floor. "It's inside the wards."

The king took one step towards the crystal. "How close to the palace?"

"The lowest terrace is gone. It crossed the southern watch court before I reached the stair." His voice roughened. "Majesty, the men aren't only fighting it. They're seeing things. Hearing people who aren't there."

"How many companies between it and the inner wall?"

"Two. One, by now."

The king turned to Thaeroval. "Take the palace guard."

"No."

It stopped the wounded soldier more completely than it stopped the king.

"It turns armed men on whoever stands nearest," Thaeroval said. "Send another company, and it will turn them too."

"You have not seen what it is."

"Neither have you."

He stepped past the soldier. The king followed him into the corridor.

"Thaeroval."

He stopped.

"Nothing reaches the palace. Do you understand me?"

"Clear the terraces above it. Keep every soldier who has not heard the voices behind the inner wall."

"That is not an answer."

Thaeroval looked back at him.

"It will not reach the palace."

In the boarding bay, a landing craft still sat unsecured from the flight back from Mydea, ash caked on one armoured fin.

"Release it," Thaeroval told the crew.

"First Blade, the core's still hot from the descent."

"Then the pilot will watch it."

The braces drew back while he climbed in, and the deck fell away beneath them before the pilot had properly reached his seat.

The warship filled every window until the craft turned south. Then Natharul opened below.

The palace held the mountain's broadest shelf, its towers and courts stepping down through terraces cut into white stone. Lights had woken across the upper levels, and servants were crossing the courtyards towards the northern shelters.

The southern slope lay dark.

One watchtower burned beside the broken aqueduct. Past it, brief spells flared and died among the lower courtyards: orange fire against a wall, a white stroke of Wind, the blue glare of Water striking stone.

None of them were aimed the same way.

The pilot saw it too. "Where do I put you?"

"The third terrace. Land behind the western stair and go back to the ship."

"I was ordered to stay with the First Blade."

"You were ordered before we knew what killed the first company." Thaeroval settled the sword more firmly at his hip. "Go back to the ship."

The pilot brought them down.

Stone hit the landing struts hard enough to throw sparks past the windows. Thaeroval had the side open before the craft had settled. He stepped out into smoke, and the pilot lifted away.

No line of soldiers waited to receive him.

---

The third terrace had been built round a long reflecting pool.

The pool was full of bodies now. Green cloaks moved under water the lamps along its edge had turned red. Beside it, a soldier knelt on the paving with both hands wrapped round a spear driven through another man's armour. He was whispering apologies, and he was still leaning on it.

The man under the spear had one hand closed round the kneeling soldier's wrist. It had stopped trying to push him away.

Thaeroval took hold of the shaft and pulled it out.

The kneeling soldier turned. One of his pupils had spread until almost no colour was left round it.

"You put her in there," he said.

Thaeroval struck him once under the ear with the sheathed hilt, and he folded onto the stones beside the pool.

A woman in broken armour was backing out of the western colonnade. Blood ran from her hair into her collar. She raised a short blade as he came towards her.

"Stay away from me."

"Put it down."

"You're wearing his face."

"Whose?"

The answer came from the dark arch behind him.

"The one who left us."

An older man's voice. It spoke gently, patiently, the way you'd speak to a frightened child.

The woman made a sound that broke somewhere between anger and grief. Her blade swung away from Thaeroval towards the arch.

"Father?"

Black roots slid over the stone inside it.

Thaeroval stepped between her and the dark and pushed her blade down before her arm could come up again.

"The man speaking is not there. Go to the landing court."

"He knows my name."

"Go."

She stared past him until another voice called from the upper stair.

"First Blade!"

Three soldiers crouched behind an overturned stone bench. One of them was holding up a fourth, whose leg bent below the knee at an angle no armour could hide. Relief went across all three faces at once.

"First Blade, here!"

Thaeroval took the bleeding woman by her good arm and brought her to them.

"Get behind the western stair. Do not answer anything that speaks from a shadow."

The eldest soldier looked past him towards the lower terrace. "There are men alive below us."

"How many?"

"We heard a dozen at least. Then they started killing each other."

"Take these two up. I will bring whoever is left."

The injured man tried to put his foot down. Bone shifted under the greave.

Thaeroval crouched, lifted him without touching the ruined leg, and carried him behind the stair. The others followed with the woman between them. He set the man against the wall and unbuckled the clasp that was strangling the swollen limb.

"The next landing craft will take you."

The man caught the edge of his vambrace. "It uses them. The dead. It sounds like the dead."

"Then give it no answer."

Thaeroval stood and went down into the lower court.

The killing there had nearly finished.

Hundreds had come down through the southern terraces since the first alarm. They lay among shattered weapons and roots thick enough to split paving. Some had died facing the dark at the end of the court. More had died facing each other.

A line of archers had fallen under their own arrows. Two were still alive. One was dragging herself between the bodies with a shaft through her side. The other sat against a pillar, sawing a knife through the straps under his breastplate.

"Before the Tree," he was saying. "Arilen, before the Tree, I didn't mean it, I didn't —"

Thaeroval took the knife out of his hand. The soldier clawed after it.

"She's inside me."

"No one is inside you."

"I *heard* her."

"You heard what wanted you to open your armour." Thaeroval hauled him upright and laid the crawling archer's arm across his shoulders. "Carry her to the western stair."

The soldier looked at the dark under the eastern wall. "It'll follow."

"Then do not stop."

Training held where reason had gone. The man went.

Low things moved among the dead, pale as the roots, many-legged, no bigger than cats. Where the lamplight touched them, they went still. When it moved on, they went back to feeding.

Something laughed inside the hollow pillar behind him.

It used Cyrandor's voice.

"The next time she saw you, she ran the other way."

Thaeroval kept walking.

The laugh moved ahead of him. It slid into the shadow under a fallen balcony and came out of a dead soldier's open mouth.

"And when morning came, she still had no answer."

His pace didn't change.

The roots changed instead. They'd got into the bodies nearest the eastern wall through the gaps in their armour and through open wounds. Black-purple threads ran under exposed skin. Thicker growths held the dead upright where they'd fallen and turned them, one by one, to face him as he passed.

A hand closed round his ankle.

The soldier it belonged to had no strength left to lift his head. Dark veins covered one side of his face.

"Please," he said.

Thaeroval bent and cut the root round his waist with the narrow blade in his gauntlet. The growth shrank back from the metal. He dragged the man clear and laid him down beyond its reach.

"Can you stand?"

"I don't know."

"Try."

The soldier reached for the offered forearm. His legs held long enough for two survivors to break from behind a fallen wall and take him between them.

"Upper terrace," Thaeroval told them. "Keep to the lit side."

One of them bowed while she was still holding the man up.

"You came," she said. Relief broke her voice on the second word. The other tried to bow as well and nearly dropped the wounded man between them.

Thaeroval turned to the eastern wall.

The creature came out of its shadow.

It stood a little taller than a man, though its shape refused every proportion that would have made it one. Its arms hung past its knees and ended in hooked black fingers. A narrow head leaned out from shoulders woven of smoke and root and wet strands that moved with no wind to move them. Two white eyes opened in a face with no skin.

The mouth opened last. Pale teeth crowded it in several uneven rows.

Behind it, its shadow covered half the wall.

The arch it had come through was no bigger than a door.

"First Blade," whispered the soldier beside him.

Five survivors were close enough to hear him. One had no weapon. Another held a sword in both hands because neither hand could keep it steady on its own. They'd gathered behind Thaeroval without being told, and now they watched the creature over his shoulders.

The white eyes moved across them.

The court filled with voices.

A mother called for her son from under the pool. A dead commander ordered the archers back into line. Someone wept behind the eastern wall. Someone else whispered that the woman with the sword was rootless, that she had sold them once and would do it again if they let her live.

"Rootless," the man beside her said, as if he'd only now understood it. He turned towards her.

Thaeroval stepped back between them.

"Put your weapons on the ground."

"It'll kill us."

"Put them down."

The creature's shadow reached for their feet.

"He wants you helpless," the dead commander warned from the pool. "Look at his sword. He came to finish what we started."

The man lifted his blade towards Thaeroval.

Thaeroval caught the flat of it between two armoured fingers and turned it aside.

"If I meant to kill you, you would not be holding that."

The sword slid out of the man's grip and rang on the paving. The others followed.

The creature tilted its head.

Its shadow slid over Thaeroval's boots.

Voices came out of it one after another. Cyrandor. Leorin. The king. Soldiers dead for a thousand years. Each of them accused him of something different.

None of them used Seralune's voice.

None of them got an answer.

The creature's mouth closed.

For the first time since Thaeroval had come onto the terrace, it moved backwards.

He put himself fully between it and the survivors.

"Go to the western stair."

A woman bent for her sword.

"Leave it."

"First Blade, there might be more below."

"There will be nothing below when I am finished."

They ran.

By the time they reached the stair, there was no one living left between Thaeroval and the eastern wall.

The creature sank into the stone.

Its white eyes opened again in the shadow of a ruined tower thirty paces to his left. One hooked arm followed them out. The rest of it stayed stretched across the distance between the two shadows, too long and too thin to belong inside the court.

Thaeroval drew his sword.

It cleared the sheath by a hand's width, then came free. The nearest lamp went on burning exactly as it had.

He turned the edge towards the creature. Black light ran from his wrist into the metal and swallowed every reflection along its length.

Then the pressure came.

Every other living thing on the terrace lost the strength in its knees. The survivors fell against the western stair. Higher up, soldiers dropped their spears and caught at the walls. A landing craft coming down through the smoke lurched as its pilot fought the controls.

The creature let go of the tower. Its white eyes opened in the shadow under the pool. Then under a body. Then in the broken aqueduct beyond the court.

Distance didn't take it beyond the edge.

Thaeroval cut once.

A line crossed the world.

The creature came apart first. Its body stayed standing until the upper half slid off the lower and struck the paving. Every pale tooth had been divided at the same height. No blood came out of it. Black vapour poured from both halves and ended before it reached the ground.

Beyond the court, roots came apart inside the stone.

The cut ran on through the broken aqueduct. From chambers no soldier had reached came the scrape of claws, then bodies falling, one after another. The pale things among the dead stopped mid-step and toppled over. The growth knotted round the mountain's buried wards came apart with them. No flame followed. No burst of mana. Things that had been joined a heartbeat before simply stopped meeting.

The southern mountainside opened.

Stone hundreds of feet thick slid along a level line. Trees leaned towards each other as the earth beneath them parted. What was left of the crater face began to slide towards Waluna, carrying terraces and dead roots and the foundations of the lowest watchtower with it.

Far above, the clouds divided. Moonlight and the first grey of dawn met in the gap.

The line reached the coast south of Waluna's outer headland.

The water stood apart.

For several heartbeats, the sea made two dark walls beyond the headland. The floor between them ran out towards the horizon, bare, divided by the same line: through fish, through broken anchors, through the ribs of ships lost before Waluna's oldest pier was built.

No wave crossed the division. No sound did either.

Then the world moved again.

The mountain fell.

**KRRRRAAAAAAM.**

The southern slope came down on the lower terraces. Stone leapt into the air. The watchtower vanished inside it.

The sea came back with a roar that reached the palace.

The two walls met along the severed line and struck the outer face of the headland. The stone took the first blow. A broken surge curled round it into the harbour, flinging ships against their moorings and turning every seaward window silver.

Thaeroval lowered his sword.

Beyond the court, the creature's remains lay under the broken aqueduct. The people behind him were whole. Not one new wound had opened among them.

All across the terrace, the voices had stopped.

The soldier with the dark veins went past on a stretcher. The roots round him had died, but the black-purple marks under his skin went on pulsing. A physician turned him away from the other wounded and ordered a landing craft cleared for him alone.

Behind the western stair, a soldier began to laugh. It lasted a few breaths, then turned into sobbing. Others lifted their heads and looked round at the people they had wounded.

The woman from the third terrace had reached the court above. She tried to stand when she saw him.

"First Blade."

Thaeroval sheathed the sword.

The pressure lifted. The survivors got up slowly, holding on to the walls and to each other.

The man nearest him reached for the edge of his armour.

"You saved us."

Thaeroval stepped past the hand.

"Move everyone above the fourth terrace. The slope is still going."

The man stayed on his knees.

"Did you hear me?"

"Yes, First Blade."

"Then move."

Thaeroval crossed the court without looking at the wounded beside the pool.

Above the palace, the warship had turned broadside to the coast. Figures lined its forward windows.

The king stood apart from them. When Thaeroval looked up at the ship, his father stepped back from the glass.

---

Dawn found the dead roots in a maintenance channel under the fourth terrace.

The mountain was still settling beyond the cordon, and physicians worked under the western colonnade. In the narrow passage, Thaeroval stood behind a kneeling wardwright while Leorin held the lamp.

The black growth had hardened round a bundle of silver conduits. Its fibres went in at the joints and came out further along, fused so completely that the wardwright's knife tore pieces from both.

He cleared another section and stopped.

"This shouldn't be here."

"The root?" Leorin asked.

"The junction." The wardwright scraped centuries of mineral stain off a silver plate. A branching seal showed under his thumb. "This conduit belonged to the lower circle. It carried the excess away from the central chamber."

Leorin brought the lamp closer. "Away to where?"

"The lower channels took it to an outlet under the southern slope. It should have gone out into the sea. Nothing should have stayed here."

"And yet the roots found it."

"My lord, this growth may have been feeding on the lower ward for —"

"Enough," Thaeroval said.

He held out his hand. "Your report."

The wardwright gave up a folded sheet covered in diagrams and hurried writing.

"Who else has seen this junction?"

"One other from my order. Lord Leorin. The recovery captain."

"All four of you report to me. No copy goes into the court record until I return."

The wardwright glanced at Leorin, who didn't object.

"Yes, First Blade."

Thaeroval folded the sheet and put it inside his armour.

"Seal the channel. No one comes in without my authority."

The wardwright gathered his tools and went. Leorin stayed.

"The king should see that."

"He saw what came out of it."

"That is not the same as seeing where it grew."

"Father has already bound Seralune's waking to the army in Mydea. If he learns these roots fed from her seal, he will bind them to every death on that terrace as well."

"Perhaps he should."

"You watched her wake." There was no anger in it. "Did she send that creature against anyone?"

"No."

"Then the report waits."

"You are hiding evidence from the king."

"I am keeping him from deciding what happened before the wardwrights have finished their work."

Leorin's hand tightened on the lamp. "I swore root and crown, cousin. So did you."

"Then keep your oath. If he asks you, tell him what you saw."

"I saw you take it."

"Then you will have something true to tell him."

Thaeroval left the channel. Leorin followed several paces behind.

Neither of them mentioned how far the sea had opened.

---

The king was waiting aboard the warship with a black case on the chart table.

He'd cleaned the ash from his face. The armour and the stained mantle hadn't changed. Two guards stood outside the chamber this time, and the doors stayed open until Thaeroval was inside.

The king sent the guards away.

He didn't lock the doors.

"Waluna still stands," he said.

"Yes."

The king read from the crystal without looking down at it. "The harbour master reports two piers torn loose and the lower storehouses flooded. Nineteen injured. Six drowned in the lower harbour." He stopped. "A fishing family, and two men from the docks. The youngest was seven."

Thaeroval waited beside the table.

"Is the eastern pier standing?"

The king looked at him.

"The courier will need somewhere to set down," Thaeroval said.

The king went on looking at him. Then his eyes dropped to the sword at Thaeroval's hip, and stayed there.

"The terraces would have fallen within the hour," he said at last, "if that corruption had gone on through the wards. You stopped it."

There was no praise in it.

Thaeroval opened the black case.

Four bands of dark metal lay inside, joined by fine silver chains. Curved lines covered their inner faces. Laid together, the bands made the same broken circle that had ringed the hollow where Seralune woke.

"These were made after the lower seal failed," the king said. "They interrupt the flow between her mana and the space round her. Not for long. Long enough to bring her home."

Thaeroval lifted one. It opened under his thumb and closed again with a soft metal click.

"Who made them?"

"The three wardwrights who still remember the upper design."

"Who tested them?"

"No one can test a restraint made for Seralune."

He laid the band back in its place.

"If they harm her, I take them off."

"If you take them off too soon, she may harm everyone round her."

"Then I will choose the moment."

The king's mouth opened. Whatever he'd meant to say never came out.

Thaeroval closed the case.

"The royal courier is ready," the king said. "Waluna first. The harbour records will narrow down which ships left after the mountain opened. If she showed your mother's signet to anyone, they will remember it."

"And after Waluna?"

"Mydean waters. Every northern harbour, if it comes to that."

"I will go alone."

"You asked for no company on the terraces either."

"Men beside me would only give Seralune more lives to be afraid for."

The king moved back as Thaeroval fastened the case at his side. He kept the table between them.

"Then go alone."

Thaeroval walked to the open doors.

"Bring your sister home," the king said behind him.

He didn't answer.

He crossed the boarding bridge into the palace and went along the infirmary corridor.

Cyrandor's door stood open while two attendants carried out bowls of bloodied water. The old man lay awake under clean blankets. Fever had left a grey sheen on his face, but his remaining hand no longer pressed on the bandage.

Thaeroval stopped outside.

Cyrandor saw the black case. His eyes went from it to the sword, and last of all to Thaeroval's face.

No guard told the prince to move on. No physician came out to turn him away.

Thaeroval's hand came up as far as the doorframe. It stopped there, a finger's width from the wood.

Then it came down, and he went on.

Cyrandor watched him go.

---

The royal courier waited alone at the edge of the lower bay. It was narrow beside the landing craft, built round a single enclosed seat and two swept armoured fins. Three Wind cores burned under its frame, their pale light driving mist across the floor.

Thaeroval strapped the case behind the seat, to the inner brace. The sword he kept at his hip.

Leorin stood beyond the marked line while the bay crew drew back the clamps.

"And if Waluna's records tell you nothing?" he called over the rising Wind.

"Then I ask who lied."

"And when Mydea asks why a Ruler of Natharul has crossed into the country of another Ruler, uninvited?"

Thaeroval closed the courier's side.

"Tell them I am looking for my sister."

The last clamp let go.

Wind struck the bay hard enough to drive every loose chain across the floor. The courier slid forward, dropped away beneath the warship, and turned south before the palace could rise behind it.

Morning had reached the coast.

The sea had closed over its wound. Beyond Waluna, ships were moving again, drawing white paths across water that gave no sign it had ever stood apart.

The mountain had kept the cut.

It ran through the southern slope in one unbroken line, too straight for any fault and too deep for the new light to find the bottom of it.

Thaeroval flew above it, towards the harbour.
