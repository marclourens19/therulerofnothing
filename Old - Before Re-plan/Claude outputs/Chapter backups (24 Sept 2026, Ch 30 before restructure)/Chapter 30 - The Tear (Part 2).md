# The Tear (Part 2)

The noise came first.

It had always been there, at the edges, in every piece of the field that had broken through to her. Now there were no edges.

Steel on steel, and steel on bone. Screaming, from men and from horses, and from things that were neither. Under all of it, the ringing, deep and slow and enormous, like a bell the size of a hill being struck and struck again.

She knew what made it now. She could see them.

They walked. They were silver, and as tall as houses, and they walked on long jointed legs that bent the wrong way, stepping over the dead as a heron steps through reeds. Every time one of them was struck, it rang.

One stood above her on her left with its silver gone black to the knee. One had fallen, far off, across a whole line of men, and lay ringing on its side.

The ground was red mud to the ankle. It sucked at her. Every step she pulled free cost more than the last.

She took the next one anyway.

There were soldiers everywhere, living and dead. Men in iron. Men in leather. Beastfolk with horns curling from their heads, standing in the same line as the men, shoulder to shoulder, facing the same way. Elves behind them, pale and long-eared like her own people, like her.

Banners she didn't know, black with mud, trodden under.

A man was on his knees in front of her with both hands over his face, and blood coming out between his fingers. She went round him.

A soldier lay across her way with his helmet gone and his eyes open. She stepped over him.

Someone was calling a name. Over and over, the same name, in a voice that had stopped expecting an answer.

So many people were crying. It came from everywhere, from so many throats that it wasn't voices any more, only a sound the field made, and some of it was hers. She was crying. She could taste it. Her face was wet with it, and with mud, and with something else.

There was blood on her hands. She didn't know whose.

She was running.

She could see her own arm out in front of her as she ran, reaching. Her sleeve was stiff with gold thread and filthy to the elbow, and her hand at the end of it was open.

She knew where she was running.

She'd always known. In every piece of this that had come to her since she came to Favale, in the loft and at the coppers and in the lanes, she'd known she was running towards someone. She hadn't known who. Now she did. She didn't have a name for him. She didn't need one. She knew him the way she knew her own hand in the dark.

He was here. He was just ahead. She was so close.

And she was late.

She'd been late all day. She had been late, and late, and the field was between them, and she'd come through it anyway, over the dead and round the dying, and she was nearly there.

The smoke tore open in front of her.

He was there.

---

He was standing among the dead, turning.

He'd been looking for her too. She could see it in how he turned: too fast, the whole of him at once, like someone turning at a sound they've been listening for.

He was young. His hair was dark brown and curling, stuck to his forehead with sweat and blood. There was mud to his thighs and blood across his face, and none of it was his. His eyes were green.

He saw her.

She saw him see her. She saw his whole face change.

She knew that face. She knew it better than her own.

She knew how it looked asleep, and how it looked laughing, and how it looked when he was trying not to laugh, which was worse. She knew it in lamplight. She knew the exact place at the corner of his mouth where the laugh started.

She'd lost it. She'd lost all of it. Someone had taken it out of her and left her the shape of the hole, and she'd walked round the shape ever since she woke, without knowing what it was.

He reached for her.

His bare left hand closed round her wrist.

His thumb lay against the inside of it, where her pulse was, as it always had. As it had a hundred times: in doorways, on stairs, under tables where no one could see. It was the most ordinary thing in the world. It was his hand, and her wrist, and the field went quiet round them.

It went quiet. The ringing and the screaming and the crying all went on, and she couldn't hear any of it. There was only his hand, and his face, a hand's breadth from hers.

His mouth moved. He was saying something.

She couldn't hear it.

She watched his lips. She knew them. She knew every word they'd ever said to her, and she couldn't hear this one. There was no sound at all.

Then the white.

It came from everywhere at once. It came from the sky and the ground and the air between them. It had no edges and no shadows. It came through the smoke, and through the silver giants, and through the dead, and through him.

It came through her own hand, so that she could see the bones in it, and then she couldn't see her hand at all.

In the white, there was a line of black.

She saw it for the space of a heartbeat. It ran across the white like a crack across ice, and it swallowed every gleam along its length, every reflection, until there was nothing on it or near it that shone. It didn't shine itself. It only took.

His hand wasn't on her wrist.

There was nothing in her hand.

There was nothing in front of her. There was no field. There was no one standing on it.

She was holding nothing.

She was late.

---

The stair.

She was on her knees on the stair, in the straw, with Nereth in her arms, and the saint's hand on Nereth's throat. The white was still there under the saint's hand. The bells were still ringing.

Nereth wasn't breathing.

She had been late.

She had been late once, and he'd been standing right there, and he'd reached for her, and she'd been one breath too late, and they'd taken him out of her hand.

*Not again.*

It came up in her.

It came up as it always had, under her ribs and behind her eyes and beneath her skin, full and patient and with nowhere to go. It had always had nowhere to go.

It had filled her and filled her and pressed at the inside of her, all her life, and there had never been anywhere for it.

There was now.

She reached for him.

She didn't know where he was. She didn't know anything. She only reached, as she'd reached on the field, with everything, with her whole self. Every part of what was in her went out of her at once, in one direction. Away from the hill, out over the roofs, as far as there was.

Towards him, wherever he was.

And something answered.

It answered the way a hand closes. Once.

Then the world tore.

---

She didn't hear it.

It was too big to hear. It went in at her ears and through her head and out the other side, and it took the hearing with it.

She saw it.

The stair went away from her.

It went all at once, in every direction, the way a ring goes out from a stone dropped in still water. Except that it was stone, and it was people.

The step below her lifted in one piece with a family on it, and broke in the air. The step below that went. Below that, the stair held, and the wave went over it.

The doors and hurdles and the sick lying on them went down the hill in one long wave. The wave was made of everything. Straw and lamps and sun tokens. Arms. A child's shoe. A door turning over and over. All of it went down, and away from her, and none of it made any sound at all.

Above her, the temple came apart.

The open doors went in, and the front of the temple after them. The gold roofs lifted off the hill, one after another, up and up, and broke in the air, and came down in pieces that flashed as they turned. The great bell came out of its tower.

She saw it fall, rolling over, slow as a dream. She didn't hear it land.

It went on for longer than anything had ever gone on.

Then it stopped, and the dust came down.

---

It came down for a long time.

It came down grey and brown, and thick, and warm. It came down on her hair and her face and her hands, and on Nereth, and on the straw. It came down on everything, softly, like snow, until the air was so full of it that the sun was only a pale shape behind it.

There was no sound. There was a high, thin whine inside her own head, and nothing else.

She was kneeling.

She was kneeling on her step. Her step was there. The straw was under her knees, and Hobb's little bottle lay in it beside her, unbroken, with its stopper out. Nereth was in her arms.

She'd held on.

*Don't let go*, the saint had said. She hadn't let go.

Against her neck, Nereth's breath went out.

It was the breath she'd taken when the Light went in. It had been in her all this while, and now it went out of her in one long, rattling sigh, and Seralune felt it go, warm against her skin.

Then Nereth drew another.

And another.

Seralune looked down at her.

Nereth's eyes had closed again. The dust lay on her lashes and in the burned creases of her face. Under the dust the lines on her throat were dark again. They were dark and swollen and still, as still as they'd been all morning, as still as they'd ever been.

There was blood on her throat. It wasn't hers.

Seralune looked up.

The step above her was there. The priests weren't on it.

Her brother wasn't on it.

There was the step, and no one on it at all.

"Thaer?"

She said it. She felt it in her throat. She didn't hear it.

"Thaer!"

Nothing moved on the step. Nothing moved anywhere.

Across Nereth from her, where the saint had knelt in the straw with her hand on Nereth's throat, there was no one kneeling. There was the straw, and it was red.

The red went up from it. It went up over the step where her brother had stood, and the steps above that, a long way up, and there were rags of white in it, and she couldn't see anything among them that she could have called a girl.

She looked at it, and her mind wouldn't take it.

It slid off. It slid off like a hand off wet glass. It went looking for something it could hold, and it came back to Nereth's breathing, and held that.

*One breath first*, she thought.

She tried to find one. There wasn't one. There was only Nereth's.

---

She laid Nereth down.

She did it carefully, in the straw, and drew the cloak up over her to the chin. The cloak was grey with dust. It was her brother's. She tucked it round Nereth anyway, as she'd tucked it round her in the morning cool, and the dust came off it onto her hands.

Then she stood up, and turned round, and looked down the stair.

There was no stair.

There was the hill. There were steps on it, broken and tilted and heaped, going down into the dust. She couldn't see the bottom. She could see a long way down, further than she wanted to.

The sick were still on it. Some of them hadn't gone anywhere. The rest were only in the wrong places.

The old man's door had gone. His son lay three steps down from where it had been, on his face, with one arm stretched up the stair towards the place where his father's hand had been. She couldn't see the old man.

*If it's not my turn this year, you go home.*

He hadn't gone home.

Eight steps down, on the finger's width of stone she'd found at the edge of somebody else's sheet, Liss was lying on her side. She was curled round her daughter, the way you curl round something to keep the rain off it. Her face was turned up the stair. Her eyes were open. They were still on the step she'd given away.

Seralune went down to her.

She didn't decide to. She was on the step beside her. She knelt down.

There was dust on Liss's eyes. There was dust on the little girl's face, where it showed in the crook of her mother's arm. The girl's lips weren't grey any more. They were the same colour as the dust.

Seralune put out her hand, and took it back.

She got up again. She went on down.

---

She didn't count the steps. She knew them anyway. Her feet knew them.

She went down among the dead, and they were everywhere. They lay on the steps and across the steps and in drifts against the broken edges of the steps, where the wave had dropped them, heaped. Men and women. Children. Babies.

The sick and the people who'd brought them. The white stone the penitents had scrubbed showed through in places, between them.

*The Light that heaps the faithful*, the smallest of the market children had sung, and run away.

She went on down.

*Forty-three.*

---

They were all three there.

Agna had got up onto her feet at the end. Seralune had seen her do it. She'd got up with Kit's paper held over her head for the saint's shadow, as high as her arms would go.

Perhaps she'd sat down again before the wave came. Perhaps she'd still been standing, and turned. Seralune would never know which. She was lying over both of her girls now, with her arms out, and her body across the two of them, as though she'd tried to get all of them under her at once. As though she'd had time.

The blue shawl was over her head. The darn in its corner showed.

Bett's hand was out from under her mother's body, on the step, small and open. Tilde's face was turned away, into her mother's side.

Kit's paper was still in Agna's hand.

It was crumpled, and there was dust in the creases, but it was in her hand. Seralune could see the writing on it, the neat, careful letters that Father Clem had made, the week the boy was born. His name. The day.

*So they can see he's a real boy, with a real mother.*

Seralune knelt down on the forty-third step.

The whine in her head went on and on. She knelt among them in the dust, and she looked at the hand holding the paper. It was a big hand, red from the coppers, with the knuckles swollen. It had held Nereth's head on the wet laundry floor. *There. There. I've got you.*

"Agna," she said.

She felt the name in her mouth. She didn't hear it.

Agna had asked her one thing on the way up. Seralune had had her brother's face on, and she hadn't answered. She'd let her eyes go far away and none of Agna's business, and she'd climbed.

"She's alive," Seralune said.

She said it to the hand with the paper in it. She said it carefully, so that it would be heard.

"You asked me. Nereth's alive. She's alive, Agna."

The hand didn't move.

Seralune knelt there until her knees hurt. Then she got up, and went on down, because she didn't know what else to do, and down was where her feet were going.

A dozen steps further down, on the thirtieth, the older watchman lay with his spear still in his hand. He'd followed them up after all. Wil lay beside him with his mouth open, as it had been in the lane last night, when he'd come round the corner with his lantern and seen her brother standing in the hole in the wall.

*Because I've a wife*, the older one had said, *and so will you, if you live.*

Near the foot of the stair, a boy lay across her way. It was the boy whose eyes had been rolled up white this morning. They still were. She stepped over him.

She went on down.

---

The square was the worst.

The stair had broken the wave. The square had caught all of it.

There was no square. There was a bowl of broken stone and timber and bodies with the dust still falling into it, and at the bottom of the bowl there were more of them than she could look at. So she didn't. She looked at one thing at a time.

Old Hobb's cart had gone over on its side, against what was left of the rope. The little clay bottles lay everywhere across the stones, hundreds of them, broken and whole, and the river that had been in them had run out into the dust and made mud. Hobb was under the cart. She could see his arm.

*It's only out of the river. But you wet her lips.*

The white carriage lay on its side in pieces. One wheel still stood up from the wreck at an angle, not turning. The door with the tree on it had come off and lay face down on the cobbles, so that there was no tree at all, only white lacquer and dust.

The curtain hung out of the broken window, torn half away.

She didn't go close enough to see what was behind it.

Yseth was lying by the upturned wheel, where she'd stood at the window. Her basket had come with her. There were cherries all round her on the stones.

Seralune stood and looked at the cherries.

*First of the year*, the fruit woman had said, in the market, with her hands full of them. *All the cherries.*

Beyond the square, the western city had no roofs.

She could see down the streets from here, the streets she'd walked that morning, and every one of them had been opened up to the sky. The houses stood with their walls broken off at different heights, like teeth.

Smoke was coming up from some of them, thin and grey, in a dozen places, then twenty, where the lamps had fallen into straw. The banners were down. The lanterns that were to have been lit tonight hung broken from their brackets, or lay in the street in glittering drifts, or were gone.

She knew the way from here to the laundry. It was down that street, and left, and down again.

She didn't look for the laundry. She knew what she would find.

---

At the edge of the square, something moved.

It was a man. He was sitting against the stump of a wall, under what had been an arcade at the square's edge, with a beam across his legs. He was grey with dust from his hair to his boots. He was alive.

He was staring at her.

Not at her face. Higher. At her head.

Seralune put up her hand.

Her hood was gone. She didn't know when she'd lost it. Her hair was loose, and full of dust, and under her hand, through it, she could feel the tops of her own ears.

The man's lips moved.

She couldn't hear him. She didn't need to. She'd seen that word shaped often enough, on the road and at the gate and on the river road this morning, behind her brother's back.

*Elf.*

He kept looking at her. His lips moved again.

*Witch.*

She stood in the middle of the square in the falling dust, alone, with her ears bare, without a scratch on her anywhere, and he looked at her, and she let him look.

He was right. She knew it as surely as she'd known the face in the smoke. She knew it all the way down.

It was her.

The thatcher on the road. The woman on the arch. The old woman under the beam. Agna, and Tilde, and Bett. Liss and her daughter, and Denny, and his father, and Wil. Hobb. Yseth.

All of it. Everyone on the hill.

*How many more, Seralune?*

---

The first thing she heard, when her hearing came back, was a bell.

It came to her through the whine, thin and far off, from across the river. Some little temple on the far bank was still ringing for the saint's first steps, because no one had told it to stop.

Other sounds came in behind it, now that there was a way in. Fire, crackling, somewhere close. A dog barking and barking, down in the western city. And under the stones, all round the square, people. Not screaming. There was hardly anyone left to scream. It was a low sound, many voices, like wind in a chimney.

Seralune turned round and looked up the stair.

She'd left Nereth up there. She'd left her alone in the straw on the one step that was still a step, next to what was left of the saint.

She ran.

At the foot of the stair, a voice came out of the stones.

"Mam?"

It was a child's voice, small and flat, from under a slab that had come down across the bottom steps. Seralune stopped. She went down on her knees and got her hands under the edge of the slab and pulled.

It didn't move. It was bigger than a door. It was a piece of the stair itself.

"Mam?"

"I'm here," said Seralune. "I'm here. I'll —"

She didn't know what she'd do. She pulled again, until something tore in her hands, and it didn't move.

"I'll come back," she said to the stone.

She got up. She didn't look back at it. She went up.

She climbed the broken stair, over the dead, round the dead, running when she could, as she'd run on the field. Her feet knew the way.

*Thirty. Forty-three.* She didn't stop at forty-three.

---

Nereth lay in the straw where she'd left her, under the cloak, breathing.

Beside her, in the red, something was moving.

Seralune stopped two steps below and couldn't go any further.

There was no light. She'd thought there would be. The Light had gone into Nereth like the sun; she'd felt it. There was none now. There was only the red on the white stone, and the rags, and in the middle of it all, the thing that moved.

It was the saint. She was coming back.

It was bone.

It was bone first. It came together out of the red a piece at a time, knitting, slowly, and then faster. She saw a spine. She saw ribs curve up out of it, one and then the next, like the fingers of two hands folding together.

Then what goes over bone.

It came up over the bone like a tide coming in over sand, dark and wet, and it moved. It moved the whole time. It was building. It knew what it was building, and it didn't hurry, and it didn't stop.

Seralune heard it. She'd have given anything not to hear it.

Then skin.

Skin came over everything, pale, paler than the stone. It came over the ribs and the long bones and the curve of a skull. It came over a face.

The face was the same face. It was the narrow, tired girl's face that had looked up at her across Nereth and asked her name.

Its eyes opened.

They were grey. They were full of the dust. They looked straight up into the torn sky over the hill, and Seralune could see in them that she had felt all of it. Every part.

The saint drew a breath. Seralune heard it go in, ragged, into lungs that had only just finished making themselves.

Then she screamed.

She screamed lying on her back on the red stone, with her hands clawing at the step, and it wasn't a word. It wasn't a prayer. It was only a girl screaming, because it hurt.

It went on until she had no breath left for it. Then it stopped, and she lay there, and dragged in air, and shook.

There was nothing on her. Her white was gone. She lay on the stair in front of the whole broken hill without anything at all, with her ribs going in and out and the dust coming down on her skin.

Seralune went up the last two steps.

She didn't know she was going to until she'd done it. She found a piece of the white, a big piece, torn, one side of a sleeve and a length of skirt, lying in the red a little way up. She picked it up. It was heavy with blood.

She knelt down by the saint and laid it over her. Over her body, up to her chin, as she'd laid the cloak over Nereth.

"Here," Seralune said.

She didn't know why she said it. It was what the woman called Liss had said, giving up her step.

"Here."

The saint's eyes moved. They came down out of the sky, and found her.

The saint couldn't speak yet. She only looked. There was nothing in her face that Seralune could read: no anger, no fear, no holiness. She only looked at Seralune, a hand's breadth away, the way Seralune supposed she had looked herself, waking in the pale room with no idea where she was.

Then her eyes closed, and she lay on the stair under the bloody white, and breathed.

---

Seralune went back to Nereth.

She knelt down in the straw and lifted her again, cloak and splint and dust and all, and held her against her chest, with Nereth's head under her chin. She didn't know what else to do. She'd been told to hold her. She held her.

Nereth breathed against her neck.

Then Seralune looked out from the hill.

From the stair, before, she'd been able to see the whole of the western city. She could see it now.

She could see what was left of it, and the smoke going up from it in twenty places, thirty, and the river beyond it, broad and brown, and beyond the river the grey mass of the Great Library and the far bank.

And she could see the line.

It ran away from the hill. It started where she was kneeling. It ran down the broken stair and across the square, and out across the western city, straight, as straight as a thing drawn with a rule.

Everything on the line was cut.

She could see it going away from her, over the roofs, through them. Houses had been sliced through from their roofs to their cellars, and stood open, halves leaning away from each other.

Streets had been cut across. Walls. A tower, far off, stood in two pieces with a gap between them the width of a road.

The line went through the aqueduct.

It went through the arch. The one that had stood higher than the others. The arch stood in two halves, leaning apart, and water was coming out of the channel on top in two long white falls, down into the old river road.

There was no rope between them any more.

Nell was down.

The line went on. It went over the river, and the river had been cut too. She could see where: a long, heaving scar of foam across the brown water, where it had opened along the line and fallen back in on itself.

It went into the Great Library. The grey mass of it had been cut open along its side, and there, or somewhere beyond it, she lost the line in the smoke and the haze.

Above the line, the sky was open.

There was no other word for it. It had been cut, like everything else, and it hung open along its whole length, from the hill away over the river until the haze took it, like a wound. She couldn't look into it. Her eyes slid off it, the way her mind had slid off the red on the step.

She knelt at the start of it, on the only whole step on the hill, with Nereth breathing against her throat and the saint breathing on the step above her. She looked along it, over the roofs and the smoke and the river.

She had a face now. She had the feel of a thumb against the inside of her wrist. She didn't have anything else. Not a name. Not a place.

She didn't know what was at the other end.
