# The Secrets We Hide (Part 1)

Seralune had spent two days deciding what she'd say at the gate.

"Name," said Nereth.

"Sera."

"Where from?"

"The coast."

"Which part of the coast?"

"Meren. There's a bell on the harbour office, and they sell fish off the steps, and there's a man hanging from a beam over the warehouse doors."

"Leave out the man."

"He was there."

"Leave him out anyway." Nereth didn't turn her head. "And my face?"

"Fire."

Nereth nodded. She'd said it herself in Meren, and it had been true, and a stranger had brought her water for it.

"And if they pull back your scarf?"

Seralune had no answer to that one. She'd had two days to find one.

Nereth walked on her left, close enough that their sleeves nearly touched, and no closer. She'd kept exactly that distance for two days.

The gate came up out of the dusk with the last of the day's pilgrims jammed under its arch. Everybody wanted to be inside before the bell, and the people carrying the sick had stopped being polite about it.

"Watch on the gate," said a man behind her. "Heads down, girls."

Men in white and gold stood under the arch in pairs.

Seralune lowered her head. She folded her hands in front of her, made herself small in the press of bodies, and waited for the hand on her scarf.

It didn't come.

One of the watchmen was eating a heel of bread. The other was yawning so widely that she could see his back teeth.

The crowd carried her under the arch between them, and past them, and out the other side. Neither of them turned his head.

"Four keep it," somebody murmured on the far side, and somebody else said it back.

"They were pulling hoods yesterday," a woman said behind her. "My sister said."

"Well, they aren't now. Mind your feet."

Seralune didn't look back. Nereth's elbow brushed hers, once, and they walked into Favale with everyone else.

Inside the gate, the temple was giving water away. Servants in grey aprons with gold suns on the bibs ladled it from a row of buckets into cups and bowls and cupped hands.

The last bucket in the row stood unattended. Its ladle hung over the rim.

Seralune held out the cup from Nereth's pack, drank what a servant ladled into it, and passed it to Nereth. It was the first thing either of them had swallowed since the night before.

"Better?" she asked.

"Wetter," said Nereth.

The crowd carried them downhill onto a broad road beside the river. White banners hung along it on poles. Men on ladders were lighting lanterns between them, and the thick yellow glass warmed to gold as each one took.

Ahead, the aqueduct crossed the road.

It came in from the hills on long pale legs and stepped over the road on a single arch, higher than the houses.

The evening bell began.

It rang from the hill, one deep note and then another, and she felt it in her teeth. All along the river road, shutters came down. Doors banged. Up the road, voices started calling, "Curfew! Off the road! Curfew!" The pilgrims broke for the hostels in every direction, and the road that had been full a breath ago began to empty.

Nereth stopped.

She was looking up at the arch. Seralune looked where she was looking.

Something hung from the middle of the span, above the lanterns, where their light only just reached. It was grey. It was turning a little on a rope.

Then Nereth had her by the arm.

She didn't take her hand. She took her arm above the elbow, hard enough to hurt, and turned her bodily away from the arch and into the mouth of a lane.

"Nereth, what —"

"Walk."

"What was —"

"Walk, Sera."

Seralune walked. The bell went on ringing over the lane, and Nereth didn't let go of her arm until the river road was two turnings behind them.

"What was it?" Seralune said.

Nereth didn't slow down. "A thief."

"Where are we going?"

"Somewhere with a door."

Every door they passed was shutting. A woman hauled a child off a step by the back of his shirt. Twice they heard boots at the end of a lane and turned the other way.

The last door still open had a wooden paddle hung over it and steam rolling out of it. A woman was dragging a basket of wet sheets over the step.

Seralune went straight to her. "Excuse me."

The woman didn't stop dragging. "Shut."

"We need somewhere to sleep tonight. We'll work for it."

"Every hostel in the quarter's been full since the new moon."

"We don't need a hostel. We need a floor."

"Then find one." She got the basket over the step and straightened, a fist in the small of her back, and took them in properly: the scarves, the road dust, Nereth's face. Her own was square and red from steam, and it gave nothing away. "I've got a floor. It's covered in sheets."

A lantern swung round the corner at the end of the lane. Two of the watch were coming, slowly, checking doors.

"I can warm water," said Nereth.

The woman's eyes went to her.

"Your coppers," Nereth said. "Before first light. Faster than wood, and it won't cost you any."

The woman glanced at the lantern. She studied Nereth for as long as it took the watchmen to pass the first two doors.

"In," she said. "Quick. Don't think it means you're staying."

The laundry was one long room full of steam.

Three great coppers stood along the back wall in a bed of brick, with iron fire-doors beneath them. Overhead, lines of sheets ran from wall to wall, so low that Seralune had to duck, and every one of them dripped. The place smelled of lye and woodsmoke, and faintly of bacon.

Two girls were folding at a table by the lamp. The elder had her mother's square face and a scowl to go with it. The younger had a sheet round her shoulders, and was far more interested in the strangers than in the folding.

"Mam," said the elder. "Who's that?"

"Nobody yet." The woman dropped the bar across the door. Outside, the watchmen's boots went past without stopping.

The younger girl was staring at Nereth. "What happened to your face?"

"Bett," said her mother.

"I only asked."

"We don't." The woman turned to Nereth. "Go on, then."

Nereth went to the nearest copper. Its fire had burned down to ash, and the water in it had gone lukewarm and grey.

Seralune caught her sleeve.

Nereth hadn't called her fire since the tunnels under the palace. The last time, it had come by itself, orange with a white heart, and Seralune had closed her hand round Nereth's wrist and told her never to call it again.

"It's washing water," Nereth said, very low. "It's the one thing anybody ever hired me for."

"And if it —"

"Then count."

Seralune let go.

Nereth pushed back her right sleeve, and only her right, and laid both hands flat on the rim of the copper.

Seralune counted. One breath first, as Thaer had taught her. One. The steam on her face. Two. A drip from the sheets onto her hood. Three. Nereth's back, straight and square over the work.

The water shivered.

Beads rose from the bottom, small and bright, then bigger ones. Then the whole copper rolled over on itself and began to steam.

Under Nereth's palms, a flame lay pressed along the rim, no wider than a finger. It was orange. At its heart, where it touched the metal, it was white.

"It's white in the middle," said a voice, low down, from the end of the coppers.

Bett giggled. Her sister hissed at her. Their mother shut her eyes, like someone counting to ten.

Nereth took her hands away, and the flame went with them. In the copper, the water went on rolling.

The woman went to it, pushed up her own sleeve and put her elbow in. She held it there longer than Seralune would have thought anyone could bear.

"By the Four," she said.

"That's quicker than Da ever did it," said Bett.

"Nobody asked you." The woman pulled her arm out, red to the elbow, and shook it off. "All three," she said to Nereth. "Before first light, every morning you're under my roof. You get the corner of the loft and your supper, and not a crust more. That's the bargain."

"That's very generous," said Seralune. "Thank you."

The elder girl snorted.

"Generous." The woman turned to Seralune. "Hark at her. I'm not generous. I'm short of wood."

"She means thank you," Nereth said.

"I know what she means. I've got ears." The woman dried her arm on her apron. "Agna. That's Tilde, and that's Bett, and you've heard plenty out of both of them. You?"

"Sera."

Nereth hesitated. "Nereth."

Agna nodded, as though the names were a price and she'd heard worse. Then she went to the end of the row of coppers, bent down, and knocked twice on a little iron door set low in the brick.

Two knocks.

Seralune's whole body turned towards the sound before she could stop it. For one breath she wasn't in a laundry. She was sitting in a pale room with her hands in her lap, listening to footsteps stop outside the door.

The iron door swung open, and a boy crawled out.

He was eleven or twelve, all elbows, with soot on his face, cobwebs in his hair and a wide grin. He stood up and dusted himself down, which made no difference, and went straight past his mother to Nereth.

"Do it again," he said.

"Kit," said Agna.

"She could do it again. Couldn't you? Can you teach me?"

Nereth considered him. "Can you make fire?"

"No." It didn't seem to trouble him. "Water. Three drops in a cup, on a good day. Mam says when they test me they'll call me Faint and put me somewhere, so they're not going to test me."

"Kit."

"What? They've seen me now."

"So much for keeping him quiet," said Tilde.

"Hold your tongue." Agna turned to the two women. She didn't raise her voice. She didn't need to.

"I don't ask what's under anybody's scarf," she said. "That's why people come to me. You didn't see my boy. That's the rest of the bargain."

"We didn't see anyone," said Nereth.

Kit was grinning at Seralune. She glanced round the room, politely puzzled.

"See whom?" she said.

Tilde laughed before she could stop herself. Even Agna's mouth moved at one corner, and then didn't.

Supper was barley and bacon from an iron pot kept warm at the back of the fire. Agna put two bowls in front of them without ceremony. "It's barley," she said. "It's always barley." Then she went back to her sheets.

Seralune ate the first bowl too fast to taste it. She'd been hungry before, in the tunnels and on the road. She hadn't known hunger could make her hands shake over a spoon.

When the bowl was empty, she considered it. Then, because Agna's back was turned, she ran her finger round the inside.

"Sera," said Nereth.

"I'm hungry."

"I can see that. So can he."

Kit was watching from the table with his chin on his fists, enchanted. Seralune took her finger out of the bowl. Nereth picked up her own, without a word, and did exactly the same.

"Do you want to see the best thing in Favale?" Kit said.

"They want to sleep," said Agna, without turning round.

"They want to see it." He was already at the ladder. "It's only the roof. It's where I go. Mam knows."

"Mam wishes she didn't," said Agna. "Don't fall off."

The ladder went up through the loft, where the sheets hung so close together that it was like climbing through cloud, and out through a square hatch onto the roof.

The roof was flat and still warm from the day, with a low wall round it. Kit scrambled out ahead of them, crouching from habit, and went to the wall.

"There," he said.

Seralune came to stand beside him.

Favale lay below them in the dark, and it was full of light.

"Oh," said Nereth, very quietly.

Kit looked delighted. "Told you."

The lanterns ran along the river road in one long gold line. They branched off it up other roads and crossed the city in threads and ribbons, until the streets she'd walked through as dust and crush and noise were drawn out in gold, as neat as the lines on Neldor's map.

Beyond them, the river was black, with the lanterns lying on it in pieces.

The hill stood in the middle of everything. A stair climbed its side, lit from bottom to top by lamps, a line of sparks going up into the dark. The temple's roofs above it were only shapes now.

Kit pointed. "That's the river road. All the gold ones are only practice. On the Longest Light they light every one in the city, and the temple, and the stair, and it burns all night." He pointed again. "That's the Pilgrims' Gate. That's the western market. That dark bit's the old palace. That's the aqueduct, and that's where they —"

"Which one's the temple?" said Nereth.

Kit gave her a look of enormous pity. "It's the hill."

"Is it?"

"You can't miss the temple. It's the biggest thing there is."

Seralune laughed. It was small, and it surprised her.

Kit told them everything.

"The watch come down our lane twice after the bell. Two of them, with a lantern. Then they go round by the market and come back." He traced the way with a sooty finger. "It takes them eight hundred to come round. I've counted."

"Eight hundred," said Seralune.

"Eight hundred and something. Longer if the fat one's on." He hummed a low note, then a higher one. "That's the morning bell. That one's midday. They're all different. I know every one."

"What happens on the Longest Light?" said Nereth.

"The saint comes down the stair. Right down, among the sick. This year she's going to walk the whole of it, everybody says, and anyone her shadow touches will get up and follow her down."

"Have you seen her?" Seralune asked.

"Every morning. She comes out at the top." He considered. "She's very small. I think she's small. It's far."

"Have you been to the temple?"

"No." He said it just as he'd said it about the fire. "I've been to the end of the lane. Twice."

"What's at the end of the lane?" said Nereth.

Kit thought about it. "Another lane."

From below, his mother's voice came up through the hatch. "Kit."

He sighed like a much older person, and went. At the hatch he turned. "Don't fall off," he told them, in his mother's voice exactly, and was gone.

Then they were alone.

Nereth sat down with her back against the low wall. After a while, Seralune sat down too, not quite beside her. Across the lane, behind shutters, pilgrims had started singing again, slow and sleepy.

"You never told me," Seralune said.

"Told you what?"

"That you could do that. The water."

Nereth glanced at her sideways. "You never asked what I did before you."

It was true. Seralune had woken in a pale room with a stranger by the bed in the grey and green of a royal attendant, and she'd never once wondered what that stranger had been doing the morning before.

"What did you do?"

"Laundry. Eight years. Mostly the coppers." Nereth tipped her head back against the wall. "I was good at it. I did forty coppers before the morning bell once, for the spring linens. Maerith said nobody had done forty in all the time she'd run the laundry, and she'd run it for three hundred years."

"Who's Maerith?"

"She ran the palace laundry. She had a voice like a cart going over a bridge." The corner of Nereth's mouth twitched. "Your brother walked through her drying yard once."

Seralune turned her head. "Thaer never went near a drying yard in his life."

"He did once. He was late for council, and he took the short way, and it was a washing day." Nereth held up her hands and drew the lines in the air, row behind row. "He went straight through the middle. Didn't slow down. Didn't duck. He came out the other side with a pillowcase on his head."

Seralune stared at her.

"Nobody laughed," Nereth said. "Nobody breathed. There were twelve of us in that yard, holding our pegs. He stopped. He took it off. And then he folded it, Sera." Her hands made the folds, one and then another, precise. "Corners and all. Properly. Then he handed it to Maerith and walked on."

Seralune laughed.

It came out of her all at once, and this time it didn't stop.

She could see him. Grave as a judge, with a wet pillowcase over his face, and then his long careful hands doing the corners, because he couldn't bear to hand anyone something crumpled. She laughed until she had to hold her ribs.

"She kept it," Nereth said. "Maerith. She never washed it again. She kept it in a drawer and took it out to show the new girls. 'A prince folded that.'"

Seralune bent over her knees. It went on and on. Her eyes ran. It filled her up under the ribs, bright and enormous, and kept on filling —

She stopped.

She pressed her hand over her mouth and held it there until the laugh went down. It hurt a little.

Behind the laughing, where she hadn't been looking, Thaer was waiting for her: his hand held out in a dark passage, and his voice asking her to come back. Missing him came in right behind the laugh, and she wasn't ready for it.

She counted. One. Two. Three.

When she took her hand away, Nereth was watching her.

"You stopped," Nereth said.

"I had to."

"Did you?"

"I don't know what it does. Laughing. I don't know if it's the same. I didn't want to find out on you."

Nereth didn't answer. Her eyes had gone to Seralune's hand, lying on the warm roof between them.

Then she reached out and took it.

She did it on purpose. Seralune felt the choice in it. Nereth's fingers came round hers and closed and held on, and all the while she kept her gaze on the lanterns, as though she didn't trust what her face would do.

"Nereth —"

"If it burns, it burns."

"It might."

"Then it might." Nereth's voice wasn't steady. "Two days, Sera. I've been careful for two days. I'm tired."

"So am I," said Seralune, and held on.

Nothing burned. They sat with their backs to the wall and their hands together on the roof, and watched Favale shine, and across the lane the hymn wound down and stopped.

It came without any warning.

A line of heat drew across her right palm, quick and thin, like a thread pulled through the skin.

And her mana went.

All her life it had filled her with nowhere to go. Now, for one heartbeat, it knew exactly where it was going.

It poured out of her in a single direction, over the roofs and the lanes and the gold lines of the lanterns, towards the long dark shape of the aqueduct.

It didn't go far. There was no sea in the way this time. It went somewhere close, a street away or ten, and at the other end of it, something took hold.

Then it was gone.

Her mana was a sea again, with no shore anywhere. Her palm was only her palm. When she turned it up to the light, the faint line of an old cut was the only mark on it.

Nereth's hand was still in her other one.

Seralune went cold. "Nereth."

"I'm here."

"Did it —"

"No." Nereth was touching her own neck with her free hand, under the scarf, along the scars. She took her fingers away and checked them. "Nothing. It didn't touch me. What was that?"

"I don't know."

"It was the same. Wasn't it? As on the ship."

On the ship it had gone through Nereth like a hot wire. Seralune remembered the flinch, and Nereth's hand flying up to cover the lines.

"The same," she said. "Only it didn't hurt you."

"Are you all right?"

"I don't know."

She got up and went to the wall. Out over the roofs, the arches of the aqueduct marched away into the dark above the lanterns. Somewhere over there.

She could have walked to it. She could have gone down the ladder and out of the door, and followed the empty gold roads until she found whatever had reached back.

Below her, two lanterns were coming along the lane, slowly, in step.

"Sera," Nereth said. "Curfew."

Seralune watched the lanterns go by. She stayed at the wall a while after they'd gone. Then she came back and sat down.

Nereth held out her hand again. Seralune took it, and neither of them let go first.

---

Nereth was gone from beside her when she woke.

Below the loft, an iron door clanged and Agna snapped a word. Under it ran a low, steady roar: a great deal of water coming to the boil.

Seralune lay among the sheets and listened. Nereth was down there doing it on her own, and nothing was going wrong.

When she climbed out through the hatch, the city was grey.

It wasn't dawn yet. The lanterns had burned out in the night, and the roads were only roads again. But Favale was awake.

Out of every lane and doorway she could see, small lights were coming.

All of them went the same way. Hundreds of lamps moved uphill through the dark streets, joining and thickening, pouring towards the foot of the stair like water running the wrong way.

Other lanterns were moving too, in pairs, fast, and not uphill. She watched two of them stop at a door below the aqueduct. Then another pair. Then four together.

"That's not right," said Kit.

He was sitting against the low wall with his knees under his chin. She hadn't seen him there.

"They don't knock at dawn," he said. "They never knock at dawn."

"Perhaps someone's ill."

Kit didn't answer. He was watching the lanterns hard, counting under his breath.

Then Seralune looked at the hill, and forgot the lanterns.

In the grey light she could see the land properly for the first time.

The river came down across the plain and swung round the foot of the hill in one long brown bend. The hill stood up out of the flat country on its own, the only one for miles.

She knew it.

She knew it as she knew the stair to her own rooms. The height of it. The steep side, and the long side. How the river leaned against its foot and then turned away.

She'd stood somewhere high, a long time ago, and looked at this hill, and there'd been something on it.

Not the temple. The temple was wrong. It sat on that hill like a crown on the wrong head.

She reached for what ought to have been there instead, and found nothing. Not a blur, not a colour. Only the same smooth gap she'd found in herself on the ship, the night she'd remembered a young man's hand and not his name.

The sky behind the hill went from grey to white.

"There," said Kit, very softly. "There she is."

Someone had come out at the top of the stair.

It was a person in white, very small at that distance.

The sun was rising directly behind the temple, and it caught her edges, so that she stood there burning round the rim with the whole western city lying in her shadow.

Below her, in rows, lay the sick. In the streets and on the roofs, people had stopped moving.

The Ruler of Light.

The word caught on Seralune again, as it had by the fire on the pilgrim road. A sleeve on a nail. She knew it. She didn't know from where.

The small white figure looked out over the city. Then she turned and went back in.

"She's small," said Kit, satisfied. "I said."

Seralune gazed down at the stair, at the rows of the sick in the long shadow, and saw Nereth lying there among them, with the dark lines fading from her face like ink from wet paper.

Below her, through the hatch, the coppers roared.
