# Blood Upon Empty Hands

Knuckles struck the yard door a little after the third bell of the night.

Once. Then twice, quickly.

Alaric looked up from the empty inn.

The fire had burned low while he waited. Nearly an hour ago, Marta had hidden Wena under the canvas of her provision cart and taken her out through the yard, along with the charcoal coat and the bloodstained black clothes he'd arrived in, wrapped up beneath the stores. All that was left of the dog now were the towels she'd slept on and a few pale hairs caught between the floorboards.

Wena had looked back once before Marta dropped the canvas. Alaric had watched the cart roll out past the stable wall and hadn't followed.

Gerolt had told him to take her. And he'd let the only living part of the old farmer leave without him.

The latch lifted.

Silas came in with the night still clinging to his cloak. One shoulder was damp, though the windows were dry. He shut the door carefully, drew the bolt, and dropped a bundle of coarse grey cloth on the nearest table.

"She's under the fort."

Alaric pushed himself up. Sleep had stiffened every place Marta had bandaged. His ankle fought the first step. Pain climbed from his heel into his ribs and settled under his right shoulder before he'd reached the table.

"You saw her?"

"I saw her name on a transfer order from the lower cells." Silas pulled off his gloves with his teeth. "They move her at dawn. Not through a gate. They've got an airship tied up over Fort Kelmend."

"An airship?"

Silas looked at him.

Alaric waited for the word to mean something. It didn't.

"A ship held up in the air by a Wind core," Silas said. "You can gawp at it once we've stopped it carrying her somewhere we can't follow."

He opened the bundle. A shapeless tunic lay inside with a pair of trousers patched at both knees. Neither had seen washing in a long time. An old stain darkened one sleeve, and the whole lot smelled of flour, sweat and smoke.

"Most of the fort's useful men are out searching grain sheds for you," Silas went on. "The rest are getting the transfer ready. Faint labourers are still going in through the service yard — food, lamp oil, waste. Things soldiers need and would rather not think about."

Alaric touched the tunic. "How do we get under the fort?"

Silas drew a narrow knife from inside his cloak and tested its edge on his thumb. "Through the service yard. I'll be wearing a guard's armour. You'll be carrying something heavy and looking grateful for the privilege. Once we're in, there's a stair down behind the kitchens."

"And Darcy?"

"Somewhere below that."

Alaric looked up. "You don't know where."

"I had one hour, a fort full of soldiers, and no wish to spend the rest of my life underneath it." Silas kept his eyes on the blade. "I found a way in. Don't mistake it for a plan."

The inn felt bigger without Marta's voice in it. There was nothing left on the long table but two guttering lamps, Gerolt's token, and the rough clothes Silas expected him to wear.

"How many guards?"

"I counted six between the service yard and the stair. There'll be ones I missed. And you can't fight any of them."

"And if we can't reach her?"

"We leave."

No thought in it at all. Alaric's fingers closed on the patched cloth.

"Then she'll never hear Marta's offer."

"She'll hear nothing at all if we die over her head." Silas slid the knife away. "Marta asked you to talk to her. If the way closes before we get there, we leave."

Alaric looked at the yard door.

Marta had promised him a way out of Kelmend if he said no. By now she and Wena would be well past the streets the patrols were watching. He could go to the broken well and be gone before daylight put his face in front of every guard in town. She'd offered him that road without a trace of contempt. Taking it wouldn't break their bargain.

Gerolt's token lay on the table, the broken shield face up.

He picked it up and pushed it into the deepest pocket at his hip. Its edge pressed cold against him.

"Tell me how to walk."

Silas studied him.

"Eyes down. Speak only if someone speaks to you, and even then, try not to sound like you expect an answer. Limp less than you need to. Men notice a damaged tool when they think they might end up doing its work."

He pushed the grey clothes across the table.

Alaric pulled them on over the clean shirt and trousers Marta had left him. The patched trousers snagged on his wrapped ankle, and dragging the tunic over his swollen shoulder stopped his breath until the cloth settled.

Silas wound dirty strips around his hands and ankle until hardly any of Marta's clean white showed.

"And if you see something that offends you," he said, tying off the last one, "remember we're not going in there to improve the place."

Alaric met his eyes.

Silas kept his hand on the knot at Alaric's wrist. "I mean it. Walking past what you'll see will be hard enough. Don't stop."

---

Fort Kelmend rose out of the northern quarter like something the town had tried and failed to bury.

The houses round it crouched under its walls. A square tower stood at each corner, the high windows lit by pale blue-green fire. Between them, the central keep climbed above the rooftops and vanished under a shape bigger than any ship Alaric could have imagined.

Its hull hung against the paling sky.

There was no water under it. Thick cables dropped from either side into iron drums on the roof. Long ribs curved beneath its dark belly, and a glass-veined pipe ran into the lower hull near the stern, blue-green light moving through it in slow waves.

With every wave, the airship lifted against its chains.

The cables answered.

**KRRRNNNK.**

The sound came down through the fort, along the street, and into Alaric's teeth. He stopped in the shadow of a shuttered dye shop.

"What's making that?"

"The Wind core's still charging." Silas kept walking.

Another wave went into the hull. **KRRRNNNK.** Metal groaned overhead.

Below it, labourers waited in a line along the fort's eastern wall, all in the same shapeless grey-brown as Alaric's disguise. Some held baskets. Others balanced covered pots on their shoulders or dragged carts of split wood. Soldiers went in through the main arch without slowing down. The labourers were sent round the side, where rain and refuse had churned the ground to mud.

Silas steered him into an alley across from the service yard.

"Wait here."

"Where are you going?"

Silas turned his collar up over the scar on his face. "To find a guard whose armour fits."

He vanished behind a row of coal sheds.

Alaric stood beside a stack of empty barrels and counted labourers, because it was better than counting heartbeats.

Seventeen went in while he waited. Not one was asked their name.

A guard tapped a woman carrying two pails on the shoulder and turned her round without a word. He lifted one lid, sniffed, and waved her on. His eyes never once reached her face.

Then five men came down out of a narrow door further along the wall.

They wore leather harnesses across their chests. Fine copper threads ran from the straps to metal rings clamped round both wrists, and blue-green light was still trapped under the skin of their hands, fading with every step.

The first walked on his own. The second leaned on the wall. The other two carried the last one between them, his boots dragging through the mud, his head hanging, a thin line of dried blood under one nostril.

"Put him by the charge-room door," a soldier called from under the arch. "They might need him again before lift."

The men carrying him stopped. "He's given all he had," one said.

"Then let him find more."

The soldier went back to the cup warming his hands.

They laid the unconscious man against the wall, and the line moved round him.

Alaric was still staring when a hand closed on his shoulder. He spun round much too fast.

Silas stood behind him in a Mydean guard's armour and gloves. The breastplate was tight across his chest, and the sleeves were short enough to show the leather underneath. A round helmet shadowed his brow. It didn't quite hide the pale scar on his cheek.

"If I'd been a guard, you'd have shouted."

"Where's the guard?"

"Behind the sheds. Alive."

He took Alaric round to see. A man lay between the back wall and a heap of broken crates, hands tied behind him with his own belt, a wad of cloth strapped into his mouth. His eyes were open. They went wide when Silas came back.

Silas crouched and tightened the knot at his wrists. He took a small ring of keys from the man's belt and hooked it inside the borrowed breastplate. Then he checked the gag and glanced up at Alaric.

"The next watch will find him."

The guard made a wet sound behind the cloth.

Silas picked up a wooden crate full of empty oil flasks and shoved it into Alaric's arms.

Alaric looked at the man on the ground. "You could have killed him."

Silas settled the stolen sword on his hip. "I'd no reason to."

It wasn't mercy. But the living man was.

"This armour buys us minutes," Silas said. "Don't waste them."

---

The crate hid Alaric's hands. It didn't hide his limp.

He kept his steps short as Silas led him to the service door. The guard beside it looked at the stolen armour first. His eyes lingered on the breastplate where it strained across Silas's shoulders.

"You're late."

Silas cuffed the back of Alaric's crate with one gloved hand. The blow went straight through the wood into his bruised ribs.

"This one fell over twice between the storehouse and Bell Street."

The guard glanced at Alaric. Not his face. His wrapped ankle.

"Replace him."

"With which of our many volunteers?"

The guard's mouth twisted. He reached out and shoved one corner of the crate up without warning, and the wood rammed into Alaric's shoulder. White pain flashed across his sight. He caught the load before it fell.

The guard had already turned back to Silas. "Take him below when he's done. Captain wants the lower passage clear before the elf comes down."

"Which elf?"

The question sharpened the man's attention. Silas yawned inside his helmet.

"I've been searching wet sheds since midnight. If Natharul's grown another pointy-eared bastard, I'd like some warning before I salute the wrong one."

The guard gave a tired laugh. "Only the one. More than enough."

He waved them through.

The fort swallowed Alaric without ever once looking at him.

Heat met them first — old food, lamp oil, wet stone and metal held too long near a flame. The service passage squeezed between storerooms before opening out under a stair crowded with labourers. More copper lines ran across the ceiling, the blue-green light brightening, climbing and fading. The stone under his shoes began to hum. At first it was too low to hear. He felt it in his bad ankle and the roots of his teeth, a steady pressure underneath every other sound.

Above them, the airship strained at its chains. **KRRRNNNK.**

Nobody looked up.

Two workers came down the stairs with empty wrist harnesses hanging from their hands. A woman followed them on shaking legs. The blue-green veins under her skin stopped at the wrists, where the metal rings had rubbed both arms raw.

She missed the last step.

The man behind her caught her elbow before she hit the floor.

"Keep the line moving," a soldier said.

The man lifted her. The soldier watched until they'd gone on, then went back to his conversation.

"Coremaster says we'll be full before dawn."

"He said that an hour ago."

"Then send another row up."

They talked about the bodies as if the bodies were somewhere else.

Silas laid a hand on Alaric's back and moved him on. "Eyes."

Alaric lowered them. The floor showed him enough. Bare feet under grey hems. Mud dried round thin ankles. A dropped crust of bread crushed flat under a soldier's boot, because no worker dared pick it up while he stood there.

At the end of the passage, an armoured shoulder slammed into Alaric and turned him half round.

"Watch it."

He tightened his grip on the crate. "Sorry."

The soldier stopped. Silas didn't.

"He apologised to me," the man called after him.

Silas glanced back from the shadow of his helmet. "He apologises to doors. It means nothing."

The soldier looked at Alaric again, found nothing worth chasing, and went on.

Only round the next corner did Silas slow down.

"You sound educated."

"I said one word."

"Like someone who expected to be listened to." Silas kept his voice low. "Do it again and I'll have to treat you the way they expect."

Alaric could feel the stolen sword at Silas's side without looking at it. He put his head down and followed.

---

They found the kitchens by the smell.

Steam clouded the first doorway. Beyond it, knives hit boards too fast to count. Servants carried trays along the next passage to a low eating room, where three soldiers were still sitting over the remains of the changing watch.

Silas walked past the entrance. Alaric almost did.

A boy came out of the kitchen with a shallow pot held against his chest.

He was a child. His tunic was too big across the shoulders and too short at the wrists, patched with scraps that didn't match. An old bruise darkened one side of his jaw. The pot shook in his hands.

One of the seated soldiers hooked a foot round the leg of the chair beside him and dragged it into the boy's path.

The boy walked into it.

Broth slopped over the rim. Most fell back in. A thin stream ran across the soldier's boot and dripped steaming onto the floor.

The whole room went quiet around the mistake.

The soldier looked down at his boot. Then he smiled.

"You little shit."

The boy set the pot on the nearest table before it could fall. "Forgive me, sir."

"You think I wear these so Faint trash can tip supper over them?"

"No, sir."

"Then why's it there?"

The boy's eyes went to the chair the soldier had moved. He didn't answer.

The first blow caught him across the mouth. It turned him right round. One hand found the table, and the soldier kicked his wrist away, and he went down beside the spilled broth.

The second man laughed, leaned over the back of his chair and spat on the boy's shoulder.

The third kept eating.

The boy started to get up. A boot drove into his stomach and folded him round it.

"Fucking trash," the soldier said. The smile had gone. "Always ruining what stronger hands put in front of you."

He kicked again.

Alaric stopped walking.

Silas was already on the far side of the doorway. "Walk," he said without turning.

The boy covered his head. The next kick caught his forearm.

All around them, the servants kept moving. A woman crossed the passage with a basket clutched so tight the weave bent under her fingers. In the kitchen, a cook lowered his eyes and went on cutting the same piece of bread into smaller and smaller pieces.

Alaric set the crate down on the floor.

"Alaric."

Silas said his name softly. The warning in it wasn't soft at all.

Alaric walked into the room.

"Get away from him."

The soldier's boot came down. For one breath, pure surprise held his face. Then he laughed.

"What did you say?"

Alaric looked at the boy between the man's boots. Blood had opened across his bottom lip. His eyes stayed fixed on the floor.

"I said leave him alone."

The second soldier pushed back from the table. "Whose Faint is this?"

Both men looked at Silas.

The third was still holding his spoon, but it had stopped halfway to his mouth.

Silas came in slowly.

"Mine," he said.

"Then teach him his place."

Silas's eyes passed over the child and rested, briefly, on the boot beside the boy's ribs.

"Move away from the boy."

The first soldier's smile changed. "You new here?"

His hand dropped towards the horn at his belt.

Silas crossed the room before it got there.

The short blade came out of the borrowed armour and went in under the soldier's chin, the point vanishing behind his jaw. The man's teeth clacked together. Silas dragged him forward, using the body to block the second man's view, then tore the blade free and shoved him across the table.

Wood split under his weight.

The second guard went for his sword. Alaric saw the movement start. Silas had already seen it finish.

Fire closed round his empty hand, and he caught the guard's wrist before the blade had cleared its sheath. The smell of burning leather filled the room. The guard screamed. His fingers sprang open. Silas twisted the arm, drove a shoulder into his chest and cracked his head against the stone wall.

Once.

The man went slack. Silas lowered him to the floor and pressed two fingers under his jaw.

"Is he alive?" Alaric asked.

"For now."

The third soldier was standing at the far side of the room. He hadn't drawn anything. His eyes went from the dead man across the table, to Silas, to the open passage.

Silas raised the bloody knife.

"Choose."

The soldier looked at the boy on the floor, and whatever he saw there made his choice for him. He lifted both hands.

"I saw nothing."

"In the pantry. Slowly."

The man went. Silas dragged the unconscious one in after him, shut the door and jammed a serving knife through the outer latch.

The first fist hit the wood before he'd turned round.

The kitchen beyond had gone completely silent. The cook still held his knife over the ruined bread. He looked at the dead soldier, then began quietly moving the other servants towards the back passage. Nobody reached for an alarm.

Whether they were helping, or simply refusing to die for men who beat children, Alaric couldn't tell.

"How long will that hold?" he asked.

"Less time than we need."

Silas wiped his blade on the dead man's sleeve, took him by the collar and dragged him behind the broken table. One arm still showed under the splintered wood.

The boy had pressed himself back against the wall. He was staring at Alaric, and there was no gratitude anywhere in his face. Only terror.

Alaric crouched in front of him. Pain went through his ankle. He stayed down.

"Can you stand?"

The boy's eyes went to the grey clothes. "You're not Faint."

It wasn't a question.

Alaric glanced at the arm under the table. "I need to find a woman they're moving at dawn."

The boy's face closed. "I don't know anyone."

"Water Faint," Silas said, and nodded at the lower passage. "Kept downstairs. Draws maps for the captain."

Recognition got to the boy's face before he could hide it. Silas saw.

"Where?"

The boy looked at the dead soldier again. "If they find him here, they'll say I did something."

"If they find you next to him, they'll blame you whether you talk or not," Silas said. "Take the broth upstairs. Anyone asks, these men sent you to the charge room. Stay there till the bells change."

The boy looked at Alaric.

Alaric wished he had something safer to give him. All he had was Silas's instruction, and a room that already smelled of blood.

"Which way?" he asked gently.

The boy pressed a sleeve to his mouth. "Past the salt store. There's a stair behind the ash door. The lower guards use it when they want food without going through the officers' hall."

Silas frowned at the passage they'd meant to take. "That stair was bricked up."

"The first one." The boy's voice shook. "There's two."

He picked up the pot again. His hands trembled so badly the broth lapped against the rim.

Alaric reached out to steady it.

The boy flinched away from him. "Don't."

Alaric lowered his hands.

The boy went out through the back of the kitchen and didn't look round.

Silas watched him go. Then he rounded on Alaric.

"You were told to walk."

Alaric stood up carefully. "They were going to kill him."

"Maybe." Silas looked at the arm sticking out from under the table. "Now a man's dead, two more are one open door away from raising the alarm, and that boy gets questioned if they catch him."

Anger rose in Alaric and then faltered, staring at the blood running from the dead soldier's jaw.

Silas stepped closer.

"You wanted your choice to matter. Look at all of it."

Alaric looked.

The body. The open door. The broth going cold on the floor where the boy had fallen.

Silas picked up the crate and shoved it back into his arms.

"Now make the time it cost us matter."

---

The second ash door opened onto a stair barely wide enough for Silas's stolen armour.

They went down under the kitchens. The heat fell away one step at a time. The Wind core's hum stayed inside the walls, but the kitchen noise thinned until Alaric could hear only their feet and his own uneven breathing. The crate kept knocking against the stone whenever his bad shoulder dropped. Silas took it off him without asking and left it under the first landing.

"You said carrying something was the disguise."

"Down here, it's just slowing you down."

At the bottom, a narrow service corridor ran under the central keep. Caged lamps lined one wall, most turned low. The other wall was all shut store doors and gaps where older passages disappeared into the dark.

Silas stopped at the corner. Voices were coming.

He pulled Alaric into one of the gaps and pressed them both flat against the cold stone.

A guard went past close enough for the edge of his cloak to brush the opening. Lamp in one hand. Alarm horn at his belt. Scratching under his breastplate with the other hand.

Silas waited until the footsteps reached the stair.

Then they stopped.

"Harl?"

The name came into the dark. Silas didn't move.

The guard took one step back. Then another. His lamp found the stolen helmet first. The light crossed the cheek beneath it and caught the pale scar.

His face changed.

"Silas?"

Silas knocked the lamp out of his hand. Glass shattered and the dark came down, and the horn was already rising to the man's lips.

Alaric grabbed his wrist with both hands.

The guard twisted and drove an elbow into Alaric's shoulder. Pain broke his grip. His back hit the wall, then the floor, and the breath went out of him before it could become a cry.

Silas seized the horn. The guard dragged his sword half out. Silas trapped it in its sheath with one hand and slammed his forehead into the man's face. Metal hit bone. The guard staggered and didn't fall. He let the sword go, pulled a dagger from behind his belt and drove it at the gap under Silas's breastplate.

Silas caught his wrist. Both men crashed into the opposite wall. The borrowed helmet fell off and rolled through the spilled lamp oil. The dagger came down, and Silas held it above his own ribs with both hands while the guard leaned his whole weight behind the point.

Alaric reached for the nearest leg. His fingers closed on leather, and he pulled.

The guard's boot slid in the oil. His body swung away from Silas and came down on top of Alaric.

An arm crashed across his throat. The stone drove into his bruised shoulder. Something clattered onto the floor right beside his ear.

The dagger.

The guard saw it. So did Alaric. They both reached.

Alaric's wrapped fingers got to the hilt first.

The guard caught his wrist.

His face hung inches above Alaric's. Blood was running freely from his nose, over his lips, gathering on his jaw. He smelled of lamp oil and old sweat. He was heavier. Stronger. He began forcing Alaric's wrist down towards the floor.

Past him, Silas dragged in a breath and tried to get up.

The forearm on Alaric's throat pressed harder. Dark gathered at the edges of his sight.

He twisted the dagger towards the only space left between their bodies.

The point touched cloth. Stopped.

He pushed.

Resistance came back up the hilt into both his hands. The cloth gave first. Something under it didn't. His arms shook. The guard's eyes went wide — not with pain yet, but with understanding of what Alaric was doing.

Alaric pushed again.

The blade went in.

The guard's breath burst out against his face. His grip clamped painfully round Alaric's wrist, and every part of him went rigid.

Then the weight on Alaric's throat eased. Not all the way.

The guard looked down between them. His hand found the dagger buried under his ribs and closed round Alaric's fingers instead of the wound.

"Wait," Alaric whispered.

He didn't know which of them he was asking.

The man tried to breathe. A wet sound came up into his chest and stayed there.

Blood reached Alaric's hands. It soaked through the dirty strips and into Marta's clean bandages underneath, warm where the river and the battlefield had both been cold.

The smell came back first. Mud against his cheek. A dead man's arm across his ribs. Blood that had belonged to someone he couldn't name.

This blood had a face.

It was looking at him.

The guard's mouth moved. Nothing came out but another broken breath. His fingers weakened round Alaric's hand, tightened once more, and let go.

Alaric held the dagger where it was. Pulling it out felt like doing it all over again. Leaving it felt worse.

Silas pulled the body off him, and the blade came with it. Blood splashed across Alaric's grey tunic. The guard rolled onto his side and drew his knees up round the wound, one hand pressed uselessly over it.

Alaric reached for him.

Silas caught his arm. "He's still breathing."

"Not for long."

Alaric tried to pull free. Silas's grip tightened.

"You saved me." There was no gratitude in his voice and no comfort, either. "Now move."

The guard drew another wet breath.

Alaric's body wouldn't get up. Silas hauled him to his feet. Pain hit his ankle, his ribs and his shoulder all at once, and his knees went, but the wall held him until his legs remembered what they were for.

Silas dragged the dying man into the dark opening and picked up the horn.

Alaric stayed in the corridor. Blood darkened the wrappings on both his hands.

He had woken up under another man's blood once, with no idea how it got there.

This time, he knew.

Silas put the dagger back on the dead man's belt. "Can you walk?"

Alaric looked into the opening. The guard wasn't moving anymore.

"Alaric."

He made one foot go forward.

Silas picked up the fallen helmet and pulled it back on. "Good enough."

They went on under the fort.

---

Voices reached them past the next stair.

Silas slowed and raised a hand. The passage opened into a service gallery overlooking part of the command level. Light fell across the floor from a half-open door, and inside, someone was turning paper.

"The transfer goes ahead at dawn," a man said. "The core will be ready."

His voice was controlled and unhurried — the voice of a man who had never once needed to repeat himself.

Another voice answered, precise, every word cut cleanly off from the next. Alaric had last heard that way of speaking under Gerolt's broken roof.

Natharul.

"Captain Gilmot," the elf said, "Natharul was promised a strategist prepared for service. Not a disobedient servant to be carried aboard."

Every part of Alaric went still except his breathing.

Silas's fingers closed over his bloody wrist and drew him lower against the wall.

Through the gap, Alaric could see a long table covered in maps. A man in a dark Mydean officer's coat stood at the head of it, gold across his collar and shoulders. One hand rested near a candle whose flame leaned towards him, though no draught came into the room.

Across from him stood a slender figure in Natharul armour. Alaric could see only one pale hand, the tip of a pointed ear, and gold lines moving over dark metal. The elf didn't stand like a guest.

"The strategist has refused three frontier revisions," the elf said. "And if she refuses to board?"

Gilmot straightened one corner of the transfer order. "Darcy understands the provision attached to her stewardship."

"Fear becomes unreliable when the life threatened belongs to the one making the choice."

"The lives threatened aren't hers."

The candle flame climbed along Gilmot's fingers. He didn't look at it.

"If she refuses service, leaves military custody, or is removed by any other party, the Faint quarter will be entered into the record as a district complicit in the theft of state property." Paper turned under his hand. "Its adults will be suppressed. Fire companies will clear the structures before the second morning bell."

"You sail with her at dawn," the elf said. "Your order will be a day behind you by noon."

"The order stays on this wall. The garrison commander holds the copy." Gilmot turned the page back. "And I'll be standing beside her the whole way. If she's ever lost between here and the frontier, one bird brings it home."

The elf said nothing for a moment.

"Including the children?"

"There are always children."

Gilmot lowered his hand, and the flame settled with it.

"Evacuating them would weaken the corrective purpose."

The words went into Alaric more slowly than the dagger had gone into the guard.

Marta would be waiting at the edge of that quarter. Wena would be with her. A whole district lived out past Kelmend's wall, and Gilmot had put every life in it under Darcy's name before anyone had even offered her a choice.

Alaric's bloody fingers dug into the stone.

The wrapping scraped. Softly.

The elf's head turned. The pointed ear angled towards the gallery.

"Someone is outside."

Silas pulled him back from the door. They moved as the light in the room changed and a chair scraped across stone behind them. Alaric's ankle caught the first step down. Silas grabbed the back of his tunic before he fell and drove him on down into the dark.

Above them, the command-room door opened.

"Who's there?" Gilmot called.

Silas didn't answer.

The Wind core pulsed through the walls. High above, the airship strained at its chains.

**KRRRNNNK.**

The sound filled the stairwell and covered every step of their descent.

---

The lower cells began behind an iron gate.

An old gaoler sat beyond it with his chair tipped back against the wall and a ledger open on his stomach. He woke when Silas tried one of the stolen keys in the lock.

"Who —"

Silas reached through the bars, grabbed the front of his coat and pulled his head against the iron. The first blow opened the old man's hands. The second closed his eyes. Silas held two fingers under his jaw before taking the ring of keys.

"Alive?" Alaric asked.

"Breathing. Keep moving."

He lowered the gaoler behind his desk and opened the gate.

Cold waited on the other side. The corridor sloped down between rows of cells cut straight into the rock. No guards stood between the prisoners and the stair. Iron, stone and exhaustion had been judged enough.

Faces appeared behind the bars as Silas passed. A hand pulled back before the stolen armour reached it. In the next cell, a woman sat against the wall with a sleeping child tucked under her coat. When Alaric looked in, she covered the child's face. An old man crouched over a piece of bread no bigger than his palm, breaking it into portions for people Alaric couldn't see.

Nobody called out for help. Hope would have meant believing someone ever came down here for them.

Alaric stopped at the first occupied door. "We've got the keys."

Silas was comparing the numbers carved into the wall against the marks on the ring. "We came to open one door."

"We can open these."

"And then?"

Alaric looked down the corridor. "They can leave."

Silas turned to face him.

"Past the gaoler? Up through the command level? Past the kitchen where one man's dead and two more are trying to break out? Across a yard filling up with soldiers before dawn?" His voice stayed quiet. It didn't soften. "Half of them can't climb the stairs. The rest get cut down when the first horn sounds. Open those doors if you want to decide that for them."

In the nearest cell, the woman was listening without lifting her head.

Alaric's hand stayed on the bars.

He'd stopped for one child and left a dead man behind him. He'd killed another man to keep Silas alive. Neither choice had made him strong enough to open every door between these people and daylight.

"Which one's Darcy's?"

Silas held his eyes for one breath, then turned back to the wall. "End of the passage."

Alaric let go of the bars. He didn't apologise to the prisoners. He had nothing honest enough to put behind the word.

They walked on.

---

Darcy's cell had a writing desk in it. That was what set it apart from every other cage under the fort.

Maps covered one wall, pinned through corners gone soft from handling. Thin columns of figures filled every gap between them. Sealed orders waited in a wooden tray beside three broken quills. A narrow bed stood against the back wall.

The woman asleep inside had never made it that far.

She'd collapsed at the desk, one arm folded under her head, the other hanging beside the chair with her fingers barely clear of the floor. Ink stained two of them. The back of her hand was unmarked.

Higher up, where her sleeve had bunched near the elbow, burns crossed her forearm in careful, deliberate bands. Some had faded pale. Others hadn't finished healing.

Silas put the key in the lock.

The iron turned. It was a very small sound, after everything it had taken to reach it.

Darcy's eyes opened before the door did.

She lifted her head. Exhaustion had hollowed the skin under her eyes. Dark hair, threaded lightly with grey, had come loose down one side of her face. Her gaze found Silas in his stolen armour and sharpened before any confusion could get there.

"Darcy," he said.

It wasn't welcome in her face, and it wasn't only surprise. She looked at him the way Marta sometimes did, when the anger loosened just enough for an older wound to show underneath.

Then she saw Alaric.

Her eyes went over the labourer's clothes, the swollen ankle, the blood soaking both his hands. They came back to Silas. Understanding arrived before either of them could explain anything.

Silas opened the cell.

Darcy stood. One hand closed on the edge of the desk until her legs steadied. The movement pulled her sleeve higher and showed another narrow burn, disappearing above the elbow.

She looked from Silas to Alaric once more. Pity touched her face. It didn't make the warning any gentler.

"Whatever Marta promised you, it wasn't worth coming here."
