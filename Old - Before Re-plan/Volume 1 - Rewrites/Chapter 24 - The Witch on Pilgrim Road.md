# The Witch on Pilgrim Road

The man lit the fire with his bare hand.

He didn't make a show of it. He crouched over the heap of twigs and dried dung the pilgrims had gathered from the verge, cupped his palm as though something small and alive were sitting in it, and blew across it once. Flame stood up out of his skin. He tipped it into the kindling like a man pouring water from a jug, and the fire took, and a dozen tired people leaned towards it at once.

Seralune had watched fire made that way a hundred times in the palace, by servants who never looked up from the work. She had never watched anyone do it for strangers on the side of a road.

"There," the man said, shaking out his hand. "Four keep it."

"Four keep it," the pilgrims said back.

He looked across the fire at Nereth and pointed with his chin at the upwind side, where the smoke wasn't. "You sit there. You look like you walked all the way from the sea."

Nereth had. She didn't say so. She eased herself down where he'd pointed, carefully, one hand flat on the ground, and when she was settled she let out a breath Seralune had been listening for since noon.

The inland road had brought them to the pilgrim road late on their fourth afternoon out of Meren, and at first Seralune had taken it for a river. It came down out of the east between the fields, broad and brown and slow, and it moved. People filled it from verge to verge. They walked in families and in villages. They carried their sick on doors and hurdles and litters of lashed poles, on handcarts, on their backs. A boy no older than twelve had passed them with his grandmother riding on his shoulders, her feet tied together in front of his chest so she wouldn't slip. Nearly everyone wore a sun somewhere: a disc of tin at the throat, a yellow thread round the wrist, a circle chalked on a cart.

Where the two roads met there had been a shrine with a stone basin, and a priest in white and gold at the basin with a ledger. The pilgrims had drunk in the order he called them by. The Faint had drunk last. Nobody argued with him. Seralune had waited with Nereth near the back, with her scarf low over her ears and her hands folded, and when their turn came the water was warm and tasted of everyone who'd gone before.

Now the road had stopped for the night on both verges, and small fires were coming up along it for as far as she could see, like a second road laid over the first.

A girl came round the fire holding half a flat loaf in both hands, as though it might try to escape.

"Mam says you're to have this," she told Seralune.

She was seven, perhaps, with her hair cut short against lice and a sun token hanging almost to her waist on a string too long for her. Behind her, on a handcart made from a door and two mismatched wheels, a woman lay under a blanket with a cloth pressed to her mouth. When she took the cloth away to smile at them, there was blood on it.

"Thank you," Seralune said. "And thank your mother."

The girl didn't go. She stood with her weight on one foot and studied Seralune from the top of her scarf to her boots.

"You talk like a lady," she said.

"Wren," said the man at the fire, without heat.

"She does, Da."

"I've heard her. Let her eat."

Wren sat down next to Seralune anyway, close enough that her elbow bumped Seralune's arm, and watched her eat with the attention of someone who'd paid to see it.

"Do ladies have a room just for shoes?" she asked.

Seralune thought about it. "I knew a lady who had a room only for hats."

Wren's mouth fell open. "What for?"

"She had a great many hats."

"How many?"

"More than anyone needed. She wore them in the garden and never once in the rain."

Wren considered this with enormous seriousness. "That's stupid."

Seralune laughed.

It came up out of her before she knew it was there, the first laugh since Meren, and it startled her so much she put her hand over her mouth. Across the fire Nereth was watching her with an expression she couldn't read in the smoke. Then Nereth looked away, and Seralune thought she might have been smiling.

The man's name was Osric. He offered it while he fed the fire, as easily as his wife had offered the bread. He was a thatcher from the east, with a thatcher's forearms. The woman on the handcart was his wife, and she'd been coughing since the spring.

"We're taking her to Atera," he said.

To him, she might have been a physician in the next town: someone real, with a door you could knock on.

"The saint," said the woman across the fire from him. She was old, with a voice like a gate that needed oil. "The living saint. She walks the temple steps at dawn, and they lay the sick where her shadow falls. My sister's boy walked home. He'd been carried there."

"She's no older than you, they say," someone else put in, nodding at Seralune.

"The priests don't call her a saint," Osric said. "They call her the Ruler of Light."

*Ruler.*

The word caught on something in Seralune, like a sleeve on a nail. She knew it. She was nearly sure she knew it. But when she reached for where it belonged, there was only the smoke, and a step missing in the dark.

"They say she hasn't left the temple since she was four," the old woman said. "Not once. Not a step outside it."

"Poor thing," said Osric's wife from the handcart, and coughed.

The talk moved on. Somebody's harvest, somebody's feud, the price of lamp oil since the requisitions. Then someone mentioned the elves, and the fire changed.

It didn't get quieter. It got more careful. People looked at their hands. Somebody said the northern camp had taken every horse in their valley, and somebody else said at least they'd left the valley. Osric fed the fire a stick at a time, and didn't say anything for a while.

"My sister married into Harl's Ford," he said at last.

Nobody asked what had happened to Harl's Ford. They all knew.

"They wouldn't give the collectors any more. There wasn't any more to give." He broke the next stick in half with his hands, very neatly. "The collectors came back with their own soldiers."

Seralune had seen that name. On a board in Meren, with a line through it, and one word in a different hand beside it.

She said nothing. She sat with the bread in her lap and the fire on her face, and felt Nereth's eyes on her through the smoke.

Later, when the fires were low, Nereth fell asleep sitting up, and her head came down onto Seralune's shoulder. Wren was asleep across her father's knees. Osric sat with his back against the handcart, one hand on his daughter and the other reaching back over the side to rest on his wife's blanket, and he watched the fire the way a man watches something he's made.

Seralune didn't sleep. It wasn't unhappiness. It was something she had no name for, sitting among strangers who had fed her.

---

The road moved again at first light, slowly, singing.

Wren walked beside Seralune for most of the morning with a fistful of her cloak, as if Seralune were a cart she'd been told to keep up with. Osric pushed the handcart. Every so often his wife lifted a hand from under the blanket, and he took it without looking and let it go again.

Nereth walked on Seralune's other side. She'd said nothing about the pain since the fire, which meant it was bad.

"If she's real," Seralune said quietly. "Atera."

Nereth kept her eyes on the road. "If."

"Could she help you?"

"They lay the sick where her shadow falls." Nereth's mouth twisted. "I don't think they mean elves."

"They wouldn't have to know."

"Sera."

It was a warning, and a kind one. Seralune let it be. But for the rest of the morning she let herself see it anyway: white steps in the dawn, and a girl her own age at the top of them, and Nereth lying in her shadow while the dark lines faded from her face like ink from wet paper.

It was the first time since the aqueduct she'd let herself imagine Nereth well.

The riders came up the road in the middle of the morning.

The pilgrims heard them before they saw them. The hymn at the front of their part of the road broke off; heads turned; the sound came up from behind, the hard, quick rhythm of shod horses that weren't walking, and weren't going to.

"Off the road," someone said. "Off, off —"

People moved. Not fast enough. The road was too full, and the sick couldn't be hurried, and the ditch was steep on both sides. The riders came through anyway.

They wore pale armour. Seralune saw it in pieces between the heads of the crowd: a pale shoulder, a pale helm, the gleam of gold worked along an edge. There were eight of them, riding in two lines around a closed carriage drawn by greys. None of them slowed. They rode through the pilgrims as if the pilgrims were weather.

A litter went over.

It was the old woman with the voice like a gate. Her bearers had been trying to get her down into the ditch, and they hadn't been quick enough, and a horse's shoulder struck the front pole. The litter tipped and spilled her into the ditch like a bundle of washing. One of the bearers shouted. A rider leaned from the saddle and struck him across the back with the flat of his sword, not hard, the way you'd move a dog off a step.

"Leave it," the rider behind him said.

He said it in Elvish. Apart from the two of them, none of the pilgrims would have understood it.

The carriage went past close enough to touch. Its door was lacquered white, and on the white, in gold, stood the royal tree.

She didn't need to count its stars. She knew there'd be nine.

Everything came at once. The palace, and the pale corridors, and the lamps that had answered her. The lock turning on the outside of her door. Leorin calling her "Your Highness" to her face as if she were a stranger. Her brother's hand held out to her in the passage, and his voice asking her to come back.

She didn't know who was in the carriage. It didn't matter. Her father's tree was on its door, on a human road, rolling past her through people it didn't see.

Her mana rose.

It came up under her ribs and behind her eyes and into her hands, as it always did, full and patient and with nowhere to go, and it kept rising.

She counted. One breath first, her brother had always said. One. Two. The carriage passed. Three. The riders' backs, going away up the road towards Favale. Four.

It didn't hold.

Beside her, Nereth made a small sound. It wasn't a word.

Then she went down.

She fell as if someone had cut the strings. Her knees hit the road, and then the rest of her, and she lay on her side in the dirt with her whole body pulled tight as a drawn bow. Her heels scraped. Her hands clawed at nothing. Her head was forced back and back, towards her shoulder, and her mouth was open, and no sound was coming out of it.

Seralune dropped beside her.

The scars were moving.

There was no mistaking it this time. There was no lantern to blame, no tears in her eyes. In the plain morning light the black-purple lines under Nereth's eye were turning under the skin, slowly, like roots moving through a whole season in a single breath. Heat came off them. Seralune could feel it on her own face.

"Nereth. Nereth, look at me. Look at me —"

---

"Give her air."

It was Osric. He'd pushed through and knelt on Nereth's other side, and he'd already put Wren behind him with one arm.

"My uncle took fits," he said. "You have to give them air. Help me turn her."

He was so calm. Seralune could have wept at how calm he was. He got his hand under Nereth's head so it wouldn't strike the road, and with the other he pulled at the scarf wound tight round her throat, to loosen it.

The scarf came away.

It came away all at once, like a knot worked from the wrong end, and it took the hood with it, and Nereth's hair spilled out over the road, and her ear with it.

Pointed. Unmistakable. In daylight, on a human road, a hand's breadth from a man whose sister had lived in Harl's Ford.

Osric looked at it.

Seralune watched his face change. It didn't change quickly. It went through several things on its way, and she saw all of them: confusion, then understanding, then something that looked almost like grief, and then the thing underneath the grief, which had been waiting there since Harl's Ford for somewhere to go.

"Elf," he said.

He didn't shout it. He didn't need to. The people round them had been watching since Nereth fell, and it went through them like wind through standing wheat.

"Elf."

"There's an elf —"

"On the road? On this road?"

The old woman was still in the ditch. Her bearers were still trying to lift her. Somebody was still crying over the man who'd been struck with the flat of a sword. And into all of that, into the middle of a morning like that, the pilgrims found an elf on her back in the dirt.

They didn't make speeches. They made accusations. Seralune heard them from every side: villages, horses, sons. A name, again and again, that meant nothing to her and everything to the woman shouting it, and then: "My boy. My boy was in the northern camp." A stone struck the road beside Nereth's head and bounced.

Osric took Nereth by the collar with both hands and dragged her up.

Her body was still rigid. Her heels trailed. Her head lolled against his wrist, and where his fists were closed in her collar, his knuckles pressed against the scars on her neck.

"Did you do it?" he said into her face. "Were you there? Were you at Harl's Ford?"

"She's never been —" Seralune began.

Hands came at her from behind. Somebody had her hood and was pulling it, and she caught it with both hands and held it down over her ears. Someone shoved her. She went to one knee on the road.

"Let her go," she said. "Please. She's never been anywhere near —"

The fear came up then.

Not like the fear at the carriage. That had been old, the palace fear, the fear of doors. This was new and immediate and simple. They were going to kill Nereth in the road, in front of her, and there was nothing she could do with her hands or her voice to stop it.

Her mana answered.

It went out of her as it had in the aqueduct, with the cloth pressed to Nereth's face. It didn't go everywhere. It went where there were hands: into Nereth, into the corruption and out along it, into the fists that were holding her, and into every hand that had been laid on Seralune herself.

Seralune felt it go. She tried to pull it back, and it was like trying to pull back a shout.

Osric stiffened.

The black-purple came up out of Nereth's scars and into his knuckles. She watched it happen. It ran up the backs of his hands in thin branching lines, like frost across a window, and into his wrists, and under his sleeves.

He looked down at his hands. He looked confused, like a man who's heard his name called in an empty house.

Then his Fire turned on him.

It came up from inside him. She saw his throat work. She saw the light in it, under the skin, orange and then white at the heart, the same white that had burned in the aqueduct. His mouth opened and it came out of his mouth. It came out of his eyes.

He didn't let go of Nereth. Seralune didn't think he could.

He burned standing up, holding her, with the fire coming out of him and the black lines climbing his neck, and it took no longer than the time it takes to say it. Then his hands opened, and Nereth dropped to the road, and Osric went down beside her with smoke rising off him in the still air. His coat wasn't even scorched. Whatever had burned had burned inside him.

Wren was three paces away.

She'd seen all of it. She stood where he'd put her, behind where his arm had been, with her token hanging down to her waist on its too-long string, and she looked at her father on the road, and she didn't make a sound.

Round them, other people were screaming.

A woman who'd had Seralune by the hood was on her knees coughing up water, clear water, more of it than a body could hold, pouring from her mouth and her nose onto the road. Past her, the man who'd shoved Seralune down was in the dirt with his leg bent the wrong way below the knee. The road had split under his feet, one long crack straight through the packed earth, and closed again on his boot.

The crowd broke.

It broke outwards, away from them, like a flock from a hawk. People fell over each other in the ditches. Somebody was shouting, "Witch, witch, it's a witch," and somebody else was shouting, "The Last Dark take her, the Last Dark take her," over and over, as if the words were a fence.

At the edge of the space that had opened round them, a road priest in white and gold held up his sun disc on its chain. He was pointing it at them the way you'd hold a torch up to a wolf. His hands were shaking so badly the disc spun.

Nobody came near them after that.

Seralune understood, slowly, that they were free to go. The people were afraid of them.

---

She didn't remember lifting Nereth.

She remembered the orchard, afterwards. Rows of small grey trees going up a slope, and Nereth's weight on her shoulder, and Nereth's feet finding the ground about one step in three. She remembered a stream, and the cold of it over her boots, and the far bank, and the hill beyond it. She remembered stopping only when the road was a brown thread below them and the voices were gone.

Somewhere behind them, Wren had started to cry. It was the only sound that had followed them up the slope. It went on and on. Then the wind turned, and it stopped.

They'd come out on a hillside above the orchard, where a shepherd's fold stood roofless against a rock. Seralune got Nereth inside the walls and laid her down on the old straw, and knelt, and for a while did nothing.

Nereth wasn't rigid any more. She lay on her back with her eyes open, breathing in short pulls, and her face was wet. Seralune couldn't tell if it was sweat or tears. She didn't ask.

"Did I hurt him?" Nereth whispered.

Seralune looked at her.

"His hands." Nereth's eyes were on the sky. "I felt it go through me. Into his hands. Did I —"

"No," said Seralune. "You didn't hurt anyone."

It was true. It was the truest thing she'd said all day. She couldn't make herself say the rest of it.

The scars had climbed.

New branches ran up from Nereth's collar, fine as the veins in a leaf, over the side of her neck and under her jaw. When Seralune eased back her sleeve, they ran down her left arm too, all the way to the wrist, where they thinned out and stopped, as if they'd run out of time rather than skin. Heat came off them. It didn't fade when Seralune laid her palm near them. It only waited.

If the marks went hot, Nyana had said, or anything moved under the skin, they were to stop and find someone trained to help.

There was no one. The only healer she'd heard of was a girl in a temple who never left it, and every pilgrim on the road was going to tell that temple what had happened.

The packs had come with them. Seralune didn't remember bringing them either. She found the stoppered jar and the cup in Nereth's pack, and counted five drops into the cup, and lost count at three because her hands wouldn't keep still, and tipped it out onto the straw, and started again.

Count them into the cup before you add the water, Nyana had said. Lose count, start again.

Five. She added water. She held the cup to Nereth's mouth, and Nereth drank, and turned her face away.

From the gap where the fold's gate had been, Seralune could see down the hill to the road.

The pilgrims had started moving again. Not the way they'd been moving before. They were walking fast, faster than people who were carrying their sick should walk, all of them in the same direction, towards Favale. Ahead of them, well ahead of them, a single horseman was galloping up the road, and even from here she could see the church's white and gold on his back.

She understood what he was carrying.

An elf on the pilgrim road. A witch. A man burned from the inside in front of his own child.

They'd seen Nereth's ears. They hadn't seen hers. And the man who'd died had died with his hands on Nereth.

It would be Nereth's face they told the temple about. The scarred one. The burned one. The witch.

Seralune sat down against the wall of the fold, with the sun going over and the straw sharp under her, and looked at her own hands in her lap.

---

Nereth told her after dark.

She'd been lying so still that Seralune thought she was asleep. The fold had filled up with blue shadow, and then black, and there was no fire, because they couldn't risk one. The first stars had come out over the broken wall.

"It happened on the ship," Nereth said.

Seralune turned her head.

"The night you remembered him. The young man." Nereth's voice was flat and careful, as it had been in the palace, before. "You fell, and I caught your wrist, and it went through me like a hot wire. The marks moved. I felt them move. I put my hand over them so you wouldn't see."

"I saw."

"I know. I hoped you'd think you hadn't." She was quiet. "In Meren, too. When the clerk was writing us down. You were so frightened. I thought my face was going to split open."

Seralune said nothing. There didn't seem to be anything that she was allowed to say.

"And this morning," Nereth said. "When the carriage came. Before any of them touched me. Before any of it." She swallowed. "It happens when you're frightened. Or when you grieve. When you feel things so hard they spill."

"Why didn't you tell me?"

"You had enough." It came out quickly, like something she'd prepared. Then, more slowly: "And I wanted to be wrong. I wanted it to be the corruption doing it by itself. Anything else."

Seralune looked at her hands.

She didn't want to test it. She knew that she had to.

She let herself think about Osric.

Not the burning. Before. The fire standing up out of his palm. *You sit there. You look like you walked all the way from the sea.* His hand on his wife's blanket over the side of the cart. His daughter's head in his lap.

The grief came up under her ribs.

Nereth gasped.

It was a short, sharp sound, the sound of someone touching a pan without knowing it was hot. Her hand flew to her neck. In the starlight, Seralune couldn't see the lines move, but she didn't need to.

*Count.*

The dark room. Her brother's voice, patient, a long time ago. *Slowly. One breath first.*

One. She breathed in. Two. She put Osric somewhere she couldn't see him. Three. Four. The fullness under her ribs went down a little, sullenly, like water settling back into a cistern.

Nereth's hand came away from her neck. Her breathing eased.

Neither of them said anything for a long time.

Seralune went back through it all, because she couldn't stop herself. The aqueduct, and the cloth, and the cold going into the cut in her palm. The white fire. Nereth's face as it had been in the palace, and as it was now. The black lines climbing Osric's wrists. His fire, coming out of his eyes.

It had come from her. All of it. It always had.

She'd spent her whole life being told she had a sea of power with no shore to it and nothing to shape it. Nobody had ever told her what a sea does when it finally finds somewhere to go.

Nereth had turned her head on the straw and was looking at her.

Seralune reached out without thinking. She only meant to touch the new scar under Nereth's jaw, the one that hadn't been there this morning, the way she'd have touched a bruise on someone she loved. To say sorry with her fingers, because she didn't trust her voice.

Nereth flinched.

It was small. It was over in a heartbeat. Her shoulders drew in and her head turned away from the hand, and then she caught herself, and her face filled with a shame so naked that Seralune had to look away from it.

On the ship, Nereth had flinched from the pain. This time she had flinched from Seralune.

"I didn't mean —" Nereth said. "Seralune. I didn't mean it. I didn't —"

"I know."

Seralune took her hand back. She put it in her lap, with the other one, and folded them together.

Nereth didn't move away. Seralune didn't ask her to. Neither of them said anything about it. After a while, Nereth's breathing slowed and deepened, and she slept, or pretended to.

Seralune stayed where she was, against the wall, a little apart.

She had sat like this before. In a pale room with the door locked from outside, with her hands folded in her lap, waiting for two knocks, making herself small and still so that nothing in her would reach anything outside it.

She'd escaped that room. She'd gone down into the dark under the palace, and across the sea, and into a country that hated her, to get out of it.

And here she was, making it again, around herself.

She counted.

She counted the stars through the broken wall until there were too many. She counted Nereth's breaths. She counted to a hundred and started again, and every time the grief came up under her ribs she pushed it back down and counted it under, one breath first, one breath at a time, all night, until the stars had gone and the sky over the hills went grey.

She didn't let herself feel anything, and nobody on the hill burned.
