# A War Without Sound

*"……re ….u … urt?"*

*"B… are… y… hu…?"*

Broken words, dragged in from somewhere too far away. They sounded like they'd been travelling towards him for a very long time, and most of them hadn't survived the trip.

He tried to answer.

Nothing came out.

Mud on his cheek. Blood in the air. Something heavy lying across his ribs. The weight of an arm, with none of its warmth.

Burnt meat. Crows.

He couldn't remember what his own voice sounded like.

*Where…*

The thought fell apart before he could finish it. Every idea he reached for slid out of his hands like something pulled from black water.

*Where am I?*

Wet earth pressed against his cheekbone and crusted the edge of one nostril. Under it was something slicker. Stickier. He swallowed, and iron flooded his mouth.

*Is that… blood?*

His heartbeat slammed into the inside of his skull like a fist on a door.

**THOOM.**

His whole body flinched.

**THOOM.**

He waited for the next one. There was nothing else he could do.

Pain split open behind his eyes, white and total, and beneath it lay something worse. The feeling of having been unwound and wound back the wrong way. As if whatever counted the gap between one second and the next had lost track of him, then put him back down crooked.

He tried to lift his hand. It didn't move.

He tried again. His fingers twitched, and even that cost him a fresh wave of agony rolling through his head.

*What happened to me?*

He reached back for whatever had come before the pain.

There was nothing there. Not even the memory of having been somewhere else.

"…boy?"

The voice came from above him. Closer now. The word hit like it had weight, and every aching part of his skull rang with it.

"Boy! Can you hear me?"

He tried to open his eyes — and something else opened instead.

Light. His eyes were still shut, still glued with mud, and yet there was light, and it wasn't coming from outside him. Pure white swallowed everything. For one impossible moment he wasn't lying in the mud at all. No ground. No sky. No up or down. Only the unbearable sense of being stretched thin and dragged towards a place he could never reach.

The light didn't blind him. It searched him. Like it already knew his shape and was hunting through the white for the rest of him.

Somewhere inside it, someone screamed. He couldn't tell if it was him.

Then it let go, and he was back in the plain, ordinary dark behind his own eyelids.

The boy opened his eyes.

The sun hung directly overhead. And inches from his face, a dead man's hand jutted out of the mud, fingers still curled around a spear shaft snapped off halfway up.

He couldn't turn his head away from it. He strained his eyes as far sideways as they would go and found more of the same: bodies, some in armour, some in almost nothing, none of them moving. He dragged in a breath so hard his chest locked around it.

Heavy hands grabbed his shoulders.

"Easy! Easy, lad — by the Four, boy, steady —"

Someone rolled him onto his back.

The boy screamed.

**THOOM.**

The movement drove a spike straight through the middle of his skull, and his heartbeat hammered it deeper, like a mallet on a bell.

"Easy now. I've got you." The hands stopped trying to move him and simply held on.

He squeezed his eyes shut against the glare and breathed in ragged pulls. Underneath the pain, one thin fact surfaced and refused to go away: he was alive. He understood the word perfectly. He couldn't remember the life it was supposed to describe.

Something sticky slid out of his hair and down the curve of his temple. He lifted a shaking hand to it. His fingers came back smeared with mud and dark blood. A stiff lock of brown hair sagged across his eyes, and he pushed it aside with the heel of his palm.

A broad shape leaned over him, blocking the worst of the sun.

"Can you hear me, lad? Where are you hurt?"

The voice was deep and gravelly. Its owner kept glancing past him at the field, even while he waited for an answer.

The boy tried to speak and managed only a dry rasp. He clutched his head instead and curled around the pain.

"Your head?" The man followed the movement, then took in the rest of him: blood from collar to boots. "Hold still a moment. Let me see."

Rough, calloused hands began working down his lean frame. Arms. Chest. Stomach. Legs. Pressing, checking, feeling for anything broken.

Twice, those hands stopped. Both times, the boy heard the man's breathing change.

There was no iron plate. No boiled leather. No family crest stamped across a breastplate, no weapon hanging at his hip. Only his clothes, and they were strange. The cloth was smooth and soft, nothing like the coarse weave the man was wearing, and it fitted the boy perfectly. Not a seam had split. There wasn't a single tear or puncture anywhere on it.

The stitching seemed to bother the old man most. He rolled one seam between his fingers, then another, frowning at stitches so small and even the boy could barely tell them apart. Whatever he was looking for, he didn't find it.

"Not a mark on you," he muttered. "All this blood, and not one mark."

He looked out across the field, then back at the boy. Whatever had been confusion on his face a moment ago was turning into something closer to unease.

"Can you sit up?"

The boy planted one hand in the mud and pushed. His arm shook under him. Before it could buckle, the old man slid an arm behind his shoulders and hauled him upright.

The world smeared into brown and red and white. He lurched sideways and grabbed the man's sleeve to keep from going over.

Slowly, the shapes steadied.

He looked towards the horizon, and forgot to breathe.

---

Scorched earth stretched away beneath a pale sky, and the dead lay across it in their thousands.

This wasn't a field anymore. It was a graveyard nobody had bothered to dig.

Men sprawled in split armour, twisted into shapes bodies shouldn't make. Broken spears stood up out of the ash like the trunks of a burned forest. Shields lay half-buried between them, cracked through.

And above it all loomed the machines.

Huge metal husks, seamless and silver, like carriages built for war by someone who had never seen a horse. The boy couldn't have explained how they worked. He didn't need to. Everything about them said what they were for. One had collapsed onto a set of jointed metal legs. Another faced the ruin through a long slot in its smooth front, the edges blackened, as if something had burned its way out.

Not all of the dead were human, either.

Pale soldiers lay among the men, narrow pointed ears rising through hair stiff with blood. And among them lay creatures with horns and claws, built too broad and heavy to be men at all, their fists still locked around weapons no human could have lifted.

The longer he looked, the more wrong it became.

A horse lay with its forelegs stretched out mid-stride, a stride it had never finished. Its rider was pinned beneath it, one hand reaching towards a soldier slumped on his knees. Their fingers were inches apart.

These people hadn't been beaten. They'd been *interrupted*.

As if death hadn't fought its way through them at all. As if it had simply arrived, everywhere, all at once.

Crows squabbled between the bodies, wings beating loud against the quiet. One braced its feet on a corpse and tugged at something under a ruined breastplate.

Then he saw where the destruction ended.

It just… stopped.

No churned-up approach. No road of trampled earth. No siege line, no abandoned camp, no trail of broken carts. Past the outermost body, the wheat stood untouched, calm golden rows shifting in the wind as if nothing had happened.

No army had marched into this field. The battle hadn't spread into it from anywhere. It had appeared right in the middle, whole.

The boy couldn't look away. He searched the ruin for anything that belonged to him: a crest he knew, a face he recognised, something that would turn these dead into more than strangers.

His mind gave him nothing.

He had no idea who these men were, or what they'd died for.

"…That was wheat," the old man said quietly behind him. "When I barred my door last night, all of it was wheat. Hip-high. Two weeks off the sickle, near enough."

The boy looked from him to the gold beyond the bodies.

"You were here?" His throat scraped around the words. "Did you hear it happen?"

The farmer shook his head.

"Fifty winters I've slept with one ear open. Wolf worrying the fold, fox in the hens, some bastard testing my fence on a moonless night — I wake. Every time."

He crouched beside the nearest body, close enough to touch it. His hand hovered over the ruined armour and didn't come down.

"Last night? Nothing. Quiet enough I lay there listening to the owls hunt." The hand dropped back to his knee. "All these dead, and not one shout. Not one horn. Not one poor lad crying for his mother."

He looked out over the field.

"I've walked battlefields the morning after. Every war leaves its crawlers — men dragging themselves anywhere but where they fell. There's always a trail. Blood. Broken grass. Something." He had to force the rest out. "There's no trail here. Not one. It's like they were dead before they ever touched my ground."

The boy looked farther out and found something the carnage hadn't prepared him for.

Most of the ruin made a horrible kind of sense. Iron had split armour and flesh. Fire had blackened the earth.

Not all of it, though.

Here and there, the ground still crackled with violet light. Tiny arcs jumped between charred stones, hunting for somewhere to ground themselves. Elsewhere, patches of frost held the daylight wrong, dim and pale, as if dusk had fallen on that one stretch of earth and nowhere else.

Then he saw the circle.

It lay a short way from where he'd woken: a perfect boundary drawn through the devastation. Outside it, the earth was burned or frozen or torn apart. Inside it, the grass was still green beneath a skin of frost as thin as glass. Every blade had been pressed flat into a spiral, winding in towards a single point.

No crater. No ash. No blood, no scrap of armour, no bone.

Not even a footprint.

It was perfectly, immaculately empty, and the boy stared at it until the sight started to ache somewhere under his ribs. It wasn't the emptiness that frightened him. It was the *shape* of it. This wasn't a place where something had been destroyed.

It was a place where something had been spared.

His eyes settled on the centre. Nothing stood there now. But some instinct buried deeper than his missing memories insisted that someone should have been.

A word surfaced. Not a guess. A certainty.

『Magic』

*…How do I know that?*

Something stirred under the recognition. It wasn't an image, or a voice. Just the lurch of falling towards something, and being torn away before he could reach it.

Then that was gone too, and the battlefield came flooding back into him.

The stench of blood and ash and opened bodies coated the back of his throat. His stomach turned over. He clapped a hand over his mouth.

Too late.

He folded forward and retched bile and bloody spit onto the scorched earth.

"Easy! Easy, lad!"

The farmer was right beside him. His voice wasn't. The first *easy* reached the boy clearly. The second sounded like it came from the far end of a tunnel. *Lad* faded out before it arrived at all.

The world tilted. The crows smeared into black streaks. The silver machines bled together, and the violet sparks stretched into long, trembling threads.

Rough arms caught him and heaved his limp body over a broad shoulder.

From somewhere beyond the closing dark, the farmer kept muttering.

"…heavy little shite… bleeding all over my good wheat…"

One violet thread stayed stretched across the boy's fading sight, thin as a hair.

Then it snapped.

---

When he opened his eyes again, the smell of death was gone.

Stewing vegetables had replaced it, earthy and sweet, along with the sharp tang of burning pine.

For a while, the boy lay there and breathed. Someone had wiped the mud and dried blood from his face. A heavy wool blanket lay over him, and a straw mattress crackled softly under his weight.

A stone hearth popped and hissed nearby, throwing long shadows up rough wooden walls and across thick ceiling beams. One of those shadows seemed to hang on a little after the fire had moved.

He blinked. It behaved itself.

A small table with a candle burning low. Iron pots hanging from hooks. A shuttered window, black with night. By any measure it was an ordinary cabin, and he searched every corner of it for something familiar: a meal eaten at that table, a night spent under those beams, the memory of that fire's warmth.

None of it had ever been his.

The old man from the battlefield sat on a low stool beside the hearth with a dented metal mug between his hands. He looked like the day had sat on his shoulders and refused to get off.

The blankets rustled. The man looked up over the rim of his mug, and when the boy tried to push himself upright, he raised one broad hand.

"Easy, lad. You went out like a snuffed candle on me back there. Give yourself a moment."

The boy tried to swallow. His mouth felt lined with dust.

"…Where?"

The man lowered the mug to his knee and leaned in. "What was that?"

"Where am I?"

"Still on my farm. Mydean soil." The man's eyes went to the shuttered window before he added, "Name's Gerolt, if you need one for the fool who dragged you in here."

"…Mydea?"

Gerolt's thumb stopped against the rim of the mug.

"Mydea," he repeated. "The kingdom you're lying in. That name means nothing to you?"

The boy turned the word over. It brought him no road, no border, no place he could remember standing. He shook his head.

"By the Four." Gerolt dragged a thumb across his mouth. "What happened to you, lad?"

He looked the boy over again, lingering on his clothes.

"You don't look like any soldier I've known. Barely twenty winters on you, I'd wager. And what in the Last Dark are you wearing? No iron. No leather. Soft as bed linen, and not a tear in it after all that." He pointed at the cuff. "Who made that? I went over every seam while I was cleaning you up. Never seen work like it. Not from any trader who's come down this road in thirty years."

The boy looked down. The dark fabric sat on him like it had been made for no one else. He turned one cuff between his fingers, half hoping the feel of it would shake something loose: a pair of hands fastening it, a voice telling him to stand still, anything.

Nothing came. He opened his mouth. Closed it again. His fingers kept worrying at the seam.

"Leave the cloth for now," Gerolt said. "What's your name, lad?"

His name.

He knew that, surely. Everyone had a name.

He reached for his —

— and found the same dark he'd found everywhere else.

No word. No face. No voice calling him home.

His breathing shortened.

"What is it?" Gerolt asked.

"I…" The boy stared at his shaking hands and closed them into fists. *Think. There has to be something.* "I don't know."

It came out as a whisper. He couldn't make himself look up.

"You don't know your own name?"

He shook his head.

"Where you're from, then?"

The word *home* surfaced with no place behind it. *Mother* followed, hollow. A word with nobody standing inside it.

"I understand you," he said, and his grip on the blanket tightened. "I know what things are. I just can't remember where I learnt any of it. I can't remember anyone."

"Nothing before this morning?"

"There has to be something." His voice was climbing now. "I can't have *nothing*."

"Leave it a moment, lad. You're shaking."

The boy barely heard him. His fingers went back to his collar, then the cuffs, then every strip of stitching he could reach. Somebody had made these clothes. Somebody might recognise them. There was nothing hidden in the lining. He checked every seam twice anyway, and made himself let go before he could start a third time.

Fine. If there was no answer inside his head, he'd have to start with whatever was outside it.

"There's something else we can try," Gerolt said, and waited until the boy looked at him. "Your『Affinity』. Reach for it, lad. Tell me what answers."

"My… what?"

"Your element. Fire, water, earth or wind, for most folk. You're born with it. Losing your memory shouldn't take it from you."

The boy looked at his hands. "How do I find it?"

Gerolt tapped his own chest. "Close your eyes. Don't go chasing memories. Reach inward and listen. Tell me what you feel."

The boy did. He sank into the dark behind his eyes and searched for a spark, a current, a weight, a breath of wind, anything that felt like it belonged to him.

Nothing answered.

He opened his eyes. Gerolt was watching him over a mug he'd stopped drinking from.

"I can't find anything."

Gerolt set the mug down on the floor very carefully, and kept his fingers on the handle after it had settled.

"That's not possible."

The boy pulled the blanket closer. "Then tell me what I'm doing wrong."

"I knew a man who came home from the war and couldn't name his own wife," Gerolt said. "He could still call his element. It isn't a memory, lad. You should feel something, even if you can't tell me what it is."

"There isn't anything." His voice wobbled under that stare. "I *tried*."

Gerolt let go of the mug. His eyes went to the door, then back to the boy, and this time he didn't lean any closer.

"No past. No name. No spark. You're not just addled. You're…"

His voice dropped.

"Empty."

The boy looked down at his hands and had no argument to give him.

Gerolt got up and crossed to the window. He cracked the shutter open a finger's width and stared out into the night, one hand braced against the frame hard enough to whiten his knuckles.

"I didn't know a single banner on that field," he said, without turning around. "I've buried men under half the flags in this kingdom, and I didn't know one of those. And men don't die shoulder to shoulder with beastfolk. Not in any war I've ever heard of."

He glanced back towards the bed.

"Wena barks at owls. Barks her fool head off every night of her life. Last night, not a sound. Found her this morning pressed flat against my door, shaking like a whelp."

As if she'd heard her name, something under the bed let out a thin, miserable whine.

The boy looked down. Two frightened eyes caught the firelight in the dark beneath the frame, then vanished again.

"She's been under there ever since," Gerolt said. "Won't come out. Not even for me."

He turned from the window. In the firelight he looked ten years older than he had that morning.

"You were lying right in the middle of those dead. Not another soul breathing." It wasn't an accusation. It was worse: a man asking because he needed to know. "What happened out there?"

"I —"

The boy searched for something, anything, to give him. Gerolt's gaze dropped to the fists knotted in the blanket.

"Aye. I know." Gerolt scrubbed a hand down his face. "I just keep expecting you to tell me."

The boy's stomach picked that exact moment to growl.

*Loudly.*

It rolled through the quiet cabin like distant thunder. The boy went rigid and dragged the blanket higher, heat flooding into his face.

Gerolt stared at him.

Neither of them said anything.

Then the old farmer let out a dry, shaky breath that very nearly turned into a laugh.

"Well." He looked towards the pot on the hearth. "At least I know what to do about that."

He took a wooden bowl down from the counter and ladled it full.

"Here. Mind the bowl, it's hot." He didn't let go until the boy had both hands wrapped around it.

The hollow ache in the boy's belly had stopped being ignorable a while ago. He lifted a spoonful to his cracked lips and swallowed.

Warmth. Root vegetables, salted meat, thick broth. Simple, real things that asked nothing of him at all.

Then something flickered.

A corridor full of light that came from nowhere, humming with a low chord no instrument could play.

Gone.

A hand that wasn't his, brushing hair back from someone's face.

Gone.

One word, spoken gently, in a voice that felt closer to him than his own heartbeat —

Gone, before he could tell whether it had been his name.

And then his mind split open.

The cabin disappeared. White light lanced through his vision and kept going, through flesh, through memory, pulling him apart one strand at a time.

Beneath the pain came something colder.

The feeling of being *counted*.

Weighed.

And, for reasons he couldn't begin to understand, found worth keeping.

Fragments tore through him. Light. Too much light. Someone falling. A hand reaching out —

*"Don't leave me alone…"*

*"Please…"*

*"Don't take him from me!"*

Someone was screaming. The voice was warped and worn thin, like it had crossed a distance no voice should survive. It didn't sound like it came from inside a room.

It sounded like it was bleeding through a tear in the world.

He reached for it —

— and the words came apart in his hands. A hollow rush of cold ripped through the vision, and the voice was gone.

His throat locked shut.

"——hk!! ——ghah…!!"

He lurched forward, hands spasming. The bowl slipped from his fingers and cracked against the floor, hot stew splashing across the boards.

He didn't feel it.

He couldn't breathe. There was no air in him. There was no room for air in him.

He slid off the mattress onto his knees, one hand clutching his chest, the other clawing at his throat.

"Lad!"

Gerolt had him by the shoulders.

"Look at me, lad!" His grip tightened as the boy's knees started to slide. "Can you hear me?"

The boy couldn't answer.

Then the panic stopped.

It didn't ease off. It didn't fade. It stopped, the way a held note stops when someone cuts the string.

His hands went slack. Tears kept running down his face, silent now.

He didn't know whose voice that had been, or why it had sounded so utterly, desolately afraid.

He reached inside himself one more time. For his name. For his『Affinity』. For whoever had been screaming.

The dark gave him back exactly what it had given him all day.

---

Gerolt stayed crouched beside him, one hand steady on his shoulder.

Then his fingers dug in.

His head turned towards the road, and every part of him went still.

At first, the boy heard nothing. Then a faint rhythm trembled up through the floorboards and into his knees.

Hoofbeats. Far off, but coming fast.

Under the bed, Wena's whimpering cut off like someone had closed a hand around it.

Gerolt was at the window before the boy could ask what was wrong. He pressed one eye to the narrow gap in the shutters, and whatever he saw out there wiped the exhaustion clean off his face and left fear behind in its place.

He moved without a word.

The iron poker scraped through the hearth as he raked ash over the coals, and their orange glow died one ember at a time. He crossed to the table and pinched out the candle between finger and thumb, and didn't even seem to feel the burn.

Darkness swallowed the cabin. Only thin blades of moonlight slipped between the shutters.

The hoofbeats kept coming.

Outside, a torch flared to life. Harsh orange light cut through the gaps in the shutters and laid long bars across the floor. One of them swept straight across the boy.

He froze. For one naked instant, the light lay across his face and chest.

Then he saw Gerolt's face, and understood.

He flattened himself against the floorboards and dragged the blanket down with him, keeping its folds out of the light. Gerolt crossed the room in two strides, eased the bench onto its side and pulled the boy in behind it.

"Not a sound," he breathed. "Not one. Do you understand me?"

"What's —"

"Riding openly across Mydean fields…" Gerolt's voice was barely there. "They shouldn't be here. Not ever."

His mouth started to shape a name. Whatever it was, he swallowed it.

The boy's lips parted.

Riders meant people. People meant answers. One of them might know what had happened on that field. One of them might even know *him*.

He drew breath to call out —

— and Gerolt's hand tightened on his shoulder.

The boy looked at him. At the man who had walked through thousands of corpses that morning without breaking. Who had carried a bleeding stranger home across his own ruined harvest, and put food in his hands, and was now crouched in the dark beside a smothered fire, hiding from whoever was out there rather than let them know he was home.

Calling out wouldn't be the boy's choice alone. It would make it for both of them.

He looked once towards the door.

Then he closed his mouth.

He wanted answers. He chose silence.

Outside, the hoofbeats slowed. Changed. Harness creaked. Metal chinked softly against metal.

Then the hoofbeats stopped.

The boy held his breath. In the dark, his heart was hammering against his ribs so hard he was sure it would give them away.

Then a voice, murmuring beyond the wall.

It wasn't any language the boy knew. The syllables overlapped in a way that made his skin crawl, as if several throats were shaping the same sounds at once, not quite in step.

Gerolt's fingers dug harder into his shoulder. His eyes never left the door.

The boy gave up trying to understand the voice and listened to what was underneath it instead. A buckle shifting. A hoof scraping at the dirt. Torchlight sliding between the shutters, disappearing, then coming back from a different angle.

None of it added up to a picture. He didn't know how many were out there. Where they stood. What they wanted.

So he watched Gerolt.

If the farmer understood that language, his face didn't show it. But he knew enough to be afraid. For now, that was enough for the boy, too.

Outside, a stirrup rang softly.

A mount let out a long breath as a weight lifted off its back.

Someone dismounted.

The boy waited for the ground to answer: the thud of boots, the crunch of dirt.

It never came.

**Tock.**

**Tock.**

**Tock.**

Knuckles on the cabin door.

Gentle.

Patient.

Like whoever stood out there was willing to wait forever.

Gerolt's eyes went wide. He looked down at the boy, and the boy looked back. His heart was battering his ribs and every breath shook on the way out, but he didn't turn towards the door.

*Don't move*, Gerolt mouthed. No sound came with it at all.

Outside, slow and deliberate as a funeral bell, the knocking came a fourth time.

And somewhere under his fear, a small, wordless part of the boy went very quiet. Because he knew, in the same place that had known the word『Magic』, that opening that door would be the last thing he ever did.
