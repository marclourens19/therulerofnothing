# The Shape of Absence

Thaeroval did not move.

Seralune’s arms remained around him, her cheek pressed against the cold plates covering his chest. Beneath the armour, his breathing came too quickly. Each breath lifted her by the smallest amount, then left his body more rigid than before.

His eyes stayed fixed upon the broken circle.

“Thaer?” Seralune loosened her embrace, trying to see what held his attention beyond her.

A tremor passed through him, beginning somewhere beneath her hands and travelling through his shoulders. The point of the blade hanging at his side shifted across the stone with a faint metallic scrape.

Seralune drew back far enough to see him clearly.

“What is it?” she asked. “You’re frightening me.”

Thaeroval’s gaze lowered to her at last. He fumbled once at his hip before the sword slid into its sheath. Then he reached for her. One hand touched her shoulder, stopped, then crossed her back. The other followed, and he pulled her against him.

The force of it drove the breath from her. One arm locked around her shoulders while his other hand closed over the back of her head, pressing her face beneath his chin. His armour bit into her ribs. She tried to speak, but his hold tightened before the first word could leave her.

“Seralune.” His voice broke against her hair. He said nothing else.

Seralune felt his breath touch the crown of her head. Once. Again. Neither was steady.

She placed both hands against his chest and waited.

Thaeroval had embraced her before leaving for the border and before ceremonies he knew she hated. Once, after a physician had mistaken cruelty for honesty and told her that all the mana in the world could not make an『Affinity』where none existed.

Never like this.

“You’re hurting me,” she whispered.

His arms opened immediately.

“Forgive me.”

He did not release her completely. Both hands remained upon her shoulders while his eyes moved from her brow to her mouth, then along her jaw. He studied each part as though familiarity alone could no longer be trusted.

“How long have you been awake?”

Seralune glanced towards the darkness behind her.

“I don’t know. A little while.” Her throat hurt when she swallowed. “I couldn’t see anything. I called for you until someone came.”

His gaze dropped to the dust upon her hands, then to the bare feet beneath the hem of her dress.

“Did you touch the circle?” Thaeroval asked, so quickly that she glanced behind her.

“I woke inside it,” Seralune said. “I had to feel for a way out.”

Something tightened in his face.

Seralune looked past him. Beyond the ruined doorway waited a stone passage she did not recognise. White crystals had been set along the ceiling, their light too pale and constant to belong to flame. It caught upon Thaeroval’s armour and the sword hilt at his hip. The cut edges of the doorway were clean beneath the fallen dust.

“Where am I?” she asked.

Running footsteps answered before he could.

Thaeroval turned.

The change passed through him at once. His shoulders settled, and the sword came free again. He stepped between Seralune and the broken doorway with the ease of a movement too often practised to require thought.

Figures crowded the passage beyond. Dark armour. Grey robes. Gold caught at throats and wrists. The first guards reached the opening with their weapons drawn, then stopped so abruptly that those behind struck their backs.

No one entered. Their attention moved past Thaeroval and found Seralune.

She had stood before crowded courts without lowering her eyes. She knew admiration, curiosity and the carefully hidden disappointment that followed whenever someone learned what she could not do.

This was none of them.

One guard’s fingers tightened around his spear. A woman behind him covered her mouth. Someone whispered the opening words of a warding prayer.

“Lower your weapons.”

The order came from farther down the passage. The crowd divided, and a man walked between them.

His robes were black beneath a mantle of deep green, their borders worked with the branching gold Seralune had seen upon every member of the royal court since childhood. A narrow circlet rested across his brow. Not a crown, but close enough to one that those around him moved before he needed to ask.

She knew the way he held his mouth when something displeased him and the slight unevenness of his ears, the right curving closer to his head than the left. She remembered ink across those fingers and a summer afternoon spent hiding from their tutors beneath the western stairs.

But silver marked his hair at the temples, and fine lines rested around his eyes. Not the faint creases of exhaustion or illness. They belonged there. Time had settled into his face and made itself comfortable.

“Leorin?”

Her cousin stopped. For one moment, the composure left him. His lips parted. Something close to recognition moved through his eyes—warm, wounded and gone too quickly for Seralune to hold.

Then he bowed.

“Your Highness.”

The words placed more distance between them than the length of the chamber.

Seralune looked at his hair again.

_What happened to you?_

She almost asked.

Leorin’s gaze travelled to Thaeroval instead.

“How long has she been conscious?”

“Ask her,” Thaeroval said. He remained between Seralune and the waiting guards.

Leorin looked back, and the silence waited for Seralune to fill it.

“I don’t know,” she said. “Why is everyone asking me that?”

Neither man answered. Leorin stepped towards the doorway.

Thaeroval’s sword crossed the opening. It did not touch him. It did not need to.

Leorin stopped.

“Has she used her mana?”

“I am standing here,” Seralune said, stepping far enough beside her brother for Leorin to see her. “Ask me.”

His eyes returned to her. Something in them softened. His face did not.

“Have you used your mana?” Leorin asked. His voice was gentler, but he watched her hands as he waited.

Seralune looked from him to her brother.

“I tried to make light.” Seralune’s fingers closed against her dress as the people in the corridor went still. “It didn’t work.”

Relief passed through the assembled guards so quietly that she might have imagined it. She had seen that same relief in physicians when her failures proved their understanding of the world remained correct.

It had never frightened her before.

Leorin turned towards the chamber. From where he stood, he could see only part of the pattern cut into its floor. It was enough.

“Close the lower passages,” he said. “No one enters without my authority.”

Orders moved behind him. Guards turned. Robes swept across the stone.

“Send for the wardwrights,” he continued. “Every wardwright in the palace.”

“No.”

Thaeroval spoke quietly, and the passage emptied of sound.

Leorin looked at the blade between them.

“The circle is broken,” Leorin said. “We need them to examine it.”

“No one touches my sister.” Thaeroval’s grip tightened upon the sword.

Leorin looked past the blade to Seralune, then back at him. “Then take her upstairs. But let the wardwrights through.”

For a moment, neither man moved. The guards waited with their eyes upon the blade.

Thaeroval lowered the blade and held one hand towards her.

“Come with me.”

Seralune left his hand untouched.

“Where am I?” she asked.

“Beneath the palace.” Thaeroval’s voice softened as he looked at her bare feet. “Let me get you somewhere warm. Please.”

Thaeroval had commanded armed companies before breakfast and silenced her father’s council with a look. Seralune could not remember the last time she had heard him ask anyone for anything.

She placed her hand in his. His fingers closed gently around hers and did not loosen as he led her out.

---

The passage climbed.

Seralune felt each step in her knees. Thaeroval slowed whenever her weight shifted against his arm, though she never asked him to. Behind them, Leorin spoke in a low voice to the guards. The words blurred beneath bootsteps and the soft metallic whisper of Thaeroval’s sword entering its sheath.

At the first turning, Thaeroval murmured to one of the guards. The man hurried ahead without looking at Seralune.

Nothing they passed belonged to the palace she remembered. The walls were too bare. No windows opened onto the inner courtyards. At every turning, pale crystals burned inside cages of black metal, filling the air with a faint hum she felt through her teeth.

They passed beneath one arch, then another. The third carried the royal tree carved into its stone.

Seralune stopped.

She knew that tree. Its roots should have divided around four stars, one for each of the old houses that had first bent their knees to her family.

Here, the roots encircled nine. New branches crowded its crown.

“Thaer. That carving is wrong.” Seralune held his hand tighter when he tried to continue. “There were only four stars.”

He followed her gaze, his thumb moving once across the back of her hand.

“You remember it correctly.” Thaeroval glanced towards the guards behind them. “We will speak when we are alone.”

He guided her onwards. Seralune looked back until the arch disappeared behind Leorin and the guards.

The corridors beyond it began to resemble home. The stone lightened first. Dark floors gave way to pale marble crossed with green veins. Tall windows opened upon gardens silvered by moonlight. The palace rose around them in familiar bones—and wore a stranger’s face.

The curtains were the wrong colour. Statues occupied alcoves that had once held flowers. Unknown faces watched from painted walls, their features composed into the solemn dignity artists gave the dead.

Seralune searched them for someone she knew.

She found no one. Servants stood pressed against the walls. Some bowed. Others forgot until the person beside them pulled them down.

An elderly man carrying folded linen dropped to both knees.

“Your Highness,” he whispered.

The linen slipped from his arms. Tears ran down his face, and he made no attempt to wipe them away.

Seralune tried to turn towards him, but her knees faltered. Thaeroval steadied her against his arm and kept walking. She had to follow to remain upon her feet.

They reached a door worked with stone lilies. Thaeroval opened it himself, and warm air touched Seralune’s face.

A fire burned behind a brass screen. Lamps softened the corners of a room arranged for someone who had not yet arrived: fresh water steaming in a basin, folded nightclothes upon the bed, a tray of fruit and bread beside the hearth.

The preparations unsettled her more than the unfamiliar halls.

“How did they know I was coming here?” Seralune asked, looking at the steam rising from the basin.

“I sent someone ahead.” Thaeroval followed her inside and waited while she steadied herself against the bed.

A young woman waited near the bed in the grey and green of a royal attendant. Seralune did not recognise her. The woman bowed without raising her eyes.

“She will remain with you,” Thaeroval said, indicating the attendant.

“Where is Elowen?” Seralune asked.

The name produced no reaction in the attendant.

Something tightened beside Thaeroval’s mouth, gone almost before it appeared.

Before he could answer, voices gathered in the corridor. Leorin had arrived.

“I need to speak to him,” Thaeroval said, looking towards the door.

“I asked you about Elowen.” Seralune watched his face, waiting for the name to bring an answer.

“I heard you.” His hand settled briefly over hers upon the bed. “I will return.”

Seralune caught his wrist before he could turn away.

“Before evening?”

The old promise came out before she understood why she had chosen it.

His face changed—not fear this time, but something quieter and worse.

Thaeroval covered her hand with his.

“Soon.”

He freed himself gently and left.

The door closed between them.

---

The attendant poured water into a silver cup. Her hands shook, and the pitcher touched its rim with a faint clink.

“What is your name?” Seralune asked gently.

The woman almost dropped the pitcher.

“Nereth, Your Highness.”

“How long have you served my family, Nereth?”

“Since I was ten, Your Highness.”

Seralune looked at the young woman before her. She should have known someone who had been here long enough to grow up.

Seralune accepted the cup. Nereth’s fingers withdrew before theirs could touch.

Water pressed cold against Seralune’s teeth. She had not realised how thirsty she was until the first swallow hurt.

“Do you know Elowen?”

Nereth lowered the pitcher.

“No, Your Highness.”

“She attends my rooms.” Seralune waited for some sign that the name had reached her. “She was there yesterday.”

The woman’s gaze lifted. For the briefest moment, Seralune saw pity before Nereth looked down again.

“Forgive me,” Nereth said. “I have never heard anyone by that name.”

Beyond the door, Leorin’s voice rose—not enough for words, only the hard shape of disagreement. Thaeroval answered more quietly, forcing Leorin’s voice down with his own.

Seralune placed the cup upon the table and crossed the room.

Nereth watched her go but did not try to stop her. Seralune stood beside the door and pressed her fingertips against the wood.

The voices sharpened.

“You should have sent for the king the moment the chamber opened,” Leorin said.

“It did not open,” Thaeroval said. “Something broke.”

Seralune’s hand tightened against the door.

“Then he must be recalled. He left for Mydea before midnight. A messenger could still reach him before sunrise.”

“No messenger leaves until I have spoken to her,” Thaeroval said.

“You have placed guards outside her door and forbidden the wardwrights to touch her.” Leorin’s voice rose again. “What am I to tell your father when he asks why no one informed him?”

Seralune looked at Nereth, whose face had emptied of expression.

“I will answer him myself,” Thaeroval said. “Give me until dawn.”

“And if she remembers before then?”

Seralune moved closer until her hair brushed the door.

“She remembers her family,” Thaeroval said after a pause. “Nothing more.”

“You do not know how long that will last.”

Something struck the wall—not with enough force to break it, but hard enough that dust shifted from the doorframe and Nereth flinched beside the bed.

“She is not something for you to examine,” Thaeroval said.

“No. She is your sister.” Leorin’s voice lost what little warmth it had held. “That did not prevent what happened before.”

The words settled through the room. Seralune waited for her brother’s answer, but none came.

Footsteps moved away from the door. Others followed. Orders passed along the corridor too softly for her to distinguish.

Seralune remained where she was.

_If she remembers._

She searched for the edge of something missing.

Her father’s council bell. Her mother sending pears back to the kitchens. Thaeroval promising to return. The memories stood beside one another without touching. Before them waited childhood, tutors, gardens and winters spent beside the eastern hearth.

After them, there was nothing. No darkness or pain. No sense of anything having been taken.

Her life simply stopped.

“Your Highness.”

Nereth stood a few steps behind her, holding out the folded nightclothes. Seralune looked at the unfamiliar fabric.

“What did Leorin mean?” Seralune asked.

The attendant’s arms lowered.

“I do not know, Your Highness. I was sent to attend you. No one told me what had happened.” Nereth’s fingers tightened upon the folded cloth. She looked scarcely less frightened than Seralune felt.

---

Seralune sat beside the fire, still wearing the pale dress in which she had awakened. Nereth had combed the dust from her hair and washed the shallow cut across one palm. The food upon the tray remained untouched.

Two knocks sounded against the door.

Her brother entered alone before she could answer. The familiar habit did little to ease the tightness in her chest.

Nereth bowed and moved towards the door.

“Stay,” Seralune said.

The attendant stopped, looking from Seralune to her brother.

“Wait outside, Nereth,” Thaeroval said. “I need to speak with my sister alone.”

Nereth hesitated, then bowed to Seralune without meeting her eyes. She left, and the door closed.

Thaeroval crossed to the fire but did not sit. Without the sword in his hand, he appeared almost as she remembered him, too serious until someone gave him reason not to be. A strand of dark hair had fallen loose across his brow.

Seralune had pushed that same strand from his face a hundred times. Tonight, she kept her hands in her lap.

“How long was I in that chamber?”

Thaeroval looked into the fire.

“A thousand years.”

The answer was too large to enter the room.

Seralune looked at his reflection in the brass screen.

“No.” Seralune turned from the reflection to her brother. “You were in my doorway yesterday. I was waiting for you to come back.”

Yet Leorin’s hair had been silver. Nereth had grown up in her father’s palace without ever hearing Elowen’s name.

She waited for him to withdraw the answer. He did not.

“How could I have been there that long?” Her voice had begun to shake. She tightened her fingers together. “Why was I there?”

Thaeroval crossed the space between them and lowered himself to one knee.

“Before I answer, tell me what you remember of that day.”

“I just told you.” Seralune tried to keep the hurt from her voice and failed. “You came to my rooms. Mother was arguing with the kitchens before that. Nothing happened.”

“About pears,” he said softly. “They had cooked them again.”

Seralune stared at him. For one moment, they stood together inside the same memory.

“Yes. And you promised you would be back before evening.” She searched his face. “That is all I remember, Thaer. I don’t remember going to bed. I don’t remember anything after you left.”

“Were you afraid?”

Seralune searched his face.

“Afraid of what?”

Thaeroval closed his eyes briefly. “I should not have asked you that.”

“Then why did you?” Seralune leaned towards him. “What happened to me, Thaer?”

He reached for her hands. Seralune allowed him to take them, waiting for an answer.

His palms were warm. A pale scar crossed the base of his right thumb. She did not remember it.

“I am afraid those memories could make your mana harder to control,” he said. “It is already unstable. I do not know what forcing them back might do.”

“To me?”

His fingers tightened around hers.

“To anyone.”

“And Mother? Where is she?” Seralune asked.

Thaeroval went still, and Seralune felt the change in his fingers.

He lowered his gaze to their joined hands.

“I wish I knew,” he said.

The room became very quiet.

“Is she dead?”

His head lifted at once.

“Seralune, I did not say that.” He watched her with such pain that she looked down at their hands.

Seralune pulled one hand from his. He let her.

“Leorin said there are guards outside.”

“They are for your protection.”

“Would they let me leave?”

“No. I need you to remain here until morning.”

The words were gentle. They still closed around her.

“I am frightened, Thaer.”

Thaeroval’s face folded around the words. He bowed his head until his forehead rested against the hand he still held.

For a while, neither of them spoke. Seralune felt his breath against her knuckles. He remained there as he had when they were children and an apology had been too difficult for either of them to say aloud.

Seralune’s free hand moved towards his hair before she stopped it. She wanted to comfort him, and she wanted him to look at her and answer.

Thaeroval raised his head.

“I will come back at first light,” Thaeroval said. “We will speak again then.”

“You will answer me?”

“I will tell you what I can. Please, Seralune. Trust me.”

Not everything. She heard the boundary even if he had not spoken it.

Seralune looked into her brother’s face.

He had always been the one certainty she never needed to question. Whatever had happened, whatever Leorin believed, this was still Thaeroval.

“I trust you,” she said.

Relief softened his expression. For the first time in her life, Seralune wished she were as certain of those words as he appeared to be.

Thaeroval stood and drew her into his arms once more. This embrace did not hurt. He pressed his lips against her forehead.

Seralune almost asked him not to leave, but the words reached her throat and remained there.

Thaeroval crossed the room. Nereth waited beyond the door, entering as he left and setting another piece of wood upon the fire without looking at Seralune.

“Will you remain?” Seralune asked.

“Until I am relieved, Your Highness.” Nereth settled the brass screen back into place. Her voice had softened, though she still would not look directly at Seralune.

Seralune turned towards the window.

Behind her, the door closed, and a lock turned on the other side.

---

Seralune did not sleep.

Nereth sat near the door with her hands folded in her lap. She did not speak unless Seralune addressed her, and when she did, every answer passed through caution before reaching the room.

Outside the window, Natharul waited beneath the dark.

The palace rose upon a mountainside. Seralune knew that much. She recognised the dark ridge beyond the eastern towers and the white line of water falling through the forest below. Everything between them had changed.

Lights covered slopes that had once belonged to trees. Bridges crossed empty air between towers she had never seen. Farther down, the city spread beyond its old walls in terraces of silver and green, each descending level held against the mountain by columns of light.

She had never seen Natharul so large—or so unfamiliar.

Somewhere within it, a bell sounded. Its note was higher than the one she remembered from the eastern tower, and it carried from the west.

One. Two. Three. Seralune stopped counting.

Whenever she closed her eyes, she tried to move through that day one moment at a time. The gown chosen for breakfast. The book left open beside the window. The exact colour of the sky when the council bell rang.

Always, her brother left. She could not remember what she had done once he was gone.

Below the floor, something heavy moved through stone. The sound came faintly at first. A dragging weight. A pause. Then three measured blows that trembled through the legs of Seralune’s chair.

Nereth looked towards the floor.

“What are they doing?” Seralune asked.

“Repairing the lower chamber, I think,” Nereth said.

“Why can it not wait until morning?”

The attendant’s eyes remained lowered.

“I don’t know, Your Highness.”

Another blow entered the room through the stone. The lamps trembled, and Seralune reached inward without meaning to.

Mana waited everywhere.

It answered her fear with the same limitless readiness it had always offered, filling her before she had decided what she wanted from it. She tried to hold it still.

For one breath, the crystals inside the lamps brightened.

Nereth rose so quickly that her chair struck the wall.

The light returned to normal.

Seralune stared at the nearest lamp.

She had not shaped the light. She had not shaped anything.

“I’m sorry,” she said.

Nereth stood with one hand pressed against the door, her face gone pale.

“I did not mean—” Seralune began.

The lock moved from the other side.

Guards entered before the door had opened fully. Steel appeared between Seralune and the fire. A wardwright in green robes followed, one hand gripping a length of silver chain covered in tiny carved symbols.

Nereth had not called out. The noise of her chair had been enough to bring them through the door.

“Stop.” Seralune lifted both hands, staring at the blades.

She let the mana settle. It sank beneath her skin until only its impossible depth remained.

The lamps stayed dim.

No one lowered a weapon.

Nereth stepped between Seralune and the nearest guard. Only after she had moved did surprise cross her face.

“Prince Thaeroval forbade this.”

“Lord Leorin’s orders govern this chamber,” the wardwright said.

“The lamps brightened,” Nereth said. Her voice shook, but she did not move aside. “She has not hurt anyone.”

The wardwright looked past her at Seralune’s hands.

“Did she shape anything?”

“No. I was watching her.” Nereth’s fingers tightened around the folds of her skirt.

The silver chain tightened between the wardwright’s fingers.

“It was an accident,” Seralune said, lowering her hands slowly.

No one acknowledged that she had spoken.

The wardwright waited, watching the lamps. Their light remained steady. At last, the chain loosened between his fingers, and he stepped back.

“Wake Lord Leorin if it happens again.”

The guards withdrew with him. The door closed, and the lock turned.

Nereth returned to her chair, but her knees failed before she reached it. She caught the wooden arm with both hands and lowered herself the rest of the way.

“Thank you,” Seralune said, looking at the hands Nereth still held upon the chair. “For standing in front of me.”

“They came in with swords. You hadn’t hurt anyone.” Nereth glanced towards the door after she spoke.

Seralune looked back towards the lamps.

“They are afraid of me.”

The attendant said nothing. This time, Seralune did not ask her to.

The hours continued, and the fire sank. Shortly before dawn, the lock turned.

One of the guards stood beyond the open door.

“You are relieved.”

Nereth rose from her chair and paused beside Seralune.

“Your Highness.”

Seralune looked at her. The woman seemed about to speak, but the guard shifted, armour touching stone. Whatever Nereth had meant to say remained behind her teeth.

She bowed and left. No one replaced her. The door closed, and the lock turned again.

Seralune returned to the window. Darkness withdrew from the mountain one shade at a time, revealing the unfamiliar city beneath it. Roofs first, then bridges, then the highest branches of the forest turned silver by the approaching sun.

No answers came with them. No one explained what had broken beneath the palace, what she was supposed to remember or where her mother had gone.

By the time pale morning light reached the floor, Seralune had not slept.

Something heavy struck the stone outside.

Seralune turned from the window.

Metal scraped across the corridor floor.

Then came a second impact.

Closer.

A short struggle followed—cloth drawn tight, a boot striking stone once.

Then nothing.

Seralune stood very still.

Silence gathered beyond the door.

Footsteps approached from beyond it.

Slow.

Unhurried.

They stopped outside her room.

Two knocks sounded against the door.

Thaeroval had always knocked twice before entering, as though the warning excused him from waiting for permission.

The spacing was perfect.

This time, Seralune did not answer.

The lock began to turn.
