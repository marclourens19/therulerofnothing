# The Weight of the Living

Alaric ran.

His name followed him through the wheat in Gerolt’s voice.

*Take Wena and run, Alaric.*

Stalks struck his face and folded beneath his arms. Their heads burst against his chest, shedding dry husks that clung to Gerolt’s blood upon his clothing. The ground gave beneath every other step. His legs had not recovered from the pain that had torn his name out of him, and the darkness still tightened and loosened at the edges of his sight.

Wena ran somewhere behind him. He could hear her forcing a path through the wheat, her breath breaking into frightened little bursts. Whenever that sound faltered, he nearly turned. Ahead, the forest appeared only in glimpses between the stalks. He kept running towards it, unable to judge how much ground remained.

The iron token cut into his palm.

Behind him, steel struck steel.

Once.

Again.

A layered cry rose above the wheat. A horse screamed with it, the two sounds twisting together until Alaric could no longer tell where one ended.

He looked back.

Torchlight moved around the broken cabin in scattered pieces. One rider fought a rearing horse while another shape crossed the ruined doorway.

For one instant, Alaric searched for Gerolt among them.

Something hit the cabin.

The night opened orange.

Flame punched through the roof and rolled along the beams. The remaining shutters burst outward. A wave of heat passed through the wheat, bowing every stalk towards Alaric before striking his back hard enough to throw him onto his hands.

Soil filled his mouth.

He rolled onto one hip, still gripping the token.

Smoke dragged itself through the open roof. The horses nearest the cabin scattered. One tore free without its rider, saddle twisting beneath its belly as it plunged blindly through the wheat. Shouts followed it. Layered voices broke across one another, their earlier control gone.

Wena barked.

The sound was high and broken. She stood several paces behind Alaric, staring towards the fire.

Then she turned and ran back.

“Wena!”

She did not slow.

Alaric shoved the token deep into the pocket at his hip and lunged after her. He caught the leather at the back of her neck, and her weight wrenched his arm straight. He fell against her side, one knee striking the ground, and for several paces she dragged him through the soil.

“Stop—” Alaric dug his knee into the earth, struggling to keep hold of her.

Wena twisted beneath him. Her paws tore furrows towards the cabin. A low sound pulled itself from her throat—not anger, not quite, but something too desperate to remain a whine.

Alaric wrapped both hands around her collar.

Gerolt’s blood had dried dark along the fur beneath her jaw.

“He’s—”

The word would not finish.

The cabin burned behind her. Its roof sagged inward, sparks rising into the dark in swarms. Alaric kept looking for Gerolt in the doorway. If he would only call Wena, she would stop fighting. Alaric could let go.

Only the elves shouted.

Hooves struck the earth again.

Alaric pulled Wena’s head around until she faced him.

“He told me to take you,” he said, trying to make her look at him instead of the fire.

She fought him once more.

“I don’t know where else to go.” Alaric had to stop for breath. When he tried again, the words shook. “I need you to come with me.”

Wena stared past him towards the cabin.

Alaric released her collar. For one heartbeat, she remained between them—the fire behind her, the forest beyond him.

Then her ears flattened. She turned away from the cabin and ran. Alaric followed before she could change her mind.

The forest looked close until he tried to reach it. Wheat caught at his knees, and every breath scraped deeper than the one before it. Behind him, the scattered hoofbeats gathered into a single rhythm.

Wena veered sharply to the right and vanished.

“No—this way.” Alaric reached towards the place where she had disappeared, afraid she was turning back again.

Alaric stumbled after her and found a narrow drainage cut beneath the wheat. Wena was already inside it, moving towards the trees with her belly close to the soil.

She knew this land. He did not. Alaric dropped into the cut and followed.

The earth was damp and uneven. His ankle folded once in water hidden beneath the grass, sending him against the bank, but he forced the leg beneath him and continued.

An arrow passed overhead and buried itself in the wheat. A second entered the bank beside his hand, spitting soil across his knuckles.

The stalks behind him bent at once.

Wind tore over his back. Wheat ripped from the earth, and the force drove him down. He caught himself upon the stones, fresh pain tearing through his already bloodied hands.

Wena stopped at the trees and looked back.

“Go,” Alaric gasped, waving her on.

She waited until he forced himself upright, then disappeared between the trees. Alaric stumbled after her. A branch caught his cheek, and his shoes struck roots he could not see. Ahead, Wena moved without hesitation. Behind them, the first horse entered the trees, struck wood and screamed.

Elves shouted in the layered language. Harness shifted, followed by boots striking the ground.

They were continuing on foot.

Alaric caught himself against a trunk and listened. Leaves moved behind him beneath careful steps. Ahead, a continuous roar swallowed the smaller noises of the forest. He turned towards it, trying to hear through his own breathing.

Water.

Gerolt’s voice returned through blood and fire.

*West. Across the river.*

Wena had stopped farther down the slope. She looked back at him once, then slipped between two low trees. Alaric pushed away from the trunk and followed.

The ground descended sharply. Dampness entered the air, cold against the sweat upon his face. The sound grew until it filled the spaces between the trees.

Then the forest ended.

The river moved below him, swollen beyond its banks. Moonlight broke across the black current and gathered white wherever it struck the exposed stones.

Farther to the right, a bridge crossed the river.

It was little more than a dark line between the banks, its wooden rails silvered by moonlight. For a moment, relief loosened something inside Alaric.

Then torchlight moved among the trees leading towards it.

Two flames. Then a third. Riders were moving towards the bridge, already nearer to it than he was.

Behind him, a branch shifted beneath a foot that was not Wena’s.

The river in front of him pulled at whole trees.

Wena approached the water and stopped. Her ears pressed flat. She lowered her nose towards the current, then recoiled when it climbed over the bank and touched her paws.

Alaric knew what swimming was. He could picture arms moving through water, but could not remember ever doing it himself.

He looked once towards the bridge. The torches were moving faster now.

No time.

He pressed one hand over his pocket. The token was still there. He pushed it deeper, terrified that the river might take that from him too.

Then he stepped into the river.

Cold closed around his ankles.

The second step brought it to his knees. The current bent his legs, and the stones shifted beneath his feet. He turned partly sideways and moved towards the far bank.

Wena remained behind him, trembling.

“Come on.” Alaric reached back towards her, keeping his other arm out for balance.

She whined and drew away from the water.

“Wena, please.” His fingers stretched towards her collar, but she was beyond his reach.

A voice called from the trees.

The words were layered and incomprehensible. Another voice answered close enough that Alaric heard the speaker draw breath.

He stepped deeper.

The river climbed above his thighs, filling his shoes and tightening his clothing around his legs. He placed one foot ahead of the other and found nothing beneath it.

His body dropped.

The current took him before he could draw breath.

Water closed over his head. The riverbed struck his knee and vanished. His shoulder collided with stone, spinning him until he no longer knew which direction held the sky. Something tore along his ribs before he kicked away and broke the surface.

He dragged in half a breath and half the river. The cough folded him before he could take another. The current carried him between two rocks, turning the water white around his chest.

His hand struck wood. He closed his fingers before he knew what he had found.

A root stretched from the western bank, stripped pale where the river had worried away its bark. Alaric caught it with both hands. The current dragged the rest of him downstream. His shoulders burned. One hand began to slide.

He tightened his grip until the torn skin of his palm opened again.

He could see mud between the roots. Another arm’s length would let him drive one knee against the bank. He tried to pull himself closer.

Something barked upstream. Alaric turned, his grip slipping upon the wet root.

Wena was in the river.

Only her head rose above the surface. Her forelegs struck at water that offered nothing to stand upon. The current pulled her away from the eastern bank and carried her towards him, then past.

“Wena!” The shout scraped Alaric’s throat. Her eyes found him.

Alaric shifted one hand along the root and reached with the other.

His fingers touched the wet fur behind her ear.

Then nothing.

Wena spun beyond him. Her head disappeared. It surfaced again several paces downstream, lower than before.

The western bank waited beneath Alaric’s knee.

He could climb.

He remembered Gerolt’s bloodied thumb moving between Wena’s eyes.

*Take Wena.*

Alaric released the root.

The river seized him again.

He kicked towards the place Wena had vanished, unable to see her through the spray. The current rolled him sideways. When her head broke the surface beside him, he reached before she could disappear again.

His fingers passed through her fur and struck leather.

Alaric closed both hands around the collar.

Wena’s paws clawed against his chest. One caught beneath his jaw. Her weight dragged his face below the surface, but he did not release her.

Something broad and dark crossed the water ahead.

A fallen tree.

Its lowest branches vanished into the current while the trunk remained lodged between the bank and a cluster of stones. Alaric twisted until Wena struck it before he did. Her paws found bark. She scrambled, slipped and found it again.

Alaric hit the trunk shoulder-first. The impact emptied him, but he kept one hand locked around Wena’s collar and hooked the other arm over the bark.

For several breaths, neither of them moved. The river pulled. The tree held. Alaric hung between them, coughing water against the bark.

Wena dragged herself higher.

He pushed beneath her chest until her forelegs reached the bank. She clawed through mud and disappeared among the roots. Then Alaric pulled himself along the trunk, one hand at a time, until his knees struck earth.

He crawled out of the river.

Wena collapsed beside him. Water ran from her coat in dark streams. Her sides moved too quickly, each breath ending in a small, wet cough.

Alaric reached for her.

Hoofbeats broke through the trees across the river.

He caught Wena beneath the chest and dragged her back towards the eroded edge. The earth had been eaten away beneath a mass of exposed roots, leaving a hollow between mud and water scarcely deep enough for both of them.

They crawled inside.

Alaric pulled Wena against him and pressed his back into the wet earth. River water rose around his hips. Roots crossed his face like bars.

Torchlight reached the opposite bank and gathered around the marks his feet had left in the mud.

Wena tensed to shake the water from her coat. Alaric wrapped both arms around her and pressed one hand gently around her muzzle.

“No,” he breathed against her wet fur, tightening his arms when a torch turned their way.

Two torches turned towards the bridge while the others spread along the river. One passed close enough to lay a trembling path across the water, then continued downstream.

Hooves crossed the bridge.

The first horse entered the western trees and moved directly above them. Soil fell beside Alaric’s face. A hoof pressed into the earth beyond the roots, close enough that he could hear the leather creak and the animal breathe.

The rider spoke. An answer came from farther inland.

The horse moved on, and another followed.

Then only the river remained.

Alaric continued holding Wena long after she stopped struggling.

At last, she pulled her muzzle from his hand and turned towards the opposite bank.

Beyond the trees, an orange glow stained the low clouds. Smoke crossed the moon in a dark band.

Wena stared at it.

Her ears lifted at every distant crack of wood, every fading hoofbeat, every voice the river broke apart. She waited with her body pressed against Alaric, watching the place where Gerolt should have appeared.

No one came.

Alaric did not know how long they remained beneath the roots. The nearest torches vanished. His hands had gone numb around Wena, and when he tried to uncurl them, pain crawled back through his fingers.

He placed one hand between Wena’s eyes, along the path Gerolt’s thumb had taken.

She looked at him.

“Come on, girl,” he whispered. He could not make the words sound like Gerolt’s. “We have to go.”

Alaric crawled from the hollow, and Wena followed.

They moved beneath the western trees with the river at their backs. Water filled Alaric’s shoes and escaped with every step. His clothing clung to him, heavy and cold, and the pain along his ribs sharpened whenever he drew a full breath.

He kept one hand pressed over the token in his pocket.

Kelmend. The inn near the southern gate. Marta.

He repeated it all silently, afraid of losing any part of what Gerolt had told him. Wena stayed close enough that her wet coat brushed his leg.

The dog stopped.

Alaric almost walked into her. Her head had lowered, and a growl moved through her chest so quietly he felt it before he heard it.

She was looking into the trees ahead, away from the river. Alaric followed her gaze. At first, he saw nothing. Then a man shifted beneath an alder.

He had been leaning against the trunk with one boot braced against the bark behind him, still enough that the weather-dark cloak around his shoulders had become another piece of shadow. He was younger than Gerolt and older than Alaric, dark stubble roughening his jaw. One hand rested close to the short blade at his hip without touching it.

The man looked over Wena, then Alaric. His gaze paused briefly upon the hard shape beneath the wet cloth at Alaric’s hip before travelling towards the lights searching the river behind them. Alaric waited for him to offer help. The man’s hand remained beside the blade.

“So,” the stranger said over the river’s roar. “You’re the one they’re hunting.”

There was no sound.

For one impossible instant, the darkness had been white.

Then it was gone.

Seralune woke with the certainty that something had broken.

For a while, she could not move. Stone pressed against her cheek. Cold had settled into the side of her face and the fingers curled beneath it. Her other arm lay somewhere behind her, tangled beneath the weight of her body and a fall of hair.

She opened her eyes.

The darkness did not change.

She held her breath and listened for someone nearby. When she could hold it no longer, the sound of her own breathing filled the room again.

Seralune drew her trapped arm free and pushed herself onto one elbow. The movement rolled the chamber around her. Her stomach lifted. She lowered her head until the stone steadied beneath her palm.

“Brother?” Her voice crossed the darkness and returned in fragments. She waited for his answer, one hand braced against the stone to stop herself swaying.

No one answered.

She knew her name.

She knew the south garden by the scent the soil kept after rain. She knew which window held the evening sun longest. She knew the tired edge in her mother’s voice after court, and the two quick knocks her brother gave before entering without waiting—as though warning her made the intrusion polite.

Those memories arrived without effort. She tried to picture her mother coming through the door, annoyed that someone had left her daughter alone.

“Mother?” she called, more loudly this time. The chamber gave the word back to her.

Seralune tried to stand. Her knees folded before she had risen halfway, and both palms struck the stone. Pain travelled up her wrists, ordinary and sharp. She tried to rise again before it faded, frightened now by her own weakness.

Her breath shortened. Darkness pressed nearer with every attempt to fill her lungs. She reached for the edge of whatever she lay upon and found only more stone.

*Count.*

Her brother’s voice, remembered as clearly as if he knelt beside her.

*Slowly. One breath first.*

Seralune tried. The first breath caught halfway in, and she had to begin again. She held the next for a moment, then let it out as slowly as she could. She kept her palms against the stone until the trembling eased.

The darkness remained, but it stopped moving closer.

She reached inward, and mana answered without distance or delay. There was so much of it that she could never find where it ended. It filled the hollow places beneath her ribs, pressed behind her eyes and gathered beneath her skin, waiting for a direction she had never been able to give it.

Seralune asked for the smallest thing she could imagine.

Light.

The power rose. She waited with one hand held before her face, straining to see even its outline.

Not a spark appeared.

The familiar failure hurt more here than it ever had beneath the patient eyes of physicians and tutors. Then, she could leave the lesson and find the sun waiting beyond a window.

Here, there was only her useless abundance and the dark.

She lowered her hand. There was no use sitting here waiting for something she had never been able to do. She let the mana settle and began searching the stone around her.

Shallow lines had been cut into the stone around her. She followed one with her fingertips until it met something smooth set into the groove. Metal, perhaps, though it lacked the chill she expected. The strip had split cleanly near its centre. Its two ends curled away from each other like something broken beneath strain.

More fractures waited beyond it.

Seralune crawled along the pattern, tracing curves she could not see. Some continued beneath her fingers. Others ended in fractures radiating from the place where she had been lying. A loose fragment shifted beneath her palm and fell against the floor.

**Tink.**

Seralune pulled her hand back. The small sound travelled much farther than she expected, leaving her listening to the dark again.

This was not her room, nor any chamber in the palace she remembered.

She tried to remember what had happened before she woke.

Her father’s council bell sounded from the eastern tower. Her mother complained that the kitchens had ruined the pears again. Her brother leaned against her doorway and promised he would return before evening.

The memories were clear. Their order was not. She tried to place herself after her brother’s departure, doing some ordinary thing that might lead to whatever had happened. Each attempt brought her back to the stone beneath her hands.

She reached the edge of the carved circle and lowered one foot. The floor waited a short distance beneath it.

Her legs trembled when she placed weight upon them, but this time they held. She stood with both arms stretched into the darkness and moved until her fingers struck a wall.

The stone was so smooth that she could find no joint in it. She kept one hand against it and followed the wall.

Seven careful steps brought a corner. Six more brought another. The chamber was smaller than its echoes had made it seem. There were no hanging curtains, no shelves and no furniture. She found no window. Nothing disturbed the wall until her fingers entered a narrow vertical seam.

“Hello?” Seralune pressed her face close to the seam and waited for footsteps. There were always people beyond her door. Someone should have heard her by now.

“Can anyone hear me?” She straightened despite the trembling in her knees. “I need someone to open this door.”

Silence waited beyond the stone.

Seralune searched beside the seam for a latch. Her hands found only wall. She pressed against it with one shoulder, then both palms.

Nothing moved.

“Brother!”

Her voice broke upon the second syllable.

She struck the stone once with the flat of her hand.

“I’m here.” Her voice had dropped despite her effort to raise it. She pressed her ear to the seam, straining to hear an answer.

Time lost its shape in the dark.

Seralune walked the chamber again. She counted the corners. Counted her steps. Counted every breath until numbers began repeating without meaning. Twice she returned to the broken circle and lowered herself beside it, only to rise again when stillness became unbearable.

She called until her throat felt scraped hollow.

At last, a faint vibration entered the wall beneath her hand. Seralune went still. It came again, closer, followed by hurried footsteps.

Someone was running towards her.

“Here!” Seralune struck the seam, no longer caring that it hurt her hand. “I’m in here!”

The footsteps did not slow.

They stopped immediately beyond the wall.

For one breath, nothing happened.

Then something struck the other side.

**KRRRACK.**

The chamber shook.

Dust fell across her face. A thin line of light split from the top of the seam towards the floor, and Seralune stumbled away from it.

A second impact followed. The door tore free of its frame and struck the chamber wall hard enough to shatter one corner. White light flooded through the opening.

Seralune cried out and covered her eyes.

After so much darkness, the light possessed weight. It forced tears beneath her lids and glowed red through the fingers she held across her face.

Someone stood inside it.

Tall. Motionless. One arm hung at his side, a lowered blade still held in his hand.

Seralune looked through her fingers. She could not make out his face, but light caught the narrow point of one ear through his hair and the familiar line of his shoulder. She knew him before her eyes could bear to look any higher.

“Thaeroval.” His name left her with the breath she had been holding. She lowered her hands and ran towards him, her knees almost failing beneath the sudden relief.

The floor shifted beneath her weakened legs. She stumbled through the last step and caught herself against his chest, both arms closing around him.

The dark plates across his chest lay cold against her cheek. Beneath them, his breath moved too quickly.

He was here.

Whatever had happened, whatever this room was, she was no longer alone.

Seralune pressed her face against him and waited for his arms to close around her. When they did not, she tilted her face upwards against his chest.

He did not look down at her.

His arms remained at his sides.

All colour had drained from his face. His eyes were fixed beyond her, upon the broken circle and the hollow at its centre.

“Thaer?”

He did not answer.

He did not blink.

His gaze remained locked ahead, his body rigid within her embrace, as though whatever he saw had stripped him of every sense but sight.

For the first time in her life, Seralune saw fear in her brother’s eyes.
