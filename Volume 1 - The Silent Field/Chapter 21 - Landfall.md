# Landfall

Mydea first appeared as a darker line beneath the morning haze.

Seralune watched it from beside the forward mast while the line gathered roofs, chimneys and a wall built close to the sea. Smoke rose inland in narrow columns. Fishing boats moved across the harbour mouth, their brown sails turning one after another towards shore.

Nothing about it resembled an enemy country.

A bell sounded from the harbour. Three measured strikes carried across the water before the wind scattered them.

“Meren,” Neldor called from the stern.

Sailors answered from both sides of the deck. A rope came loose above Seralune and slid through a wooden ring with a dry rush. She stepped away before anyone had to tell her. A week aboard the vessel had taught her enough to recognise when the crew needed the deck more than she did.

Nereth emerged from the forward hatch with two packs hanging from one shoulder.

She had wound a plain grey scarf over her hair and around the pointed tips of her ears. A worn brown travelling cloak covered the clothing Neldor had found for her in Waluna. The scarf shadowed the black-purple marks beneath her left eye without hiding them. Against her pale skin, the scars looked almost painted until one followed their branching path into her hairline.

Seralune crossed to her and took the larger pack, settling its strap over the shoulder of her own brown cloak.

“I was carrying that one.”

“You were carrying both.”

“Only until I found you.” Nereth adjusted the lighter pack before Seralune could exchange them again. “The medicine is in this one. If you drop the food, we become hungry. If I drop the medicine, Nyana will swim after us simply to complain.”

“She would reach the harbour before we did.”

“Then let us spare Neldor the embarrassment.”

Nereth’s humour survived the walk from the hatch. Her strength did not. By the time she reached the mast, the colour had receded from her face and one foot had begun landing more carefully than the other.

Seralune lowered the food pack beside her own boots. “How much does it hurt?”

“Enough that I would rather not discuss it within Nyana’s hearing.”

“Is it worse than last night?”

“No. I’m tired, and everyone aboard has learned to listen when you ask me that question.”

Nereth tugged at the edge of Seralune’s scarf. One pointed ear had pressed a faint shape through the cloth.

“Turn your head.”

Seralune obeyed. Nereth loosened the fold and drew it lower along the side of her face.

“There. Remember which name you are using.”

“Sera.”

“And try to sound less like someone people bow to.”

Nyana arrived carrying a small wooden cup and stopped close enough to hear the final words.

“What are you two whispering about?”

“Whether Sera can survive an inspection without giving anyone orders,” Nereth said.

“I am standing here,” Seralune said.

Nyana gave her the cup. “Then drink. You can defend yourself when we are no longer docking.”

The water tasted faintly of the cask. Seralune finished it while Nyana examined Nereth’s face in the open light.

Nyana had stopped asking permission before checking the scars on the fourth day. Nereth had stopped protesting on the sixth. Nyana’s thumb rested below the lowest dark branch without touching it.

“The colour hasn’t spread,” Nyana said. “Did the pain wake you?”

“Once.”

“Twice,” Seralune corrected.

Nereth turned towards her. “You were asleep the first time.”

“You knocked the water cup over.”

“That could have been the ship.”

Nyana took the empty cup from Seralune. “If the ship begins cursing under its breath, I will apologise to you both.”

She pressed two fingers beneath Nereth’s jaw, counted quietly and stepped back before concern could become another argument.

“No running,” Nyana said. “No carrying the heavier pack because Sera looks away. If the marks become hot or the pain begins moving into your neck, you stop and find someone trained to help.”

“You have explained that every morning.”

“And yesterday I caught you carrying a crate. I’m going to keep saying it until you’re off my ship.”

The harbour bell sounded again.

This time, a yellow flag climbed the watchtower near the end of the northern pier. A white flag followed beneath it.

Neldor left the stern and crossed the deck faster than he had moved at any point during their voyage. His amusement from the morning vanished beneath the habits of a captain approaching a foreign port.

“Customs inspection before unloading,” he told the crew. “Military cargo stays sealed until they clear it. Deren, cover the aft hold. Pol, find the manifest you swore you placed in my desk.”

“It is in your desk.”

“Then find the part of my desk that stole it.”

The crew separated.

Neldor stopped before Seralune and Nereth. His attention went first to their scarves, then to the packs beside them.

“The harbour changed its signal.”

“Is that unusual?” Seralune asked.

“It means they’re boarding every vessel carrying military supplies. You two were not on the departure record when we left Waluna.”

Nyana folded her arms. “They needed treatment.”

“Tell them that. Do not expect it to satisfy them.”

“I have a treatment book. They can read it for themselves.”

Neldor nodded, then addressed Seralune.

“You can wait below while I speak to them. If they decide to compare the record with the people aboard, hiding will become harder to explain than your presence.”

The open hatch waited behind Nereth.

Seralune imagined herself beneath the deck while strangers questioned Neldor about decisions she had asked him to make. He had already carried them across the sea, fed them from supplies meant for his crew and accepted two pairs of untrained hands among people who knew their work.

She lifted the food pack again.

“We will remain where they can see us.”

Neldor accepted the answer with a short nod. “Then remember your name.”

“Sera.”

“And you?” he asked Nereth.

“Nereth. No one here has reason to know it.”

“Nereth, then.”

Nereth’s name still belonged to her. Seralune’s could lead a stranger back to the palace room whose door had been locked from the outside. She repeated the smaller name inwardly while Meren expanded before them.

Sera.

It did not feel false. It felt unfinished.

---

The vessel entered Meren behind two fishing boats and a broad cargo barge riding low with timber.

Stone arms enclosed the harbour on either side. White paint covered the upper courses, bright enough beneath the sun to make Seralune narrow her eyes. Gold circles had been set into the walls at regular intervals. Each held eight straight rays around an empty centre.

The same mark flew above the watchtower.

Mydea’s sun.

She knew Favale had been named for the God of Light. She had expected temples, perhaps statues. She had not expected the god’s sign to be fixed to warehouses, harbour posts and the iron brackets holding signal lamps above the piers.

The ship turned towards a berth on the northern side.

Meren met it in sound. Porters shouted numbers from the quay. Cart wheels struck grooves worn into the stone. Gulls fought above baskets of silver fish while a woman below threatened them with a broom and language that made two sailors laugh.

Humans filled the harbour.

The human beneath the cooper’s awning in Waluna had waited for the street to empty before crossing it. Here, humans filled every street she could see. Men and women moved between ropes, stalls and stacked cargo in clothing made for work rather than war. A child sat on an overturned crate eating a yellow fruit while his father repaired a net beside him. Three old women argued over the price of salt. A young porter balanced a barrel upon one shoulder and sang badly enough that the people near him supplied better words.

No one looked like the enemy from the histories she remembered.

Neldor’s crew threw the first mooring line. It landed across the quay and tightened around a black post. The vessel struck the fenders with a low wooden complaint.

The gangplank had barely settled when two officials came aboard.

The first was a narrow human man whose hair had begun to grey above both ears. He carried a leather ledger against his chest and wore a white tabard edged in gold over an ordinary brown coat. The woman behind him wore Mydean mail and a short sword. A sun-shaped clasp held her pale cloak at one shoulder.

The soldier left her sword untouched.

Neldor met them beside the mast and offered the manifest.

“Captain and owner?” the clerk asked.

“Neldor. Registered at Waluna.”

The clerk opened the folded sheets and checked the seal pressed into the lower corner. “Waluna registry. Natharul military contract.”

“Everything is where the record says it is.”

“That would place you ahead of three ships this morning.”

The clerk handed one page to the soldier. She went below with a sailor while he remained near the mast, reading each line before comparing it with the people on deck.

His count reached Seralune and Nereth.

“Those two are not crew.”

“They worked passage,” Neldor said.

“They are not on your departure record.”

“They came aboard before we left Waluna.”

The clerk raised the ledger slightly. “That is what a departure record is meant to record.”

Nyana stepped forward before Neldor could answer. “One of them could not remain conscious long enough to stand in the harbour office. She needed treatment immediately.”

The clerk examined the scars crossing Nereth’s face. “You are the ship’s healer?”

“Medic.”

“Do you keep a treatment log?”

Nyana disappeared below and returned with a book wrapped in waxed cloth. She opened it to a page marked by a strip of blue thread.

The clerk read the entry without taking the book from her. “Found near Waluna. Severe burns. Poisoning suspected. No household entered.”

“She was unconscious.”

Nereth spoke from beside Seralune. “I was awake for enough of it to wish otherwise.”

The clerk looked between the entry and the scars. “And the second woman?”

“She stayed with the patient,” Nyana said. “Then they both worked.”

“Work does not add names to a ship after it leaves port.”

The soldier returned from the hold. “The cargo matches the manifest. The grain seals are intact.”

“Good. Ask the harbourmaster to hold the unloading permit.”

Neldor’s voice remained level. “For how long?”

“Until I know who entered Meren aboard your ship.”

Men on the quay had begun fastening a lifting frame above the aft hold. They stopped when the soldier raised one hand. Beyond them, another vessel waited for the berth, its captain already shouting towards the watchtower.

Neldor drew a slow breath through his nose. “They have names.”

“Then I expect this will be brief.”

The clerk opened his ledger to a blank page and addressed Nereth first.

“Name?”

“Nereth.”

“Household or family?”

“I do not have one anymore.”

The answer made Nyana press her lips together. Neldor studied the mast above them as though the rigging required immediate prayer.

The clerk dipped his pen into the small inkpot fixed inside the ledger’s cover. “Place of residence?”

“Natharul, until a week ago.”

“Occupation?”

Nereth hesitated. “Lady’s attendant.”

The pen paused above the page.

Seralune understood the mistake before the clerk looked at her.

“To whom?”

“I am no longer in their service,” Nereth replied.

The clerk wrote without showing whether he believed her. “Your name?”

“Sera.”

“Household or family?”

“None I can give you.”

“That sounds different from none.”

“It is the answer.”

His pen stopped again.

The harbour continued around them. Rope scraped stone. Someone on the next berth dropped a wooden case and received an eruption of abuse from inside a warehouse. Seralune became aware of how much attention a quiet official could gather without raising his voice.

“You speak like someone accustomed to having questions end,” he said.

Seralune had spoken to courtiers, physicians and tutors in that same tone since childhood. Inside Natharul’s palace, her rank had carried the weight required to make questions end.

Here it had given the man a reason to ask another.

“I was,” she admitted. “I spoke badly. Ask what you need.”

He set the point of the pen against the page. “Start with Waluna.”

“The southern slope broke open,” Seralune said. “Nereth was injured, and we reached the harbour during the alarm.”

“Then the medic brought you aboard?”

Nyana answered before Seralune could. “I treated her ashore first. Her fever was rising, and I needed the medicines on this ship.”

“The captain agreed to take you both?”

“He did,” Seralune said. “We worked throughout the crossing.”

“Where are you going?”

“Inland.”

“Mydea contains a great deal of inland.”

“We are looking for family.”

Seralune felt the queen’s signet beneath her shirt, held against her skin by its fine chain.

“Do you know where they live?”

“No. I was told to begin near the pilgrim roads.”

Family did not mean living. Her mother might be dead, and the person Cyrandor had promised in Favale might have stopped waiting centuries ago. Seralune could offer the clerk no address because she possessed none.

“You have no travel writ and no passage record,” he said. “Do you have anything that proves what you have told me?”

The silver signet seemed heavier against her chest.

She could place it in his hand. If he recognised the queen’s seal, it might end the questions. It might also give Meren her true name before she had walked beyond the pier.

Seralune left it hidden.

“Only Nyana’s treatment book, the work we did and the people who saw us do it.”

The soldier shifted near the gangplank. Her attention had moved from Seralune to the sealed hold.

“We could question the crew separately,” the clerk said.

“Question them,” Neldor answered. “They all watched Sera lose a fight with a bucket, and Nereth spent half the crossing correcting my labels. They worked.”

Nyana closed the treatment book. “They needed correcting.”

“That is not helping.”

The soldier turned a laugh into a cough before the clerk could look around.

He asked Seralune, “Do you claim any part of the cargo?”

“No.”

“Employment with the captain or his crew?”

“No. Our passage ends here.”

“And the packs? Your belongings, or the captain’s stores?”

“We are leaving the ship at Meren,” Seralune repeated. “The packs are ours. We claim nothing else.”

The clerk resumed writing.

The soldier lifted the manifest and tapped one line with her thumb. “Another hold for the northern camp.”

“That is the contract,” Neldor said.

“People in Dalen have been buying grain by the cup.”

“I know what the cargo is. I do not choose which stores the quartermaster fills.”

“No. You only keep them full.”

“Mara,” the clerk warned.

Mara returned the manifest. “The destination matches.”

The soldier stepped down. Resentment remained in the way she looked at the sealed boards, though she did not direct it at the sailors standing near them.

Seralune had grown up hearing that humans took what elven settlements could not defend. Yet a human village had been left measuring grain while Natharul’s soldiers received holds full of it. Breaking the contract would cost Neldor his ship. Opening the hold would require Mara to disobey her orders. The grain would leave Meren without reaching Dalen.

The clerk dusted the wet ink and read the entry once before closing the ledger.

“Sera. Nereth. Taken aboard at Waluna during the southern alarm, passage discharged through labour, disembarking at Meren for inland travel.”

Hearing the names joined inside an official record gave Sera a weight it had not possessed aboard the ship.

Seralune had watched court records outlive the people who signed them. If someone asked Meren about two women carried out of Waluna, the harbour now had an answer.

The clerk signed the lower edge of Neldor’s manifest. “You may unload after the military quartermaster inspects the outer seals. These two may leave when the gangway opens.”

Neldor took the page. “How long until the quartermaster arrives?”

“Long enough for the next captain to blame you for the berth.”

The clerk and soldier crossed onto the quay.

Workers returned to the lifting frame. The other vessel’s captain began shouting before their boots had reached the end of the pier.

Neldor watched the officials enter the harbour office, then faced Seralune.

“You nearly extended our stay by half a day.”

“I know.”

“You recovered.”

Nereth adjusted the strap across her shoulder. “Do not encourage her. We still have an entire country to cross.”

Seralune lifted the heavier pack. “I will try to become less memorable.”

“Start by leaving before they ask you to demonstrate that hauling knot.”

Nyana struck her brother’s arm with the wrapped book.

---

The quartermaster arrived before noon.

By then, the sun stood high enough to burn the mist from Meren’s inner streets. Cargo left the vessel in nets and wooden slings. Each load received a mark from the quartermaster before entering a line of waiting military wagons.

Seralune and Nereth remained aboard until the lifting frame moved aside and the gangplank opened to foot traffic.

Nyana checked the medicine one final time beside the gangplank. She lifted the stoppered jar from Nereth’s pack and held it towards the sun. Dark liquid moved beneath the wax around its neck.

“Five drops in water when the pain keeps you from sleeping,” Nyana said. “No more than twice in one day. If the marks become hot, the jar will not help. You find a healer.”

“Five drops,” Nereth repeated.

“Count them into the cup before you add the water. If you lose count, start again.”

“I can count to five, Nyana.”

“You tried to tell me walking counted as rest.”

Nereth returned the jar to the pack. “Your bottles are labelled now.”

“They were labelled before.”

“Numbers are not labels if no one else knows what they mean.”

Nyana pulled Nereth into an embrace before she could continue. Nereth’s arms stayed uncertain at her sides, then rose around the medic’s back.

“Find someone better equipped than me,” Nyana murmured. “The moment the scars change.”

“That may be difficult.”

“Promise me you’ll look.”

Nereth’s answer disappeared against Nyana’s shoulder. “Thank you.”

Nyana held her a little longer before letting go.

Neldor waited at the top of the gangplank. Seralune unfolded the sailcloth map across a closed cargo crate, and he indicated the first mark beyond Meren.

“The inland gate opens onto the old coastal road. The broken-bell shrine marks the inland fork. If you do not reach it before dark, stop at the carters’ shelter here.”

His finger touched a square drawn well before the shrine.

“If daylight is failing when you reach the shelter, stay there. The shrine is not worth finding in the dark.”

Seralune glanced at Nereth. “We will stop when she says we stop.”

“Good. Listen to her.”

“I have been telling her that for seven days,” Nereth said.

Neldor folded the map and returned it to Seralune. Sailors called for him from the aft hold, where the quartermaster had begun arguing about the count of oil jars.

Seralune had rehearsed several ways to thank him. Each sounded too small beside a sea crossed and a country reached.

“You did not have to bring us here,” she said.

“Nyana made that clear until refusing became more work than sailing.”

His sister pushed him aside and started down the gangplank with Nereth.

Neldor waited until they were beyond hearing before lowering his voice.

“You almost told that clerk who you were without giving him the name.”

“I know.”

“Do that again in Favale and someone will notice.”

Seralune touched the strap across her shoulder rather than the signet beneath it. “Do you want to know my real name?”

“No.” His answer came easily. “I want to finish unloading before the tide turns. That is already more responsibility than I wanted this morning.”

He looked towards Nyana, who had reached the quay and was checking Nereth’s scarf again.

“Reach the pilgrim road before my sister decides to follow you.”

“Will you be safe returning?”

Neldor examined the military seals, the harbour officials and the sailors waiting for their captain.

“We have the contract and the proper seals. That is as much as I can promise before the return crossing. Go.”

Seralune crossed the gangplank.

The quay did not move beneath her.

After seven days aboard, still stone felt unreliable. Her body expected the ground to rise and received no answer from it. Nereth took three careful steps, stopped and looked down at her own boots.

“I dislike this.”

“The land?”

“Its confidence.”

Seralune laughed before the sound could surprise her.

Nyana kissed Nereth’s forehead, told Seralune to obey every instruction she had given and returned to the ship while she could still do so without changing her mind. Neldor had already disappeared into the argument beside the hold.

The crew returned to unloading. Seralune shouldered the food pack and faced the streets rising beyond the harbour.

---

Meren rose from the harbour in three broad levels.

The lowest belonged to warehouses, rope-makers and fish stalls. The second held inns, workshops and narrow homes built with their upper floors extending over the street. Above them stood the inland wall, its pale towers carrying white banners marked by the golden sun.

Seralune and Nereth followed the military wagons uphill.

Their scarves drew fewer looks among the harbour crowds than Seralune expected. Sailors wore cloth around their hair, labourers covered their mouths against dust and several women beneath the fish awnings had wrapped themselves against the sun. Nereth’s scars attracted more attention. Some people stared openly. Others noticed and found work elsewhere.

The road narrowed beside a warehouse painted with faded blue waves. A wagon blocked half of it while porters loaded sacks under the supervision of two soldiers.

“Those are for the northern camp,” one soldier called. “Keep the marked grain separate.”

A porter dropped his sack onto the wagon harder than necessary. “My street was told there was no marked grain left.”

“Take that complaint to the harbour office.”

“They told us to take it to the quartermaster.”

The soldier looked towards the line waiting behind the wagon. His irritation seemed aimed at the road, the porter and the order he could not change in equal measure.

“If I open a military sack, they hang both of us for theft. Put it on the cart.”

The porter obeyed. The grain still passed his hungry street on its way towards soldiers serving another country’s war.

Seralune continued uphill.

“Is this what the palace meant by contracted support?” she asked once the wagon lay behind them.

Nereth kept her reply quiet beneath the market noise. “Yes. I thought it meant coin and supplies. No one ever mentioned towns measuring grain by the cup.”

“I never asked.”

“Neither did I,” Nereth admitted.

At the next crossing, a small sun shrine occupied the corner between a bakery and a cooper’s yard. It held no statue. A polished gold disc caught the light above a stone basin filled with clear water. People touched two fingers to the surface and then to their foreheads before continuing into the street.

A woman carrying bread guided her daughter’s hand through the motion. The child splashed too hard, wetting both sleeves. Her mother scolded her, wiped the water with the corner of her apron and gave her the smaller loaf to carry.

Seralune watched them disappear into the crowd.

“You have never seen a sun basin,” Nereth said.

“Only drawings in a tutor’s book. The people inside them were kneeling.”

“Drawings prefer people who remain still.”

The road climbed again. Nereth’s pace shortened before she admitted the pain had returned. Seralune led them beneath an awning where baskets had been stacked beside a closed dye shop.

“We could remain in Meren tonight,” Seralune said.

“You want to leave.”

“The clerk wrote us into his ledger.”

“He also allowed us off the ship. Leaving before we can walk properly would waste that success.”

“Can you reach the shelter Neldor marked?”

Nereth looked uphill towards the inner gate. “How far is it?”

“He said before the shrine. The map places it several hours beyond the wall.”

“Several hours drawn by a man who crosses decks without falling over buckets.”

“The bucket moved.”

“Of course.”

Nereth rested her pack against the wall and pressed the heel of her hand below the lowest scar. She did not hide the calculation from Seralune.

“I can walk for another two hours,” she said. “Then we stop whether the shelter is in front of us or still hiding inside Neldor’s imagination.”

“Two hours.”

“And we rest once before the gate.”

Seralune set down the food pack. “We rest now.”

They shared dried fruit from the provisions Neldor had given them during the crossing. Around them, Meren continued without waiting for their decision. A cooper rolled an iron band across his yard. Two apprentices carried a painted sign above the street and struck it against the same balcony three times. Somewhere behind the dye shop, a baby began crying and was answered by a tired song.

Seralune ate slowly.

She had expected Mydea to feel foreign through its strangeness. Instead, much of it unsettled her because she understood it. People worked, argued, worshipped, worried over food and carried children through streets built long after her own world ended. A thousand years had changed the lettering above their doors. It had not changed the way a mother dried a wet sleeve.

Nereth tied the pack closed. “If we remain here much longer, you will begin studying the baskets.”

“Their weave is different from Natharul’s.”

“You have begun studying the baskets.”

They returned to the road.

Near the inland wall, the military presence thickened. Horses waited in a stone yard beside covered wagons. Notices had been fixed to a long board beneath the gatehouse, several stamped with the same sun Seralune had seen across the harbour.

One announced that the western road would close to civilian carts at dusk. Another ordered travellers to yield the centre of the road to military convoys. A third listed villages required to surrender horses, lamp oil and preserved food under emergency requisition.

The names meant nothing to Seralune; the amounts did.

An older cart driver stood reading the same notice while his mule pulled at an empty feed bag. He removed his cap and slapped dust from it against his thigh.

“Is the road to the broken-bell shrine still open?” Seralune asked.

He turned towards her scarf, then Nereth’s marked face. “Open enough if you’re walking. The army took the main road this morning. Stay behind their dust and you’ll reach the broken bell.”

“Is there shelter before it?”

“Old carters’ roof. South side of the road, past the chalk pits. Half the wall fell last winter, but the well still works.”

Neldor’s map had not lied.

“Thank you,” Seralune said.

The driver replaced his cap. “If you hear wagons behind you, get clear before they reach you. Those drivers will not stop. Keep off the centre stones.”

He led the mule towards the harbour.

At the gate, two passenger carts waited beneath a painted board. Seralune asked the woman collecting fares how much it would cost to reach the shrine.

“Three silver each,” the woman said. “Four if the soldiers close the road before we pass the chalk pits.”

Seralune possessed no Mydean coin.

The signet touched her skin when she shifted the pack. Her mother’s closed lily had once opened doors throughout Natharul. Here, revealing it might buy a seat, an arrest or a message sent across the sea.

“We will walk,” she said.

The woman had already turned to the next traveller.

Seralune and Nereth approached the inland gate on foot.

The guards checked that they carried no trade goods and waved them through with the other travellers leaving after the convoy. One complained about the heat. The other asked Nereth whether she could see well enough to walk the road. She told him she could, and he called the next person forward.

Beyond the wall, the road divided fields bleached gold beneath the afternoon sun. Dust hung in the distance where the military wagons had passed. A line of low hills softened the horizon, and somewhere beyond them waited a shrine with a broken bell, the pilgrim road and Favale.

Seralune stopped beneath the gate’s outer arch.

From there, she could see part of Meren’s harbour between the rooftops. Neldor’s vessel remained at the pier, reduced by distance to a mast among dozens. One of its sails had begun to rise.

Nereth followed her attention. “If Nyana sees us looking back, she may decide we require another examination.”

“Then we should leave while Neldor can still stop her.”

They stepped beyond the shadow of the wall.

The white-and-gold banner above them opened in the sea wind. Seralune adjusted the heavier pack, made certain her ears remained covered and matched her pace to Nereth’s without taking her arm.

Together, they began the road towards Favale.
