# What Freedom Costs

The cell remained open between them.

Darcy looked at it before she looked at either man.

“Lock the door.”

Silas kept one hand upon the iron frame. “You have not heard why we came.”

“Marta sent you.”

The certainty in her voice stopped him.

Darcy pulled the sleeve down over her forearm. The cloth caught against one of the newer burns, and something tightened beside her mouth before the mark disappeared.

“She learned about the transfer.” Darcy looked at the armour Silas had stolen, then at the blood drying through Alaric’s wrappings. “She could not come herself, so she sent the only man in Kelmend foolish enough to enter a fort before dawn—and someone the garrison would not think to fear.”

Her gaze remained upon Alaric’s hands.

“You should not have made it this far.”

“But we did,” Silas replied, impatience tightening his grip upon the door. “Hear us before you decide what that means.”

Darcy’s gaze shifted from him to the open cell. “You came to take me somewhere.”

“If you choose,” Alaric said.

Darcy’s eyes returned to him.

He stepped inside the cell, placing the open door behind him. Darcy’s sleeve covered the burns now. He could still see them in his mind—some narrow enough to have been made by a single finger, others curving around her arm.

“Marta can get you beyond Kelmend,” he said. “She has provisions waiting beyond the wall. Food, coin and a road prepared.”

Darcy did not move.

“You would not belong to the fort anymore,” Alaric continued, hurrying before she could refuse. “You could go where you wanted. Somewhere they couldn’t do this to you.”

Darcy’s gaze moved beyond him, through the open door and along the corridor she had not been permitted to walk. Her fingers tightened upon the desk. Not much. Enough to draw the skin white across her knuckles.

Then she released it.

“No.”

Somewhere above the cell, a door opened.

Boots crossed the command level. They stopped, changed direction and crossed it again.

Silas glanced through the open cell door.

Darcy did not.

Alaric had expected fear. He had expected questions, suspicion or a request for proof that Marta truly waited somewhere beyond the stone.

He had prepared himself to persuade her. He had not prepared for the answer to sound final.

“You don’t understand what they’re going to do to you,” Alaric said, disbelief forcing the words out too quickly.

“Captain Gilmot has spent years ensuring I understand exactly what he intends.”

Silas entered and drew the cell door closer without shutting it. His attention stayed upon the corridor while he spoke. “We heard him with the Natharul officer. If you disappear, he intends to punish the Faint quarter.”

Darcy’s expression did not change.

Alaric heard the command room again.

*Its adults will be suppressed.*

*Fire companies will clear the structures.*

The paper turning beneath Gilmot’s hand.

The flame bending towards him.

“If you leave,” Alaric said, “he will say the entire quarter helped steal you. He told the elf he would burn it before the second morning bell.”

Darcy lowered her eyes to the papers upon the desk.

“He said the adults would be suppressed,” Alaric continued. The next words resisted him. “The elf asked about the children.”

One of Darcy’s ink-marked fingers stopped against the edge of a map.

“What did Gilmot say?”

Alaric swallowed.

“That there are always children.”

Darcy took one breath. Then another.

Neither reached far enough.

“The woman who carried laundry to the command rooms had two sons,” Darcy said. Her voice had grown quieter. “The older one charged Wind cores. The younger worked near the ovens whenever the kitchen allowed him close enough to the heat.”

Her thumb rubbed once across the ink upon her forefinger.

“I always knew when she was coming. One wheel struck the floor differently from the others. Before she reached my door, she would lift the left side to stop it complaining.”

“I refused a river assault last winter,” Darcy said. “The thaw had broken the eastern bank. Gilmot wanted three companies across before the water rose. I told him the bridge would fail beneath the second.”

Her gaze remained upon the map, though the lines beneath it belonged to some other country and another battle.

“He brought her upstairs and fastened an iron collar around her throat. Every time my quill stopped, he heated it. When I wrote, he allowed it to cool.”

Alaric’s fingers closed.

The dried blood inside the wrappings cracked against his skin.

“Did he—”

“I completed the order.” Darcy’s voice remained level. The effort required to keep it there showed only in the hand pressing harder against the desk. “He let the collar cool. Then he asked whether I had learned.”

Her next breath caught before she could hide it.

“He heated it again before I answered.”

Alaric could not make himself ask the rest.

Darcy looked down at the map. “The bridge failed beneath the second company. He changed the record before it reached the capital.”

She said nothing about the woman.

She did not need to.

“He has killed to correct me before,” Darcy continued. “He will do it again. If I disappear tonight, he will not begin with one person.”

“Then he’ll hurt you whether you stay or not.”

The force of Alaric’s answer surprised both of them.

Darcy looked up.

“Yes.”

No self-pity entered the word.

That made it worse.

“Marta can get you beyond his reach.” Alaric stepped closer. His ankle failed to follow cleanly, and pain drove up his leg. He caught the edge of the desk before his leg could give way. “If you stay, they put you on that ship. They take you to a frontier where you’ll plan another war for them.”

“And if I leave, hundreds of people become the punishment.”

“Then warn them. Give them time to run.”

“Run where?” Darcy’s composure finally cracked. She pressed both hands against the desk and leaned towards him. “They live outside a fortified town. Gilmot gave them until the second bell. Half the adults have spent the night emptying themselves into the core above us, and the rest would be carrying children. Tell me which road they should take.”

Alaric looked towards the maps.

None of them showed the Faint quarter.

“Marta knows people,” he said.

“Does Marta have somewhere to hide hundreds of people?”

He had no answer.

Darcy held his gaze. “Do you?”

“No.”

The admission came through his teeth.

He saw the broken well waiting beyond Kelmend. Marta’s cart. Wena beneath the canvas. A road leading somewhere he could not name.

Darcy could have given that road a direction.

Instead, she had placed an entire district across it.

“I know what staying means,” Darcy said. “I still can’t leave them to him.”

Alaric’s throat tightened.

“We came all this way.”

The words escaped before he understood what they revealed.

Silas’s head turned slightly.

Darcy studied Alaric’s face.

“No,” she said. “You came for more than me.”

The Wind core pulsed somewhere above them. Its vibration passed through the desk and into Alaric’s palms.

He could have lied.

The fort was waking. The cell stood open. Marta had sent him to help a woman choose freedom, and he had allowed himself to believe the choice would lead directly towards his own past.

“Marta said you might know where I should look,” he whispered.

Darcy waited.

“I don’t remember who I am.”

The admission thinned his voice until the next words could barely leave it.

“T-there was a battlefield. Thousands were dead. Humans. Elves. Beastfolk. Machines larger than carts.”

The cell walls drew closer around the memory.

Mud against his face.

Crows tearing at what armour had failed to protect.

The perfect circle where the ruin stopped.

“A-and I was the only one alive.”

Darcy’s hand left the map.

“Where was the battlefield?” Darcy asked.

“On a farm east of Kelmend. Gerolt’s farm.” Alaric forced himself to continue. “Gerolt found me there.”

Her attention sharpened at the name.

“Where is he now?”

Alaric looked down at the blood on his wrappings.

“H-he’s dead.” His voice broke around the words. “He died so that I might live.”

Darcy closed her eyes.

The lines upon her face did not break. They simply became harder to hold.

“Marta knows?”

“I told her.”

Darcy opened her eyes again. Grief had entered them, but the fort allowed no room in which to place it.

“And she offered to help you find what happened.”

“Only after I agreed to carry her offer.”

Darcy’s eyes did not leave him.

“But you came hoping I would help you.”

“Yes.”

The admission hurt more for being freely given.

“Then this was never only about freeing me.”

“——No.”

He made himself look at her.

“——But Marta didn’t promise me you’d help. I just hoped you would.”

Darcy held his gaze long enough that he felt every selfish part of the truth remain visible inside it.

Silas moved beside him.

A muffled order passed through the ceiling. Another pair of boots joined the first.

“We are out of time.”

He looked towards the corridor, then at Darcy.

“Marta is waiting beyond the wall,” Silas said. Urgency roughened his voice, but he kept it low. “Come with us now. Once you are outside Kelmend, no one chooses the road for you.”

“I said no.”

“You are frightened of what Gilmot will do. That is not the same as wanting to stay.”

“I know what he’s doing to me, Silas. I know.” Darcy’s voice rose, and she forced it down again. “That doesn’t mean he’s bluffing about those people.”

Silas’s jaw tightened.

“If we leave you here, he puts you on the airship.”

“Yes.”

“He will burn you again.”

Darcy’s fingers touched the hidden bands beneath her sleeve.

“Yes.”

Silas took one step towards her.

Darcy did not move away.

“Your decisions always leave someone else to bury the dead.”

Silas stopped.

Silas flinched. Alaric had heard Marta speak to him like that, and he had gone quiet then too.

Then he took another step.

Alaric stepped into his path.

His injured ankle almost betrayed the movement. He placed his weight upon the other leg and remained between them.

Silas looked down at him.

“Move.”

“No.”

“You think leaving her here is kindness?”

“No.” Alaric’s voice trembled. He held it there anyway. “She answered.”

Silas could have moved him with one hand.

Alaric knew it.

Darcy knew it.

Silas’s eyes shifted past him towards Darcy’s covered arm.

The cell remained open.

He stepped back.

No apology followed. Darcy did not offer him gratitude for stopping before he touched her.

Above them, something heavy crossed the command level.

Dust loosened from the ceiling and settled across the maps.

Darcy looked at Alaric.

“Describe the machines.”

He did not understand the change quickly enough.

“What do you mean?” Alaric asked.

“The machines upon Gerolt’s farm. Describe them.”

Alaric searched the field inside his mind.

“Metal,” he said. “Silver in places. Some were shaped like carriages, but there were no horses. Their sides had no rivets I could see. No joins.”

“Did they carry weapons?” Darcy asked.

“I think so.”

“Tell me what you saw. Even if you don’t know what it was.”

He tried again. “A long opening in the front of one. Like a mouth made for fire. Another had metal limbs beneath it. They were broken, but the ground around them was burned.”

Darcy crossed to the maps pinned upon the wall. Her legs steadied more quickly this time, though one hand hovered near the desk in case they failed again.

She removed a pin from the corner of an unused sheet and turned the paper over. A broken quill scratched a circle into its back.

Inside it, she drew a smaller one.

“Behind the thickest armour, near the centre,” Darcy said, keeping her voice low. “Look for an orb small enough to carry.”

She darkened the inner circle until the quill tore the paper.

“The centre will be black if its mana is spent.”

“What is it?”

“A war core. Soldiers feed their mana into it to power the machine.” Darcy glanced towards the door as another tremor passed through the fort.

“Why would an empty one tell me anything?” Alaric asked.

Darcy looked back at the black centre she had drawn.

“The first mana placed inside leaves an impression. Along with the way the shell was made, it may tell us where the machine came from—and how old it is.”

Darcy held the drawing towards him, then looked at his blood-soaked wrappings. She laid it upon the desk instead.

“Remember the shape. Paper will not survive the route I am giving you. Take an unbroken one. If the shell is cracked or the centre has turned to powder, leave it.”

Alaric fixed the shape inside his mind.

“If I find one, can you read it?”

“Not here.”

The answer opened the same hollow as her refusal.

Then Darcy continued.

“My father can.”

“Where is he?”

“Favale.”

The name of the Holy Capital carried distance inside it.

“Ask for Helmi where the western aqueduct reaches the old river road. They will know his manor. He was a Vault Archivist in the Great Library.”

“He no longer works there?”

“They removed him when he objected to them taking me.” Darcy folded the edge of the paper beneath one finger. “They threatened his freedom and everyone in his house. An Eminent Water『Affinity』did not give him the right to keep his child.”

“Will he help me?”

“If he believes I sent you.”

“How do I prove that?”

For the first time, Darcy looked towards something the cell did not contain.

“Before they took me, he promised we would follow the river until it reached the ocean. We would leave Mydea if we had to. Find a shore where no examiner could decide who owned me.”

Her mouth moved around the memory of a smile without becoming one.

“Tell him water always finds its way to the ocean.”

Alaric repeated it once beneath his breath.

Darcy listened until he had every word.

“Then tell him I am still waiting.”

A horn sounded above them.

The note struck the stone and opened through the fort.

**BRAAAAM.**

Darcy’s head turned towards the corridor.

A second horn answered from the eastern wall.

Shorter.

Twice.

Silas reached the cell door.

“That’s the service-yard alarm,” Darcy said, looking at the stolen armour. “They may have found the man you took that from.”

Wood broke somewhere high above the lower cells.

A distant voice began shouting.

Another joined it.

The Wind core surged through the walls. Blue-green light travelled through a copper line above Darcy’s cell and vanished into the next.

**KRRRNNNK.**

The airship pulled hard enough that dust fell in thin lines from the ceiling.

Boots began descending towards the lower cells.

Not one pair.

Many.

Silas drew the sword at his hip.

“Which way?”

Darcy took the drawing from the desk and crossed to the open doorway. She tore it through its centre and pushed both pieces into the caged lamp’s flame. Fire climbed the paper and blackened the ink.

“Return towards the iron gate. Before you reach the gaoler, a narrow passage opens behind the opposite row of cells. Follow it beyond the last door. The inspection grate is at the end. The long key with two broken teeth opens it. Follow the throat down until the water turns east.”

“Where does it leave us?” Alaric asked.

“Outside the eastern wall. Near the Faint quarter.”

“Why isn’t it guarded?”

“Because Captain Gilmot forced me to correct the plans of this fort.” Darcy watched the final piece of paper fold into flame. “I corrected everything except that.”

The first order reached them clearly through the stair.

“Seal the lower cells!”

Silas moved into the corridor.

Alaric did not.

Darcy returned to the desk and lowered herself into the chair. She straightened the transfer order beneath one hand and drew the unfinished map across the space the burned drawing had occupied.

“Lock it,” she said.

Silas looked back.

“They already know someone entered the fort.”

“They do not know you reached me.”

“The grate will tell them.”

“The open door will tell them first.” Darcy lifted the broken quill, though her fingers had begun to tremble around it. “Lock it. They may waste time believing you never reached me.”

Alaric looked at the open space between the bars.

He had crossed the river. Beside Silas, he had entered the fort and left blood through its passages to place that opening before her.

Darcy had chosen what it meant.

Silas pulled the door closed.

The key entered.

**KLIK.**

The sound was smaller than the horn.

It followed Alaric more deeply.

Darcy dipped the quill into the ink.

She did not look up again.

“Go.”

Alaric stepped away from the bars.

The scratch of Darcy’s quill followed him into the passage.

Silas moved first, keeping close to the cell doors as he led them back through the lower corridor. The stolen armour appeared darker away from the lamps. Blood had dried along one edge of the breastplate, and every hurried step made the plates complain against a body they had not been fitted to hold.

Alaric’s own disguise no longer concealed much. One patched knee had split at the seam. The guard’s blood had dried almost black across the coarse grey tunic, and the wrapping around Alaric’s right hand had begun to loosen. A narrow tail of blood marked the stones behind him whenever his fingers brushed the wall.

The prisoners watched them pass.

The old man had stopped dividing his bread. One piece remained between his fingers while the other rested untouched upon the floor.

Farther down, the woman still held the child beneath her coat. The child had woken. A pair of eyes watched above the cloth as Alaric passed.

The gaoler’s keys struck Silas’s thigh.

Once.

Again.

Every cell heard them.

No one asked him to stop.

That made walking past worse.

Alaric kept his eyes ahead, but the small sounds followed him. A shift of chain. Cloth moving over stone. Someone drawing a breath and holding it until the keys had gone by.

Near the iron gate, Silas turned into the narrow passage Darcy had described. It ran behind the opposite row of cells and ended at an iron grate scarcely wider than his shoulders. Rust had gathered thickly between its bars. A shallow channel disappeared beneath it, carrying black water into a darkness too low for either man to enter standing.

Silas pulled the stolen helmet from his head and unhooked the dead guard’s alarm horn from his belt. He left both against the wall.

Silas crouched before the lock and found the long key with two broken teeth. It entered but refused to turn. He eased it back while boots gathered beyond the gaoler’s stair.

“Find out why the lower bell did not answer.”

“Captain wants every cell counted.”

“Then wake Veller.”

“He should be awake.”

Silas reversed the key and tried again. The lock clicked. He pulled, and the grate’s lower edge dragged over stone.

**SKRRRRAAAAPE.**

The sound tore through the passage.

Every voice upon the stair stopped.

Silas seized Alaric by the back of his shirt and pushed him towards the opening.

“Inside.”

Alaric lowered himself into water that smelled of rust and old filth. There was no room for his knees. He pulled with his forearms, dragging his swollen ankle over the grate, and bit into his sleeve to stop himself crying out.

Silas entered backwards, one hand keeping the grate raised while the other searched for the lock from within.

Boots reached the lower passage.

“Veller?”

Silas lowered the grate.

The iron met stone with a muted knock. He turned the key once, removed it and let the ring fall into the channel between them.

Water closed over the keys.

“Here!” a voice shouted beyond the bars. “The inspection grate!”

Silas turned in the shallow recess, wrenching his borrowed armour past the stone.

“Alaric.” His hand struck the sole of Alaric’s boot. “Go.”

Iron rang behind them. Alaric dragged himself around the first bend, where the lamp glow disappeared and his loosened bandage caught beneath his hand. Cloth peeled from the wound. He felt blood enter the cold water but could not stop to bind it.

The ceiling forced his head sideways. His cheek pressed against wet stone, and the river returned to him: the cold around his wrists, his feet leaving the ground, his next breath somewhere he could not reach.

*Not again.*

He found a seam between the stones and pulled towards it. Another followed. At the third, his hand found no floor. He slid into a wider basin, water closing over his chest, and struck his head against the arch as he surfaced.

“Quiet,” Silas hissed from the passage.

Alaric pressed both hands over his mouth.

The basin divided beneath three stone mouths. Water entered through two and left through the third, carrying the current towards the east. A thin iron grating had been set into the ceiling. Dawn had not reached it, but lamplight moved across the narrow gaps as soldiers ran through a corridor above.

Silas forced himself out of the channel. He landed heavily beside Alaric, one knee disappearing beneath the water. The borrowed breastplate had bent inwards along his ribs.

For several breaths, neither man moved.

Voices gathered beyond the grating.

“Bring the lamp closer.”

“There’s blood.”

Alaric looked down.

The black water hid his hands.

“Not here. Along the wall.”

Boots crossed directly above them.

Another voice answered from farther away, younger and suddenly unsteady.

“Sirden?”

The name passed through the grating.

Alaric stopped breathing.

The guard returned to him upon the service-passage floor. His weight above Alaric. His forearm crushing his throat. His breath hot with anger. The dagger between them. The final tightening of the fingers around Alaric’s hand before they opened.

He had been given a name after dying.

Sirden.

“He’s dead,” someone said above them.

**THOOM.**

The basin pulsed with Alaric’s heartbeat.

“Captain needs to see this.”

“The prisoners?”

“Still locked. Veller’s alive. They went through the drain.”

**THOOM.**

Sirden’s hand closed around Alaric’s wrist again.

*Wait.*

The word had been Alaric’s.

He had spoken it after the dagger entered, too late to be a warning and too helpless to become mercy.

Water moved against his chest. He knew he should follow it east. Darcy had told him to. Silas was already searching the third opening. His body understood every danger still around them.

It did not move.

A hand closed over his shoulder.

Alaric flinched so violently that water struck the roof.

Silas held him there. The man’s face had lost what little colour the darkness allowed it, and pain gathered beneath the sharpness of his eyes, but his grip did not tighten.

“Stay close to me, Alaric,” he said, his voice low enough to belong only to the space between them. “We survive this first.”

Alaric looked once towards the grating.

Then he moved.

They entered the eastern throat as iron began striking the locked grate behind them.

The eastern channel let them rise to a crouch. Blue-green light pulsed through cracks above the water as the airship strained against its chains. Alaric kept one hand upon the wall until the next bend brought them against iron.

An older grate had collapsed diagonally across the drain. Its frame had twisted between the walls, leaving a gap above the water no wider than Alaric’s head. Branches, rags and years of filth had packed themselves against the bars. The current pressed through in thin, angry streams.

Alaric tried the nearest bar.

It did not move.

Silas reached over him and pulled once. Rust flaked into the water, but the frame held.

Behind them, a faint metallic crash travelled along the tunnel.

The inspection grate had opened.

Silas looked at the narrow space above the blockage, then at Alaric’s shoulder.

“You will not fit,” Silas said.

“Neither will you.”

Silas studied the rusted joint rather than answering. “I only need enough room to turn one shoulder.”

Silas pulled the stolen glove from his right hand and set his bare palm around the corroded joint holding the grate to its frame.

Orange light appeared between his fingers.

The drain changed colour.

No flame rose from his skin. The iron began to glow beneath his hand, and water hissed against it.

Steam burst from the water.

**HSSSSSSSS.**

Alaric turned his face away. Heat touched the old blood upon his bandages and tightened the cloth around his wounds.

Silas’s breathing roughened.

The joint changed from black to red.

“When I tell you,” Silas said through his teeth, “push the lower bar.”

Footsteps entered the drain behind them.

Water splashed beneath several boots.

“Light ahead!”

Silas’s fingers trembled around the iron.

“Now.”

Alaric braced his left boot against the wall and drove both hands into the bar. Pain opened across his palms. The grate held, and his swollen ankle folded, throwing his shoulder against the frame.

“Again, Alaric!”

He thought of Darcy returning to her desk.

Of the woman holding the child behind iron.

Of Sirden’s name arriving too late to change what Alaric had done.

He pushed until something inside the wall screamed.

**KRRRAAANG.**

The heated joint tore free.

The grate twisted beneath the current. Silas caught it before it could fall towards them and forced the lower edge aside. The opening remained narrow, but it was enough.

“Through.”

Alaric ducked beneath the broken frame. Jagged iron caught the back of his labourer’s shirt and ripped it from shoulder to spine. He landed upon the other side and turned immediately.

Silas had not moved.

His right hand remained curled against his chest. Blisters shone across the palm. The orange light had gone, leaving his fingers unable to close.

The approaching lamps turned the water behind him pale.

Alaric reached through the gap.

“Give me your arm.”

Silas looked at the offered hand.

For one moment, his expression became unreadable.

Then he gave Alaric his left wrist.

Alaric pulled while Silas lowered himself beneath the frame. Armour struck iron. The breastplate caught. Silas twisted, failed and disappeared beneath the rising water.

The lamps behind them brightened.

Alaric planted both feet despite the agony in his ankle and pulled again.

Silas tore free with a splash that threw water across the roof.

A spearhead struck the grate where his head had been.

“Move!”

They ran as well as the drain allowed.

The water fell away first. Stone became mud beneath their boots. Cold air reached Alaric through the darkness, carrying smoke, damp canvas and the distant smell of cooking grain.

The drain mouth waited beyond a final bend.

Grey morning filled it.

Alaric climbed towards the light.

The culvert opened beneath the eastern wall of Kelmend. A broken cover lay beside it, half concealed by reeds and a bank of discarded timber. He gripped the outer stones and pulled himself into the ditch beyond.

Air entered his lungs without striking a ceiling.

For a moment, that was all he could feel.

Then the eastern road answered with hooves.

Many of them.

Silas emerged behind him and dragged the broken edge of the drain cover across the opening. It concealed nothing from anyone who knew where to look, but it darkened the mouth enough to steal a few breaths.

“Down,” he said.

They pressed themselves behind the timber.

A Natharul company rode towards the gate.

Their armour held the thin dawn in pale lines. Layered voices moved among them as they approached—one sentence divided between several mouths, meaning passing from rider to rider faster than ordinary speech could carry it.

At their front rode a woman with white bandages crossing half her face.

The cloth had darkened around the ruined left eye. Beneath it, the wound Gerolt had made pulled her brow and cheek into a shape that had not existed at the farm. One arm remained bound against her body. She held the reins in the other.

Alaric knew her before the horse turned its head.

The river returned cold around his waist.

Gerolt stood alone beyond the wheat.

The woman’s remaining eye passed over the ditch.

Moved on.

Then returned.

It found Alaric between the timber.

Her entire body changed.

Shock opened her face for less than a heartbeat. Fury closed it again, so complete that the layered speech around her broke apart.

“——There!”

Her heel struck the horse.

The animal left the road before the riders behind her understood the command. Mud rose beneath its hooves. The woman leaned low over its neck, her bound arm unmoving while her good hand tore the reins towards the ditch.

Silas seized Alaric’s collar.

“Run.”

They broke from cover.

The eastern wall curved ahead of them, its outer foundations crowded by lean-tos, drying frames and narrow paths that had never been measured wide enough for horses. Alaric followed Silas between two sheds as the Natharul rider reached the ditch behind them.

The reins fell across the horse’s neck as her good hand opened.

Earth cracked beneath the horse.

**KRROOM.**

The path lifted.

A ridge of packed soil tore upwards beside Alaric’s boot. He threw himself forwards as it closed across the gap, striking the wall where his ribs had been. Mud and broken stone filled the air.

Silas turned and raised his uninjured hand. Fire flickered once across his fingers, then vanished. The heat he had forced into the grate had taken more than skin.

“Keep moving,” he snarled, anger aimed as much at his hand as the rider behind them.

They entered the first line of shelters.

The horse could not follow.

The woman left the saddle before it had fully stopped. Her boots struck mud. One knee failed beneath her, but she caught herself against the wall and rose again.

“Seal the eastern paths!” several Natharul voices called behind her. “The dark-haired human——alive——the older one——”

The woman did not wait for the command to finish.

She came after them.

Alaric ran.

His ankle had passed beyond pain. It bent beneath him without warning and recovered only because the next step demanded it. Each breath entered too shallowly. The torn labourer’s shirt clung to his back while cold morning moved through it.

Silas remained half a pace behind. Whenever Alaric stumbled, his hand was there, shoving him onwards before the woman could gain on them.

The path divided around a row of washing vats. Silas took the narrower side. Alaric followed and nearly collided with a man carrying two empty buckets upon a pole. The man flattened himself against a shelter without speaking. Blue-green bruising ringed both of his wrists. The same colour had marked the workers beneath the airship.

They passed a woman binding cloth around a child’s foot. The bandage had already been washed enough times to lose its colour. Three more children slept beneath an awning beside her, pressed together for warmth beneath one torn blanket.

No one asked why armed men were running through their homes.

People simply moved aside.

They knew what happened when they did not.

Behind Alaric, the Natharul woman entered the path.

Her shoulder struck a roof support. Canvas collapsed between them. She tore through it with her good hand, shouting a command in the layered tongue. The riders behind her took a heartbeat too long to carry it onwards.

Silas pulled Alaric around another corner.

The shelters thickened.

They leaned against the outer wall of Kelmend as though the town had shaken them from itself and left them where they fell. Timber ended in different places. Roofs had been made from canvas, hammered tin and pieces of broken doors. Thin smoke escaped through holes cut above small cookfires. Water gathered beneath every walkway because no drain had been built to carry it away.

A girl no older than ten stood beside one of the fires, stirring a pot with both hands. A raw red line encircled her throat.

Alaric looked back once.

The white bandages had disappeared behind the collapsed canvas.

They would not remain there.

“Which way?” he asked, breath breaking the words apart.

Silas searched the roofs. “The broken well lies beyond the eastern kilns.”

“Is Marta there?”

“If she reached it.”

*If.*

They kept moving.

The first morning bell sounded from within Kelmend.

Its clean note travelled beyond the wall and over the roofs the town had refused to count among its streets.

People woke beneath it.

A man with one leg dragged himself from a doorway and reached for a crutch repaired with wire. Two boys carried a sleeping woman between them because neither was tall enough to bear her alone. Near a gutter, an old woman divided a heel of bread into five pieces, each too small to hide within her palm.

Hundreds of people had fit inside two words beneath the fort.

Here, the number would not remain whole.

Bruised wrists.

Empty bowls.

Children already awake because hunger had ended their sleep before the bell could.

Darcy had looked through an open door and seen every one of them.

Then she had asked Alaric to close it.

He followed Silas deeper into the Faint quarter while the alarm continued behind them.
