# Foramen

The second fire disappeared when Alaric started down the ridge.

It returned three turns later, lower than before and farther to the left, burning between walls that leaned towards one another without touching. The first remained hidden behind a tower whose upper floors had folded across the street.

Silas led them along the ridge until he found a descent that had once been a road. Most of its fitted stones had split beneath pale roots. Sand filled the gaps, smoothing the centre into a narrow path that wound towards the surviving outer district.

“Someone has moved in,” Silas said.

Redd adjusted the strap of his travelling sack. “Unless the creatures have learned to cook.”

“The last time I came through here, that district was open to the wind. No wall. No roofs.”

Alaric followed the direction of his chin. Below them, three low buildings had been joined by a wall of brown blocks. The blocks did not match the grey stone surrounding them. One section had been laid recently enough that its colour remained darker than the rest.

“How long ago were you here?” Alaric asked.

“Twelve years. Maybe thirteen.” Silas stepped over a root lifting through the road. “I didn’t mark the day.”

“Were the creatures already here?”

“Some of them.”

Redd moved nearer to Wena. “I was hoping for more than that.”

“You wanted the comforting version.”

“I was hoping age had made you generous.”

“It hasn’t.”

The city rose around them before they reached its lowest street.

What had appeared crowded from the ridge became impossible to hold inside one view. A broken arch stood above the road with enough space beneath it for six wagons to pass side by side. Beyond it, narrow houses had been built against the outer wall of a hall whose columns were thicker than Gerolt’s cabin. Their roofs had fallen long ago. Ribbon-trunked trees occupied the rooms, roots passing through doors and returning through windows.

Alaric kept expecting the scale to become ordinary once he walked inside it.

It did not.

A stair climbed the face of one building and ended against open air. Stone channels crossed above the street, carrying nothing. Faded colour remained beneath one archway where rain could not reach: blue figures painted around a circle of missing plaster, each holding something that time had taken from their hands.

Wena stopped beneath them.

Redd allowed the rope to slacken while she smelled the ground. Dust whitened her nose. She sneezed twice and continued.

The missing half of the city remained visible between the buildings. Every street angled towards it. The nearer they came, the more Alaric noticed the pull in the walls—the upper stones shifted farther than those beneath them, the windows narrowed on one side, the metal gutters stretched into points.

Pressure gathered behind his forehead.

It was slight enough to mistake for thirst. He drank when Redd passed him the bottle and returned it without mentioning the pain.

Silas had begun studying the windows instead of the road.

“Do you see anyone?” Alaric asked.

“No.”

“They can see us,” Redd said.

Silas did not ask how he knew.

A piece of stone struck the road in front of Silas’s boot.

It had come from above. Alaric found no movement among the roofs before a woman called from somewhere behind the broken arch.

“Stop there.”

Silas stopped.

Redd shortened Wena’s rope and brought her beside his leg. Alaric kept his arms away from the sword at his hip.

“We only need shelter for the night,” Silas said. His voice travelled farther than it should have and returned thinly from the empty windows. “We’ll pay for food and a place away from the road.”

“There is no road here.”

“A room, then. A yard. Anywhere we can get out of the wind.”

The point of a spear appeared above the wall to their right.

Another rose from a roofless room ahead. A man stepped into the doorway holding it with both hands. He wore a patched brown coat and no armour. The spear shook hard enough to scrape against the stone beside him.

More people emerged after him.

An elderly woman carried a woodcutting axe. A boy younger than Alaric held a bow with no arrow against its string. Two men blocked the nearest side street with a board between them, though neither end reached the walls.

They did not resemble bandits preparing an ambush.

They resembled people who had been given enough warning to become afraid.

The unseen woman spoke again. “Put down your swords.”

Silas turned his head slightly, counting the places from which the voice might have come.

“No.”

“Then turn around.”

“Dark will catch us before we clear the basin.”

“That is your mistake.”

The man in the doorway swallowed. His spear lowered towards Silas’s chest.

“Let the two behind you come forwards,” he said. “You can leave without them.”

Redd stared at him. “Without us?”

“Both of you. The dog as well.”

Silas glanced back at Alaric and Redd. His left thumb hooked beneath the strap crossing his shoulder as though he meant to pull his pack higher. “They’re already free to walk wherever they want.”

“He says that now,” someone muttered from the roofless room.

Redd pushed his hood back. “He’s spent a week telling me where to sleep, when to walk and when to keep my hand away from my sword. I can understand the confusion.”

Silas gave him a flat glance.

“But no,” Redd continued. “He doesn’t own me. He only complains as though he paid too much.”

The spear in the doorway stopped shaking for one beat. The corners of its owner’s mouth twitched before he forced them flat.

Alaric unfastened the belt carrying his sword.

“What are you doing?” Silas asked.

Alaric placed the sheathed weapon upon the road and stepped away from it.

“Pick it up.”

“They warned us before throwing anything sharper than a stone.”

“That doesn’t make the next thing harmless.”

“No. It means they want us to leave more than they want us dead.” Alaric faced the man in the doorway. “My name is Alaric. Redd is my friend. Silas brought us through the Expanse because he knows the way to Favale. None of us came here looking for you.”

The people along the walls exchanged quiet words. Alaric could not distinguish them.

“What about the dog?” the elderly woman asked.

Redd rested the end of the rope across Wena’s back. “She belongs to herself. She mostly chooses me.”

Wena smelled his boot.

“A convincing demonstration,” Silas said.

Stone clicked somewhere beyond the barricade.

The people nearest the side street moved aside.

At first Alaric thought another wall had shifted behind them. Grey-white fur caught the last light where stone should have been. A long muzzle emerged beneath a hood of faded cloth, followed by shoulders broad enough to fill the passage.

The beastman walked upright with a metal-banded staff in one hand. Old scars crossed his muzzle and disappeared into the fur beneath his left cheek. He wore pieces of armour over one shoulder and forearm, none belonging to the same set. The rest was brown cloth wrapped and belted for the dry country.

He stopped behind the board blocking the street.

The top of it reached only to his thigh.

Redd’s hand moved towards his father’s sword.

Alaric reached across and caught his sleeve before he touched it.

The beastman’s muzzle turned first towards Redd’s hand, then Alaric’s.

“If they had come to collect us,” he said, “there would be chains behind them.”

His voice was quiet. The people nearest him lowered their weapons before anyone issued an order.

The man in the doorway did not. “Kurdag, he has more Fire than anyone at this wall. I can feel it from here.”

Silas’s expression did not improve. “I can hear you. I also haven’t used it.”

“You have not needed to.” Kurdag stepped around the board. His clawed feet made little sound upon the sand. “Why are you travelling to Favale?”

“We have business there,” Silas said.

“People usually call it business when they don’t want to explain themselves.”

The satchel pressed against Alaric’s coat beneath his cloak.

Silas did not offer another answer. “We need one night. At dawn, we leave.”

“We don’t take coin.”

“What do you take?”

Kurdag glanced towards the residents lining the walls. “What we need. What we can use. Sometimes a promise that our fires were never here.”

“No one will hear about them from us,” Silas said.

Silas unfastened the smaller pouch tied beneath his pack. “We have clean bandage cloth. Marta packed salve as well.”

“You may need both before you reach the capital.”

“We have enough to share.”

Alaric did not argue.

Redd crouched beside his travelling sack. After searching beneath the spare shirt, he drew out a small cloth roll containing waxed thread, with two steel needles pushed through its edge.

“I have these,” he said. “They were meant to keep my coat together, but the coat seems determined to die.”

Kurdag examined the frayed cuff at Redd’s wrist. “It has made considerable progress.”

Redd looked up at him. “I like you already.”

The corners of Kurdag’s mouth drew back enough to show the ends of his teeth. It took Alaric an instant to recognise the expression as amusement.

“You may enter until morning,” Kurdag said. “Your weapons stay sheathed. If anyone asks you to leave their home, you leave it. If you see something you do not understand, you ask before touching it.”

He turned towards Alaric’s sword.

The elderly woman picked it up first. She tested its weight, then held it beneath one arm.

“That one stays with me until morning.”

Silas started to object.

Alaric answered before him. “All right.”

The board was pulled away.

Kurdag stepped through and waited for them to follow.

“Welcome to Foramen,” he said.

---

Foramen had been built inside the rooms of the dead city.

Brown walls divided the surviving halls into smaller homes roofed with stretched cloth or reeds around open courtyards. Scavenged doors had been cut down to fit their new frames.

The fires Alaric had seen from the ridge burned inside clay-lined pits, each with a metal cover close at hand. As the strangers passed, children were called from the street and bundles disappeared through doorways.

“They will need time,” Kurdag said.

“So will we,” Silas replied.

“You have had practice being unwelcome.”

“More than most.”

Redd walked close enough to Wena that the rope remained loose between them. “Does Foramen mean anything?”

“In Kozmagari,” Kurdag said. “An opening through which something survives.”

Redd glanced towards the missing half of the city. “Cheerful language.”

“The old name died with the people who used it. We needed another.”

They crossed beneath a channel supported by cracked pillars. Water travelled through it in a line no deeper than Alaric’s finger. Three women stood upon a platform beside the nearest cistern. Each held both hands above a stone bowl. Moisture collected against their palms, gathered into separate drops and fell.

One drop.

Then another.

A child waited with a clay jug. When the bowl filled enough to pour, he carried it to plants growing inside a roofless house. The soil there was dark, unlike the pale ground beyond the city.

“You brought that here?” Alaric asked.

Kurdag nodded. “A sack at a time. There is good earth west of the marker if you know where patrols stop looking.”

At the far end of the garden, two Earth users pressed soil into the gaps of a cracked wall. A boy watching nearby copied them. The ground pushed upwards beneath his hands, lifting a whole row of onions.

One of the women caught the nearest stalks before they overturned.

“Less,” she told him. “You’re supporting the roots, not sending them to the moon.”

The boy muttered an apology. The woman showed him how to spread his fingers and waited while he tried again.

“What classification is he?” Alaric asked.

“We haven’t tested him,” Kurdag said. “His mother won’t have it, and neither will I.”

“Then how do you know what he should learn?”

“We ask what he wants to learn. After that, we keep him from uprooting supper.”

The boy tried once more. This time, only the loose soil moved.

Redd had fallen behind them.

Alaric found him watching the child. His father’s sword hung at his hip. The hand near its hilt reminded Alaric of the ridge of Earth that had risen beneath Redd’s left palm when he saw the Natharul king.

Kurdag noticed him as well, though he said nothing.

They reached a long building whose front had been rebuilt in brown stone. Light escaped through the open centre of its roof. The elderly woman carried Alaric’s sword inside.

A second figure came to meet them.

She was elven.

Her hair had been cut close around one side of her head. Half of her right ear was missing, ending in an uneven scar. A sleeveless working tunic exposed pale lines crossing both arms and disappearing beneath the cloth at her shoulders.

Redd stopped in the doorway.

“You let elves live here?”

The residents inside turned towards him.

The elven woman did not retreat. “I’m an elf, and I live here.”

Redd let Wena’s rope fall against his leg. “Your people burned my village. I watched them do it.”

Silas moved between Redd and the nearest resident carrying a spear. He did not touch either weapon.

Kurdag remained beside the door. “Her name is Orieth. She sits on this council.”

“That doesn’t change what she is.”

Orieth pulled the edge of her tunic away from the thickest scar crossing her collarbone. “The elf who owned me cut away half my ear because I spoke while he was speaking. He never cared that I was elven too.”

Redd’s anger did not disappear. It lost the easy direction it had carried into the room.

“That doesn’t make Natharul innocent.”

“I know.” Orieth returned to the low table and began sorting dried leaves into separate bowls. “You need somewhere to sleep. I was willing to give you one. If sharing a roof with me is too much, leave before dark. I’m not going anywhere.”

“Redd,” Alaric said.

Redd faced him. “You didn’t see what they did.”

“No, I didn’t.” Alaric thought of Gerolt’s cabin opening orange behind him. “One rider escaped Gerolt and returned with more. I watched them burn his home.”

“And if she had been there?”

“Was she?”

Redd started to answer, then stopped. “That isn’t the point.”

“Then what did Orieth do?”

Redd’s voice dropped. “It’s easy for you to separate them. You didn’t grow up watching them burn everything you knew.”

Alaric struggled to answer without raising his voice. “I know I wasn’t there. But she’s standing here offering us a bed, and you haven’t even asked where she came from.”

Redd turned towards Orieth again.

She continued separating the leaves.

“I’m staying,” Redd said.

“Then keep your sword in its sheath,” Orieth replied. “My daughter lives here.”

Redd moved his hand away from the hilt.

He offered no apology. Orieth did not ask for one.

Kurdag entered after him and lowered his staff beside the table.

The council began asking questions.

---

They gave Foramen the smallest useful portion of the truth.

Silas explained that patrols had made the royal road unsafe and that Inrandeel would kill an inexperienced group. He said they were travelling to Favale for information. He added nothing to Alaric’s account of Gerolt’s farm and did not mention the wrapped object in the satchel.

Alaric answered when Kurdag asked whether anyone was following them.

“There were soldiers searching for us near Kelmend,” he said. “We haven’t seen them since we entered the Expanse.”

“Why were they searching?” Orieth asked.

Alaric considered how much of his answer could place Foramen in danger.

“I survived something they don’t understand.”

Orieth stopped sorting leaves. “Do you understand it?”

“No.”

“That is usually when powerful people become most interested.”

Silas placed Marta’s medicine pouch upon the table. “If we had seen anyone behind us, we wouldn’t have come down.”

“Tracks can last for days out here,” Kurdag said. “The ruins give plenty of cover to anyone following them.”

“We changed direction whenever we could.”

“Then we will increase the watches tonight.”

“If you decide we’re too dangerous to keep,” Silas said, “say it before the last light goes.”

“You would not clear the basin now,” Orieth replied. “The creatures beyond the outer streets begin hunting after sunset.”

Redd glanced through the doorway. “What keeps them from coming in here?”

“If we knew that, I would have put it on the council table before the bread.” Orieth returned to the leaves. “They stop before the inhabited streets. We make sure no one is outside those streets when they do.”

A thin human woman with her left leg splinted between two pieces of wood collected the medicine and Redd’s needles.

Orieth offered bread, roasted roots and a place inside an old storage hall. Silas asked what they owed.

“You already paid,” she said. “If your medicine is useless, we will reconsider.”

“Marta packed it.”

Orieth lifted one shoulder. “I don’t know Marta.”

“Then smell it again. She knows what she’s doing.”

For the first time since Redd entered the room, Orieth nearly smiled.

They ate in the central courtyard beneath the open sky.

The evening meal belonged to everyone. Pots came from separate homes and returned empty. A woman with Fire barely strong enough to hold a flame above her thumb moved between the cooking pits, relighting tinder whenever the wind took it. Two Wind users drew smoke away from the covered rooms in uneven breaths. Every few minutes, the air reversed and pushed it back beneath the roofs. People coughed, complained and continued eating.

Redd sat with Wena between his feet.

Orieth’s daughter appeared beside the table after the first bowl had been passed around. She looked four or five, with black hair tied away from her pointed ears by a strip of red cloth. When she extended one hand towards Wena, Redd drew the dog closer. The girl stopped.

Orieth called from beside the cooking pit. “Leave the dog alone, little one.”

Redd looked from the girl to Orieth, then released the rope. “Wait. Let her smell your hand first.”

The child crouched and presented her knuckles.

Wena smelled them. Her tail struck Redd’s boot once.

“She hasn’t rejected you,” Redd said. “Food will settle the matter.”

The girl took a piece of roasted root from her bowl and offered it.

Wena accepted before Redd could object.

“You’ve ruined the process,” he told her.

The girl smiled and buried both hands in the fur around Wena’s neck. Wena leaned against her hard enough to make her sit, and Redd caught the bowl before it overturned.

Orieth watched from across the room. She said nothing. Redd kept one hand near Wena’s collar while the child stroked her back.

Alaric ate the bread in his bowl and listened to the cistern receiving water outside.

Drop after drop struck the channel.

Each drop remained small enough to ignore. Together they had filled the covered reservoir beyond the doorway.

Here, Darcy’s three drops would have joined all the others.

Kurdag lowered himself onto the end of the bench, his shoulders still level with several people standing nearby. “You’ve been watching them for a while.”

“I’ve never seen Faints living like this.”

“Most people have not. That is how we remain alive.”

Alaric watched a Water user carry an empty bowl back to the platform. “They took a woman in Kelmend from her family because she could only produce three drops.”

“What is her name?”

“Darcy.”

“Where is she now?”

“The army keeps her in a cell because they need her.”

Alaric glanced around the roofless hall. “Is everyone here Faint?”

“Most were called that. A few were never tested. Some arrived with enough power that another city would have offered them rank.”

“You?”

Kurdag’s claws rested across the end of his staff. “Especially me.”

“Why do they listen to you?”

“Because I know how to find water where the ground appears dead. Because I can hold a wall while people escape beneath it. Because I’ve made enough mistakes to listen before I decide.” Kurdag indicated Orieth with the end of his staff. “And because she reminds everyone that I am not the only person in Foramen with an opinion.”

Orieth spoke without leaving the fire. “He requires frequent reminders.”

“There. You see how tirelessly she serves.”

The child beside Wena laughed.

Kurdag rose. “Walk with me, Alaric.”

Silas had been checking the strap around his burned hand. He stopped.

“Where?”

“The edge.” Kurdag addressed Alaric rather than Silas. “If he wishes to see it.”

Only when Kurdag named the edge did Alaric notice how long the pressure had remained behind his forehead.

“Why?” Alaric asked.

“Because you have spent the whole evening measuring the distance.”

Silas shifted upon the bench. “We leave at dawn. He doesn’t need a tour through unstable ruins.”

“No,” Alaric said. “But I want to see it.”

Silas studied the darkening streets beyond the doorway. “Stay inside the watch line.”

“We will,” Kurdag promised.

Alaric stood. The satchel remained hidden beneath his cloak.

Redd was showing Orieth’s daughter where Wena liked to be scratched. He glanced up when Alaric left, but did not call the dog away from the child.

---

The inhabited district ended three streets from the vanished ground.

No wall marked the boundary. The brown repairs simply stopped. Whole doorways stood open without cloth or wood behind them. The fires of Foramen remained visible between the ruins, reduced to small orange points behind them.

Kurdag carried no lamp. He crossed the broken floors without hesitation and tested uncertain stretches with the metal tip of his staff. Alaric followed where it had already passed.

The headache worsened.

It reached backwards from his forehead and spread above his ears. He continued until the houses opened around a broad stone avenue.

The avenue ended halfway across the city.

There had been no collapse.

Fitted stones ran beneath Alaric’s boots and stopped along a line too straight to belong to damage. Beyond it, the earth fell sheer into darkness. The opposite side lay so far away that the remaining buildings appeared pale and narrow beneath the last daylight. Past them, the land rose again and continued towards the centre of the Expanse.

A house stood beside the edge with two rooms exposed.

One contained half a stair. Each step ended in a smooth face where the missing portion should have continued. The other room held a stone table divided through its centre. Nothing lay beneath it. No broken half remained anywhere Alaric could see.

Alaric approached.

Pain drove through his temples.

He stopped beside the house and pressed his tongue against the back of his teeth until the sickness eased.

Kurdag remained several paces behind him. “Does it hurt everywhere in the city?”

Alaric turned.

The question had been asked too precisely.

“How did you know?”

“You keep stopping and pressing your teeth together.” Kurdag planted the staff beside him. “You were doing it on the way down. The water didn’t seem to help.”

Only then did Alaric notice his fingers resting against the smooth edge of the exposed room. He drew them back.

“It started on the road down.”

“Has this happened in other ruins?”

“This is the first one I’ve entered.”

“Do you remember this place?”

Alaric waited for the doorway, the woman’s laughter or the tap of a loose latch. None returned.

“No.”

Kurdag paused close enough that Alaric wondered whether he could feel the same absence Marta had discovered.

“There is another reason I brought you away from the others.”

Alaric thought of the satchel.

His cloak remained closed over it. No light escaped through the cloth.

Kurdag said, “There is nothing coming from you.”

Alaric forgot the headache.

“Besides you, Silas and Marta are the only people still alive who know,” Alaric said.

“Nothing at all,” Kurdag continued. “A Faint presence can be difficult to feel. Yours is not difficult. It is absent.”

“Marta called me Empty.”

Kurdag said the word once, slowly. “Empty.”

Alaric disliked hearing it from someone strong enough to recognise what he was sensing.

“Will you tell the council?”

“Do you want me to?”

“No.”

“Then I will not.”

The answer came without negotiation.

Alaric faced the divided table. “You don’t think they should know what they let inside?”

“They need to know about the soldiers following you. You’ve told them that.” Kurdag rested upon his staff. “The rest can wait until you want to tell them.”

“The rest of the world would not agree.”

“Then the rest of the world can keep its opinion. A demon lord’s soldiers killed my daughter.”

Kurdag’s voice did not rise. He moved to the vanished line and examined the darkness below as though confirming that it had remained where he left it.

“She was five,” he said. “They entered our settlement before sunrise. I had enough Earth to tear their road apart. Enough to bury the first rank. None of it put me beside her when she needed me.”

Alaric did not ask how she died.

“A woman found me after the fighting,” Kurdag continued. “She had been tested Faint. Her Earth could barely disturb the dust beneath one hand. She crossed a burning house to pull the stones away from my face.”

The metal bands around his staff had been repaired in several places. Kurdag rotated it until one of the seams faced his palm.

“She tore her hands getting me out. I told her to stop. She told me to save my breath.” Kurdag rubbed a repaired band upon the staff. “I had been ready to stay under those stones.”

Alaric thought of Gerolt opening his door to a stranger covered in blood. Darcy remaining in her cell while the captain held the Faint quarter over her. Redd giving up the house he could no longer safely enter and laughing about its chimney.

“What happened to the woman?” he asked.

“She survived. She refused to follow me here. Said living beyond every road sounded exhausting.” A low laugh roughened Kurdag’s final words. “She was usually correct.”

The daylight faded another shade. Across the absence, a stretched tower cast its shadow into empty air.

“No one in Foramen will measure you,” Kurdag said. “That doesn’t mean everyone will know what to do with you. Tell them when you’re ready, not because you think you owe them.”

“I don’t know when that will be.”

“Then don’t tell them tonight.”

Alaric stepped nearer to the vanished line.

The pain sharpened at once.

His heartbeat struck inside his head.

**THOOM.**

The divided room blurred. He forced his eyes open and found the table still cut, the stair still leading nowhere, the absence offering no face or voice in return.

**THOOM.**

Kurdag’s staff crossed in front of him before his foot reached the final stone.

“That is close enough.”

Alaric held where he was. Pain crowded behind his eyes, but his legs remained beneath him.

“What happens farther in, nearer the centre?” he asked.

“There is a crater large enough to swallow several districts. The land around it changes while you watch. Paths do not always return to the same place.”

“Is there a way around?”

“Not one I would call safe. Going around the western edge would add days, and patrols have begun moving where they did not move last season.”

“We don’t have days to add.”

“Being in a hurry won’t make that pain safer.”

Alaric stepped backwards. The next beat of his heart arrived without filling his skull.

“Favale is beyond the Expanse.”

“You still have to reach it alive.”

“The royal road has soldiers searching for us. Silas says Inrandeel would kill us.” Alaric waited until the divided table held one shape again. “That leaves this.”

Kurdag’s ears angled back. He watched Alaric steady himself beside the wall.

“Then stay away from the centre,” he said. “If the pain changes, tell your companions before it puts you on the ground.”

Alaric nodded.

“Has it put you on the ground before?”

Alaric glanced back towards the distant fires.

“Yes. I’m trying not to make it a habit.”

“Good. Begin by walking back before dark.”

Kurdag lifted the staff from Alaric’s path.

They left the vanished city behind them.

---

Foramen’s lights had multiplied while they were gone.

Small flames occupied windows cut into brown walls. Wind users guided smoke through the open roofs in pale streams. At the cistern, the women had finished their work and covered the channel. Someone played a three-stringed instrument beside the largest cooking pit, repeating the same unfinished melody while children argued over the missing note.

Alaric and Kurdag entered the main street together.

Silas waited outside the council building. “You took your time.”

“He asked too many questions,” Kurdag said.

“That sounds like him.” Silas checked Alaric’s face. “Are you all right?”

“I had another headache near the missing ground.”

“A memory?”

“No. Just pain.”

Silas glanced towards Kurdag.

“It eased when we came back,” Kurdag said. “I wouldn’t take him near that edge again tonight.”

“I’m considering whether to tie him to the next camp.”

“Use a good knot,” Redd called from the fire. “He heals quickly.”

Wena lay beside him with Orieth’s daughter asleep against her side. Redd had placed his folded cloak beneath the child’s head. Orieth sat nearby repairing the frayed edge of a water skin with one of the needles he had given away.

Redd noticed Alaric watching and pointed towards the girl. “I tried moving her. Wena objected.”

Orieth pulled the thread through the leather. “The dog opened one eye.”

“She was very clear.”

The melody reached its missing note and broke apart again. A dry wind entered from the western street, carrying dust across the fire pits.

Alaric crossed towards them.

Wena woke before he arrived.

Her head rose from beside the child. Her nose turned towards the western ruins.

The musician played the first two notes again.

Wena stood so quickly that the girl rolled against Redd’s cloak.

Redd caught her before her head reached the ground. “Easy.”

Wena pulled away from him.

Her fur lifted along her spine. A low sound entered her chest.

Alaric stopped.

He had heard that growl beneath the roots beside the river, when Wena lay against him and the elven riders searched the ground above them.

“Redd,” he said. “Take her inside.”

Wena barked.

The sound broke across the square. The child woke crying. Orieth abandoned the water skin and lifted her daughter away from Wena’s rope.

Wena barked again, fighting Redd’s grip upon her collar.

Silas was already moving towards the western street.

A whistle came from the outer ruins.

One high note.

Two lower answers followed from opposite roofs.

The people around the fire began standing.

A lookout ran beneath the broken channel. He reached Kurdag breathing hard.

“Riders. Five of them.”

“How far?” Kurdag asked.

“Past the western arch. They came through the dead street without lamps.”

Silas drew his sword. “They followed us.”

“Can you be certain?” Kurdag asked.

Alaric watched Wena strain towards the dark.

“She recognises someone.”

Another whistle sounded, nearer than the first.

Kurdag struck the end of his staff once against the road.

“Every light,” he called. “Put them out.”

Foramen moved.

Metal covers dropped across the cooking pits. Water struck coals. Wind folded smoke beneath the roofs before releasing it through hidden vents. The lights that had revealed Foramen began disappearing from the windows.

As Silas passed, he caught Alaric by the shoulder. “Stay with Redd.”

Alaric turned towards the council building.

The elderly woman had left his sword upon the low table.

He crossed the room, fastened the belt around his waist and closed his hand around the worn grip. Outside, Redd drew his father’s blade while keeping Wena behind him. Orieth carried her daughter towards the inner rooms. Kurdag walked west with his staff.

Between two dying fires, five mounted shapes entered the far end of the street.

The last light in Foramen went out before the blade cleared its sheath.
