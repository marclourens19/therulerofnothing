# A War Without Sound

“……re ….u … urt?”

“B… are… y… hu…?”

The words drifted through the darkness, distant and broken, as though the voice had been travelling towards him for longer than any voice should have survived—frayed by the crossing, little more than a memory of itself.

And still, it arrived.

He tried to answer.

Nothing came.

Mud on his cheek. Blood in the air.

Something heavy pressed across his ribs, with the weight of an arm and none of its warmth.

Burnt flesh.

Crows.

He couldn’t remember what his own voice sounded like.

His thoughts moved slowly, dragging themselves through something thick and black, and every time he grasped one it slid out of his hands.

*Where…*

The thought broke apart before he could finish it.

*Where am I?*

Something cold pressed hard against his cheekbone. Wet earth clung to the side of his face, crusted at the edge of one nostril. And beneath it, something slick.

Sticky.

He swallowed. The taste of iron filled his mouth—

*Blood?*

His heartbeat thundered inside his skull.

**THOOM.**

His entire body flinched.

Another followed.

**THOOM.**

He waited for the next beat, unable to think beyond it.

Pain tore through the space behind his eyes, blinding and absolute.

But beneath it lurked something that had no right to be there: the sensation of having been unspooled and wound back the wrong way, as though whatever measured the space between one second and the next had briefly lost count of him.

He tried to lift his hand. Nothing happened.

He tried again. This time his fingers twitched—and even that tiny movement sent a fresh wave of agony rolling through his skull.

*What… happened to me?*

He reached back for whatever had come before the pain.

There was nothing. Not even the memory of having been anywhere else.

“…boy?”

The voice came again from somewhere above him.

Closer this time.

The word boomed through his skull, setting every aching part of it trembling.

“——Boy, can you hear me?”

He tried to open his eyes, and for a moment something opened instead—something that was not sight.

Light, though his eyes were still shut, still crusted with mud. This light did not come from outside him.

Pure white swallowed everything.

And for one impossible instant, he wasn’t lying in the mud at all.

No ground, no sky, no direction—only the unbearable sensation of being stretched thin, dragged towards a place he could never reach.

The light did not blind him.

It felt, absurdly, like it was searching for something. Like it already knew his shape and was hunting for the rest of him in the white.

A voice screamed somewhere in that white, and he could not tell whether it was his own.

Then—

Nothing. His own dark again, behind his own eyelids.

At last, the boy opened his eyes.

The sun was directly above him.

And inches from his face, a dead man’s hand jutted from the mud, its fingers still curled around a spear shaft snapped off halfway up.

He couldn’t turn his head.

His gaze strained as far as it could and found more bodies—some in armour, some in very little. He gasped, drawing air hard enough that his chest seized around it.

Heavy hands grabbed his shoulders.

“Easy! Easy, lad—by the Four, boy, steady—”

Someone rolled him onto his back.

The boy screamed.

**THOOM.**

The movement drove a spike of pain through his skull, each heartbeat driving it like a hammer against a bell.

“——Easy now. I’ve got you.” The hands steadied him instead of pulling again.

He squeezed his eyes shut against the light, his breathing coming in ragged bursts. Beneath the hurt, one fact rose, thin but undeniable: he was alive. He understood the word; he could not remember the life it described.

Something sticky slid from his matted hair and traced the curve of his temple.

He lifted a trembling hand. His fingers came away smeared with mud and dark blood.

A mud-stiffened lock of brown hair sagged across his vision. He pushed it aside with the heel of his palm.

A broad silhouette leaned over him, cutting off the worst of the glare.

“Can you hear me, lad? Where are you hurt?”

The voice was deep and gravelly. Its owner glanced across the bodies even as he waited for an answer.

He tried to answer, but only a dry rasp escaped his throat.

He clutched his head instead, curling as another wave of pain rolled through him.

“Your head?” The man followed the movement, then looked over the blood covering the rest of him. “Hold still a moment, lad. Let me see.”

Rough, calloused hands began searching his lean frame—arms, chest, stomach, legs—pressing along them in search of broken bones or serious wounds.

But those hands paused several times—and he heard the man’s breathing change.

There were no heavy iron plates. No boiled leather. No family crest stamped across a breastplate, and no weapon hanging from his waist. There was only the strange clothing.

The cloth was smooth and soft, nothing like the coarse fabrics the man wore. Stranger still, it fitted him perfectly. Not a seam had split. Not a single tear or puncture marked its surface.

Even the stitching seemed to trouble the old man. He rolled one seam between his fingers, then another, studying stitches so fine and even that the boy could scarcely distinguish one from the next. Whatever the man sought there, his deepening frown said he had not found it.

“Not a mark on you,” the old man murmured. “All this blood, and not a mark.”

He glanced across the field, then back at the boy. His confusion hardened into unease.

“Can you sit up?”

The boy planted one hand in the mud and tried. His arm shook beneath him. Before it could give way, the old man slipped an arm behind his shoulders and helped him upright.

The world smeared into brown, red and white. He lurched sideways, clutching the man’s sleeve to keep himself from falling.

Slowly, the shapes settled. He looked towards the horizon, and his breath stopped.

Scorched earth stretched beneath the pale sky. Bodies lay scattered across it, tangled in broken armour and blackened grass.

None of them moved.

This was no longer a field. It was a graveyard, and the dead lay in their thousands.

Men sprawled across the scorched earth, their armour split and their bodies twisted into impossible shapes. Broken spears jutted from the ash like the trunks of a dead forest. Shattered shields lay half-buried between them.

Above the carnage loomed enormous metallic husks, carriages of war fashioned from seamless silver. He could not have explained how they worked, yet everything about them declared their purpose.

One had collapsed upon jointed metal limbs. Another faced the ruin through a long opening in its seamless front, blackened around the edges as though something had burned through it.

And not all the fallen were human.

Pale soldiers lay among them, narrow ears rising through hair stiff with blood.

Horned, clawed creatures lay among the soldiers, built too broad and heavy to be men. Their fists remained locked around weapons no human could have lifted.

Yet something about the battlefield was wrong.

A horse lay with its forelegs stretched towards a stride it had never completed. Its rider was pinned beneath it, one hand reaching towards a soldier slumped upon his knees. Their fingers rested inches apart.

They had not been overcome. They had been interrupted.

As though death had not fought its way through them.

As though it had simply arrived everywhere at once.

Crows quarrelled between the bodies, their wings beating against the silence. One braced its feet against a corpse and tugged at something beneath a ruined breastplate.

Then he saw the edge of the destruction.

It stopped.

There was no churned approach. No road of trampled earth. No siege line, abandoned camp or trail of broken carts. Beyond the outermost body, the wheat stood untouched—calm rows of gold shifting beneath the wind.

No army had marched into this field. Whatever had happened here, the battle had not spread into it. It had appeared in the middle, whole.

The boy could not look away.

He searched the ruin for something that belonged to him—a familiar crest, a remembered face, anything that might make these people more than strangers.

His mind returned nothing.

He had no idea who these men were, or what they had died for.

“…That was wheat,” the old man said behind him. “When I barred my door last night, all of it was wheat. Hip-high. Two weeks from the sickle, near enough.”

His voice had gone quiet. The boy looked from him to the wheat beyond the bodies.

“You were here?” the boy asked, his throat scraping around the words. “Did you hear it happen?”

The farmer shook his head.

“Fifty winters I’ve slept with one ear open. Wolf worrying the fold, fox among the hens, some bastard testing my fence on a moonless night—I wake. Every time.”

He crouched beside the nearest body, close enough to touch it, but his hand stopped above the ruined armour.

“Last night, nothing. Quiet enough that I lay awake listening to the owls hunt.” His hand fell back to his knee. “All these dead, and not one shout. Not one horn. Not one man crying out for his mother.”

The farmer looked across the bodies. “I’ve walked fields the morning after. Every war leaves its crawlers—men dragging themselves towards anywhere but where they fell. There’s always a trail. Blood. Broken grass. Something.”

His jaw tightened before he forced out the rest.

“There’s no trail here. Not one. It’s as though they were dead before they ever touched my ground.”

The boy looked farther across the ruin.

Then his gaze caught on something even the carnage had not prepared him for.

Most of the ruin could be understood. Iron had split armour and flesh. Fire had blackened the earth.

But not all of it.

Here and there, the ground still hissed with violet light. Tiny arcs leapt between charred stones, searching for somewhere to ground themselves. Elsewhere, frost held the daylight strangely—pale and dim, as though dusk had settled over that patch of earth and nowhere else.

Then he saw the circle.

It lay a little way from where he had awakened, a perfect boundary drawn through the devastation.

Beyond its edge, the earth was burned, frozen or torn apart. Within it, the grass remained green beneath a glass-thin layer of frost. Every blade had been flattened into a spiral that narrowed towards a single point.

There was no crater.

No ash. No blood. No fragment of armour or bone.

Not even a footprint disturbed the grass.

The absence was immaculate.

The boy stared until the sight began to ache somewhere beneath his ribs.

Not because the circle was empty.

Because its emptiness felt wrong.

This was not a place where something had been destroyed.

It was a place where something had been spared.

His gaze settled upon the centre. Nothing stood there now, yet some instinct buried deeper than his missing memories insisted that someone should have.

Then a word surfaced.

Not a guess.

A certainty.

『Magic』

*Why do I know that?*

Something stirred beneath the recognition. It was gone before he could grasp it—not an image or a voice, but the sensation of falling towards something and being torn away before he could reach it.

Then that vanished too.

The battlefield flooded back into his senses.

The stench of blood, ash and opened flesh coated the back of his throat. His stomach clenched. He covered his mouth.

Too late.

He folded forward and retched bile and bloody saliva onto the scorched earth.

“Easy! Easy, lad!”

The farmer was right beside him.

His voice was not.

The first word reached the boy clearly. The second seemed to travel from somewhere far away.

*Lad* faded before it reached him at all.

The world tilted. Crows smeared into black streaks across his vision. The metallic husks bled together, and the violet sparks lengthened into trembling threads.

Rough arms caught him and hauled his limp body over a broad shoulder.

Somewhere beyond the gathering darkness, the farmer continued to mutter.

“…heavy little shite… bleeding all over my good wheat…”

One violet thread remained, stretched thin across the boy’s failing vision.

Then it snapped.

---

When his eyes opened again, the stench of death was gone.

In its place came the earthy sweetness of stewing vegetables and the sharp scent of burning pine.

For a while, the boy did nothing but breathe. Someone had wiped the mud and dried blood from his face. A heavy woollen blanket covered him, and a straw-filled mattress shifted softly beneath his weight.

A stone hearth crackled nearby. Its flames cast long shadows over rough wooden walls and heavy ceiling beams. One shadow seemed to linger after the fire moved.

He blinked. It behaved itself again.

His gaze travelled across a small dining table with a candle burning low upon it, several hanging iron pots and a shuttered window darkened by night. By every measure, it was an ordinary cabin.

He searched for some thread of recognition—a meal eaten at that table, a night spent beneath those beams, the memory of warmth from that hearth—but nothing stirred.

The older man from the battlefield sat on a low stool beside the fire, a dented metal mug cradled between his hands. Exhaustion bowed his broad shoulders.

The blankets rustled.

The man looked over the rim of his mug. When the boy tried to push himself upright, he lifted one broad hand.

“Easy, lad. You went limp on me out there. Give yourself a moment.”

The boy tried to swallow. His mouth felt lined with dust.

“…Where?”

The man lowered the mug to one knee and leaned closer. “What was that?”

“Where am I?”

The man stared into his mug.

“Still on my farm. Mydean soil.” He looked towards the shuttered window before adding, “Name’s Gerolt, if you need one for the fool who brought you here.”

“…Mydea?”

Gerolt’s hand stopped against the rim of the mug.

“Mydea,” he repeated. “The kingdom you’re lying in. Does that name mean nothing to you?”

The boy tried the word silently. It brought him no road, no border, no place he could remember standing. He shook his head.

“By the Four.” Gerolt rubbed a thumb across his mouth. “What happened to you, lad?”

His scrutiny travelled down to the boy’s clothing.

“Can’t say you look like a soldier. Barely twenty winters on you, I’d wager. And what in the Last Dark are you wearing? No iron. No leather. Soft as bed linen, yet not one tear in it after all that.”

Gerolt pointed towards the cuff. “Who made it? I went over every seam while I was cleaning you. Never seen work like it. Not from any trader who’s travelled this road in thirty years.”

The boy lowered his gaze. The dark fabric fitted him perfectly. He turned one cuff between his fingers, hoping the sensation might pull something loose—a pair of hands fastening it, a voice telling him to stand still, anything at all.

Nothing followed. The boy opened his mouth, then closed it. His fingers kept worrying the seam.

Gerolt lowered his hand. “Leave the cloth for now. What’s your name, lad?”

His name.

Surely he knew that. Everyone had a name.

He reached for his and found only the same darkness.

No word surfaced.

No face appeared.

No voice called him back.

His breathing shortened.

“What is it?” Gerolt asked.

“I…”

The boy stared at his trembling hands and closed them into fists.

*Think.*

*There has to be something.*

“I don’t know,” he whispered. He could not make himself look up.

Gerolt’s brow furrowed.

“You don’t know your own name?”

The boy shook his head.

“Where you’re from?”

His grip tightened around the blanket.

The word *home* surfaced without a single place behind it.

*Mother* followed—hollow, a word with no one standing inside it.

“I understand you,” he said, gripping the blanket harder. “I know what things are. But I can’t remember where I learnt any of it. I can’t remember anyone.”

“Nothing before this morning?” Gerolt asked.

The boy shook his head. “There has to be something.” His voice had begun to rise. “I can’t have nothing.”

“Leave it a moment, lad. You’re shaking.”

The boy hardly heard him. His fingers returned to his collar, then the cuffs, then every strip of stitching he could reach. Someone had made these clothes. Someone might recognise them. He found nothing hidden in the lining, but checked every seam twice.

Still nothing. He let go of the cuff before he could check it a third time and looked around the cabin. If there was no answer inside his head, he would have to begin with what remained outside it.

“There’s something else we can try,” Gerolt said. He waited until the boy looked at him. “Your『Affinity』. Reach for it, lad. Tell me what answers.”

The boy frowned.

“My… what?”

“Your element. Fire, water, earth or wind, for most of us. You’re born with it. Losing your memory shouldn’t take it away.”

The boy looked down at his hands. “How do I find it?”

Gerolt tapped his own chest. “Close your eyes. Don’t chase a memory. Reach inward and listen. Tell me what you feel.”

The boy obeyed, plunging into the darkness in search of a spark, a current, a weight, a breath—anything that felt as though it belonged to him.

Nothing answered.

He opened his eyes. Gerolt was watching him over the untouched mug.

“I can’t find anything.”

Gerolt went still.

He lowered the mug to the floor and kept his fingers around its handle after it had settled.

“That’s not possible.”

The boy pulled the blanket closer. “Then tell me what I’m doing wrong.”

“I knew a man who came home from war unable to name his own wife,” Gerolt said. “He could still call his element. It isn’t a memory, lad. You should feel something, even if you cannot tell me what it is.”

“There isn’t anything.” The boy’s voice faltered under Gerolt’s stare. “I tried.”

Gerolt released the mug. His gaze moved towards the door, then back to the boy. This time, he did not lean closer.

“No past. No name. No spark. You’re not merely addled. You’re…”

His voice dropped.

“Empty.”

The word settled between them.

The boy looked down at his hands.

Gerolt rose and crossed to the window. He opened the shutter by a fraction and stared into the darkness outside, one hand braced against the frame. His fingers tightened against the wood.

“I don’t know a single banner on that field,” Gerolt said, still looking through the shutter. “I’ve buried men beneath half the flags in this kingdom, and I don’t know any of those. Men don’t die shoulder to shoulder with beastfolk. Not in any war I’ve heard of.”

He glanced towards the bed. “Wena barks at owls. Barks her fool head off every night of her life. Last night, she made no sound. I found her this morning pressed flat against my door, shaking like a whelp.”

As though answering her name, something beneath the bed gave a faint, miserable whine.

The boy looked down. Two frightened eyes glimmered in the darkness before disappearing again.

Gerolt watched the darkness beneath the bed for a moment. “She’s been under there ever since. Won’t come out, even for me.”

He turned from the window. In the firelight, he seemed ten years older.

“You were lying in the middle of those dead,” Gerolt said. His hand remained braced against the window. “Not another soul breathing. What happened out there?”

“I—” The boy searched for something to give him. Gerolt’s gaze dropped to the fists closed around the blanket.

“Aye. I know.” Gerolt rubbed one hand over his face. “I keep expecting you to tell me.”

The boy’s stomach chose that moment to growl—loudly.

The sound rolled through the silence of the cabin.

He froze and pulled the blanket higher, heat rising into his face.

Gerolt stared at him. For one bewildered moment, neither of them spoke.

Then the farmer released a dry, unsteady breath that almost became a laugh.

“Well.” Gerolt looked towards the pot upon the hearth. “At least I know what to do about that.”

He took a wooden bowl from the counter and filled it with stew.

“Here. Mind the bowl—it’s hot.” Gerolt waited until the boy had both hands around it before letting go.

The hollow ache inside him had become impossible to ignore.

He raised a spoonful to his cracked lips and swallowed.

For a moment, there was only warmth: root vegetables, salted meat and thick broth. Simple, real things.

Then something flickered.

A corridor filled with sourceless light, humming with a low chord no instrument could have produced.

Gone.

A hand that was not his brushing the hair from someone’s face.

Gone.

A single word spoken gently in a voice that felt closer to him than his own heartbeat.

Gone before he could tell whether it had been his name.

Then his mind split open.

Agony.

The cabin vanished.

White light lanced through his vision. It passed through flesh and memory alike, drawing him apart strand by strand.

Beneath the pain came something colder.

The sensation of being counted.

Weighed.

And, for reasons he could not understand, found worth keeping.

Fragments tore through him.

Light.

Too much light.

Someone falling.

A hand reaching.

Then—

“Don’t leave me alone…”

“Please…”

“Don’t take him from me!”

Someone was screaming.

The voice was distorted, worn thin by a journey no voice should have survived. It did not sound as though it had been raised inside a room.

It sounded as though it were bleeding through a tear in the world.

He reached for it.

The words came apart in his grasp.

A hollow rush of cold tore through the vision, and the voice vanished into silence.

His throat seized.

“——hk!! ——ghah…!!”

He lurched forward, his hands spasming.

The bowl slipped from his grasp and struck the floor. Hot stew splashed across the wooden boards.

He did not feel it.

He couldn’t breathe.

The air inside him didn’t exist.

He slid from the mattress and collapsed onto his knees, one hand clutching his chest while the other clawed desperately at his throat.

“Lad!”

Gerolt seized his shoulders.

“Look at me, lad!” Gerolt tightened his hold when the boy’s knees began to slip. “Can you hear me?”

The boy couldn’t answer.

Then the panic stopped.

It did not ease.

It did not fade.

It stopped the way a held note stops when its string is cut.

His hands went slack.

Tears continued down his face, silent now.

He did not know whose voice had cried out with such desolate terror.

He reached inward—for his name, his『Affinity』, the owner of that voice.

Nothing answered.

---

Gerolt remained crouched beside him, one hand firm upon his shoulder.

Then his fingers tightened.

He went completely still, his head turning towards the road.

At first, the boy heard nothing. Then a faint rhythm trembled through the floorboards.

Hoofbeats, far away but closing quickly.

Beneath the bed, Wena’s whimper cut off.

Gerolt reached the window before the boy could ask what was wrong. He pressed one eye to the narrow gap between the shutters.

Whatever waited outside erased the exhaustion from his face, leaving fear in its place. He moved without a word.

The iron poker scraped through the hearth as he raked ash over the coals. Their orange glow disappeared one ember at a time.

Then he crossed to the table and pinched out the candlewick between his fingers, without seeming to feel the burn.

Darkness swallowed the cabin. Only thin blades of moonlight remained between the shutters, and beyond them the hoofbeats kept coming.

Outside, a torch flared to life, its harsh orange glow cutting through the gaps in the shutters and casting long beams across the floor.

One of those beams swept across the boy.

He froze. For one exposed instant, the light lay across his face and chest.

Then he saw Gerolt’s expression.

The boy lowered himself flat against the floorboards and pulled the blanket with him, keeping its folds from rising into the light. Gerolt crossed the room, eased the bench onto its side and drew him behind it.

“Not a sound. Not one—do you understand me?”

“What’s—”

“Riding openly through Mydean fields…” Gerolt breathed. “They shouldn’t be here. Not ever.”

His mouth began to shape a name, but whatever it was, he swallowed it.

The boy’s lips parted. Riders meant people. People meant answers. One of them might know what had happened on that field—might even know him.

He drew breath to call out.

Gerolt’s hand tightened over his shoulder.

The same man who had walked among thousands of corpses without breaking had smothered his own fire and hidden in the dark rather than let these people know he was home.

Gerolt had carried him from the dead, given him shelter and placed food in his hands. Calling out now would make the decision for both of them.

The boy looked once towards the door, then closed his mouth.

He wanted answers. He chose silence.

Outside, the rhythm of the hoofbeats changed and slowed.

Harness shifted. A piece of metal struck softly against another.

Then the hoofbeats ceased.

The boy held his breath. In the darkness, his heartbeat hammered against his ribs, each blow seeming loud enough to give him away.

Then—a voice, murmuring beyond the walls.

The words belonged to no language the boy knew. Their syllables overlapped strangely, as though several throats were shaping the same sounds at once.

Gerolt’s fingers dug harder into the boy’s shoulder. His gaze remained fixed upon the door.

The boy gave up trying to understand the voice and listened to what little he could distinguish beneath it.

A buckle shifted somewhere beyond the wall. A hoof disturbed the earth. Torchlight passed between the shutters, vanished, then returned from a different angle.

The sounds refused to resolve into anything he could picture. He did not know how many people waited outside, where they stood or what they wanted.

So he watched Gerolt.

If the farmer understood the language, his face gave no sign of it. He knew enough to be afraid. For the moment, that was enough.

The boy lowered his hands. Fear pressed against every breath, but he held himself still and waited.

Outside, a stirrup rang softly.

A mount released a long breath as its burden shifted away.

Something dismounted.

The boy waited for the ground to answer beneath its weight.

It did not.

**Tock.**

**Tock.**

**Tock.**

Knuckles against the cabin door.

Gentle.

Patient.

Willing to wait forever.

Gerolt’s eyes went wide, and his breath hitched in his throat. He looked down at the boy, who met his gaze. The boy’s heart battered his ribs and his breath trembled in his throat, but he did not turn towards the door.

*Don’t move.*

Gerolt’s lips shaped the words. No sound came with them at all.

Outside, slow and deliberate as a tolling bell, the knocking came a fourth time.

And beneath his fear, some small, wordless part of the boy went quiet—because he knew, in the same place that had known the word『Magic』, that opening that door would be the last thing he ever did.
