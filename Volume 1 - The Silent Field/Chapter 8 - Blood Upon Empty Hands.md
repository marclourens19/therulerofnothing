# Blood Upon Empty Hands

Knuckles struck the yard door shortly after the third bell of the night.

Once.

Then twice in quick succession.

Alaric looked towards it from the empty inn.

The fire had burned low while he waited. Marta had hidden Wena beneath the canvas of the inn’s provision cart and taken her through the yard nearly an hour earlier. The charcoal coat and bloodstained black clothing in which Alaric had reached Kelmend had gone with them, wrapped beneath the provisions. Only the towels upon which Wena had slept and several pale hairs caught between the floorboards remained.

Wena had looked back once before Marta lowered the canvas. Alaric had watched the cart pass beyond the stable wall without following.

Gerolt had told him to take Wena.

Now he had allowed the only part of the farmer still living to leave without him.

The latch lifted.

Silas entered with darkness gathered around his cloak. Moisture shone across one shoulder, though the windows remained dry. He closed the door carefully, drew the bolt and placed a bundle of coarse grey cloth upon the nearest table.

“She is beneath the fort.”

Alaric pushed himself upright.

Sleep had stiffened every place Marta had wrapped. His ankle resisted the first step. Pain climbed from his heel, entered his ribs and settled beneath his right shoulder before he reached the table.

“You saw her?”

“I saw her name on a transfer order from the lower cells.” Silas pulled off his gloves with his teeth. “The garrison moves her at dawn. Not through a gate. They have an airship tied above Fort Kelmend.”

“An airship?”

Silas looked at him.

Alaric waited for knowledge to surface.

Nothing did.

“A ship held in the air by a Wind core,” Silas said. “You can stare at it after we stop it from carrying Darcy somewhere we cannot reach.”

He opened the bundle. A shapeless tunic lay inside beside a pair of trousers patched at both knees. Neither had been washed recently. An old stain darkened one sleeve, and the cloth smelled of flour, sweat and smoke.

“Most of the fort’s useful men are searching grain sheds for you,” Silas continued. “The rest are preparing the transfer. Faint labourers are still entering through the service yard. Food, lamp oil, waste. Things soldiers require but prefer not to remember exist.”

Alaric touched the tunic.

“How do we get beneath the fort?”

Silas removed a narrow knife from inside his cloak and tested its edge against his thumb. “Through the service yard. I’ll wear a guard’s armour. You carry something heavy and look grateful for the privilege. Once we are inside, there is a lower stair behind the kitchens.”

“And Darcy?”

“Somewhere below that.”

Alaric looked up.

“You don’t know where.”

“I had an hour, a fort full of soldiers and no desire to spend the remainder of my life beneath it.” Silas’s eyes remained upon the blade. “I found an opening. Do not mistake it for a plan.”

The room seemed larger without Marta’s voice inside it. Nothing remained upon the long table except two guttering lamps, Gerolt’s token and the rough clothing Silas expected Alaric to wear.

“How many guards?” Alaric asked.

“I counted six between the service yard and the lower stair,” Silas said. “There will be others I missed, and you cannot fight any of them.”

“And if we can’t reach her?”

“We leave.”

The answer came without thought.

Alaric’s fingers closed around the patched cloth.

“She’ll never hear Marta’s offer.”

“She will hear nothing if we die above her cell.” Silas sheathed the knife. “Marta asked you to speak to Darcy. If the route closes before we reach her, we leave.”

Alaric looked towards the yard door.

Marta had promised him a route away from Kelmend if he refused. By now, she and Wena would be moving beyond the streets the patrols controlled. He could meet them at the broken well and leave before daylight placed his face beneath every guard’s attention.

She had offered that road without contempt. Taking it would not betray their bargain.

Gerolt’s token lay upon the table, its broken shield turned upwards.

Alaric picked it up and forced it into the deepest pocket at his hip. Its edge pressed cold against him.

“Tell me how to walk.”

Silas studied him for a moment.

“Eyes down. Speak only if someone speaks to you, and even then, try not to sound as though you expect an answer. Limp less than you need to. Men notice damaged tools when they think the work may become theirs.”

He pushed the grey clothing towards him.

Alaric pulled the rough clothes over the clean linen shirt and plain trousers Marta had left him. The patched trousers caught upon his wrapped ankle, and pulling the tunic across his swollen shoulder made his breath stop until the cloth settled.

Silas wound stained strips around Alaric’s hands and ankle until little of Marta’s clean white cloth remained visible.

“And if you see something that offends you, remember we are not entering the fort to improve it.”

Alaric met his eyes.

Silas tied the final strip around Alaric’s wrist and held it there. “I mean it. Ignoring what you see will be difficult enough. Do not stop.”

---

Fort Kelmend rose from the northern quarter like something the town had failed to bury.

The surrounding buildings crouched beneath its walls. Four square towers occupied its corners, their upper windows lit by pale blue-green fire. Between them, the central keep climbed above the rooftops and disappeared beneath a shape larger than any ship Alaric had imagined.

Its hull hung against the paling sky.

No water held it. Thick cables descended from either side and vanished into iron drums upon the roof. Long ribs curved beneath its dark body, and a glass-veined pipe entered the lower hull near the stern. Blue-green light travelled through the pipe in slow waves.

With each one, the airship lifted against its restraints.

The cables answered.

**KRRRNNNK.**

The sound passed through the fort, along the street and into Alaric’s teeth.

He stopped beneath the shadow of a closed dye shop.

“What is doing that?”

“The Wind core is still charging.” Silas kept moving.

Another wave entered the hull.

**KRRRNNNK.**

Metal complained above them.

Below, labourers waited in a line beside the fort’s eastern wall. Their clothing carried the same grey-brown shapelessness as Alaric’s disguise. Some held baskets. Others balanced covered pots across their shoulders or dragged carts filled with split wood.

Soldiers entered through the main arch without slowing.

The labourers were sent around the side, where rain and refuse had turned the ground to mud.

Silas drew Alaric into an alley opposite the service yard.

“Wait here.”

“Where are you going?”

Silas raised his collar over the scar upon his face.

“To find a guard whose armour will fit.”

He disappeared behind a row of coal sheds.

Alaric remained beside a stack of empty barrels. The rough disguise covered his replacement clothing from throat to ankle. Stained strips hid most of Marta’s clean bandages, but Alaric could still feel the fresh wrappings tighten whenever his hands closed.

He counted labourers instead.

Seventeen entered while he waited.

None were asked their names.

A guard touched the shoulder of a woman carrying two pails and turned her around without speaking to her. He lifted one lid, smelled what lay inside and waved her onwards. His eyes never reached her face.

Five men descended from a narrow door farther along the wall.

They wore leather harnesses across their chests. Fine copper threads ran from the straps to metal rings clasped around both wrists. Blue-green light remained caught beneath the skin of their hands, fading with every step.

The first walked without assistance.

The second leaned upon the wall.

Two others carried the last between them, his boots dragging through the mud. His head hung forwards. A thin line of blood had dried beneath one nostril.

“Put him by the charge-room door,” a soldier called from beneath the arch. “They may need him again before lift.”

The men carrying him stopped.

“He gave all he had,” one said.

“Then let him find more.”

The soldier returned to the cup warming his hands.

They placed the unconscious man beside the wall.

The line moved around him.

Alaric watched until a hand closed over his shoulder.

He turned too quickly.

Silas stood behind him wearing a Mydean guard’s armour and gloves. The breastplate sat tightly across his chest, and the sleeves ended short enough to expose part of the leather beneath. A round helmet shadowed his brow. It did not entirely hide the pale scar along his cheek.

“If I had been a guard, you would have shouted.”

“Where is the guard?”

“Behind the sheds. Alive.”

He led Alaric behind the coal sheds.

A man lay between the rear wall and a heap of broken crates. His hands had been bound behind him with his own belt. Another strip of leather held cloth inside his mouth. His eyes were open.

They widened when Silas returned.

Silas crouched and tightened the knot around his wrists.

He removed a small ring of keys from the guard’s belt and hooked it inside the borrowed breastplate.

Silas checked the gag, then looked towards Alaric. “The next watch will find him.”

The guard made a wet sound behind the gag.

Silas picked up a wooden crate filled with empty oil flasks and pushed it into Alaric’s arms.

Alaric looked at the man upon the ground.

“You could have killed him.”

Silas adjusted the stolen sword at his hip.

“I had no reason to.”

The answer offered no mercy.

The living man did.

Silas stepped into the alley.

“This armour buys us minutes. Don’t waste them.”

---

The crate hid Alaric’s hands.

It did not hide his limp.

He kept his steps short as Silas led him towards the service entrance. The man beside the door looked at the stolen armour first. His gaze paused upon the breastplate where it strained beneath Silas’s shoulders.

“You’re late.”

Silas struck the back of Alaric’s crate with one gloved hand. The blow travelled through the wood and entered his bruised ribs.

“This one fell twice between the storehouse and Bell Street.”

The guard glanced at Alaric.

Not his face.

His wrapped ankle.

“Replace him.”

“With which of the many volunteers?”

The guard’s mouth twisted. He reached for the crate and shoved one corner upwards without warning. The wood struck beneath Alaric’s shoulder.

Pain opened white across his sight.

He caught the load before it fell.

The guard had already turned back to Silas.

“Take him below when he’s finished. Captain wants the lower passage cleared before the elf comes down.”

“Which elf?”

The question sharpened the guard’s attention.

Silas yawned inside the helmet.

“I have been searching wet sheds since midnight. If Natharul has grown another pointed bastard, I would like warning before I salute the wrong one.”

The guard gave a tired laugh.

“Only one. More than enough.”

He waved them inside.

The fort swallowed Alaric without ever looking at him.

Heat met them first. It carried old food, lamp oil, wet stone and metal held too long near flame. The service passage narrowed between storerooms before opening beneath a stair crowded with labourers.

More copper lines crossed the ceiling. The blue-green light inside them brightened, moved upwards and faded.

The stone beneath Alaric’s shoes began to hum.

It was too low to be heard at first. He felt it inside his injured ankle and the roots of his teeth, a continuous pressure gathering beneath every other sound.

Above them, the airship pulled against its chains.

**KRRRNNNK.**

No one looked up.

Two workers descended the stairs with empty wrist harnesses hanging from their hands. A woman followed them upon shaking legs. The blue-green veins beneath her skin disappeared at the wrists, where the metal rings had rubbed both arms raw.

She missed the final step.

The man behind her caught one elbow before she struck the floor.

“Keep the line moving,” a soldier said.

The man lifted her.

The soldier watched until they continued, then returned to speaking with another guard.

“Coremaster says we’ll be full before dawn.”

“He said that an hour ago.”

“Then send another row upstairs.”

They spoke of bodies as though the bodies were somewhere else.

Silas placed one hand against Alaric’s back and guided him past.

“Eyes.”

Alaric lowered them.

The floor showed him enough.

Bare feet beneath grey hems. Mud dried around thin ankles. A dropped crust of bread crushed beneath a soldier’s boot because no worker dared retrieve it while he stood there.

At the end of the passage, an armoured shoulder struck Alaric and turned him halfway around.

“Watch it.”

Alaric tightened both hands around the crate.

“Sorry.”

The soldier stopped.

Silas did not.

“He apologised to me,” the man called after him.

Silas looked back through the shadow beneath his helmet.

“He apologises to doors. It means nothing.”

The soldier looked at Alaric again, found nothing worth pursuing and continued.

Only after the next corner did Silas slow.

“You sound educated.”

“I said one word.”

“Like someone who expected to be heard.” Silas kept his voice low. “Do it again and I’ll have to treat you the way they expect.”

Alaric felt the stolen sword at Silas’s side without looking at it.

He lowered his head and followed.

---

They found the kitchens by smell.

Steam clouded the first doorway. Knives struck boards beyond it in a rhythm too fast to separate. Servants carried trays through the adjoining passage towards a low eating room where three soldiers remained after the changing watch.

Silas passed the entrance.

Alaric almost did.

A boy emerged from the kitchen carrying a shallow pot against his chest.

He was still a child. His tunic was too large across the shoulders and too short at the wrists, its cloth patched with pieces that did not match one another. An old bruise darkened the side of his jaw.

The pot trembled between his hands.

One of the seated soldiers hooked a foot around the leg of the chair beside him and pulled it into the boy’s path.

The boy struck it.

Broth climbed over the rim. Most fell back inside. A thin stream crossed the soldier’s boot and fell steaming onto the floor.

The room went quiet around the mistake.

The soldier looked down.

Then he smiled.

“You little shit.”

The boy placed the pot upon the nearest table before it could fall.

“Forgive me, sir.”

“You think I wear these so Faint trash can pour supper over them?”

“No, sir.”

“Then why is it there?”

The boy’s eyes went to the chair the soldier had moved.

He did not answer.

The first strike caught him across the mouth.

His body turned with it. One hand found the table, but the soldier kicked his wrist away. He fell beside the spilled broth.

Laughter came from the second man. He leaned across his chair and spat onto the boy’s shoulder.

The third kept eating.

The boy began to rise.

A boot drove into his stomach and folded him around it.

“Fucking trash,” the soldier said. The smile had left him. “Always ruining what stronger hands put in front of you.”

He kicked again.

Alaric stopped.

Silas had already reached the far side of the doorway.

“Walk,” he said without turning.

The boy covered his head.

The soldier’s next kick struck his forearm.

Around them, servants continued moving. One woman crossed the passage with a basket held so tightly against her that the woven edge bent beneath her fingers. A cook inside the kitchen lowered his eyes and began cutting the same piece of bread into smaller and smaller parts.

Alaric placed the crate upon the floor.

“Alaric.”

Silas spoke his name softly.

The warning inside it was not.

Alaric entered the room.

“Get away from him.”

The soldier’s boot lowered.

For one breath, surprise held his face.

Then he laughed.

“What did you say?”

Alaric looked at the boy between his boots. Blood had opened across his lower lip. His eyes remained fixed upon the floor.

“I said leave him alone.”

The second soldier pushed himself away from the table.

“Whose Faint is this?”

Both men looked towards Silas.

The third still held his spoon, but it no longer reached his mouth.

Silas entered slowly.

“Mine,” he said.

“Then teach him his place.”

Silas’s eyes passed over the child. They settled briefly upon the boot resting near his ribs.

“Move away from the boy.”

The first soldier’s smile changed.

“You new to the fort?”

His hand dropped towards the horn at his belt.

Silas crossed the space before it reached metal.

The short blade left his borrowed armour and entered beneath the soldier’s chin. Its point disappeared behind his jaw.

The man’s teeth struck together.

Silas pulled him forwards, using the body to block the second soldier’s view. He tore the blade free and pushed the dying man across the table.

Wood split beneath the weight.

The second guard reached for his sword.

Alaric saw the movement.

Silas had already seen its end.

Fire closed around his empty hand. He caught the guard’s wrist before the blade cleared its sheath.

The smell of burning leather filled the room.

The guard screamed. His fingers opened. Silas turned the arm, drove one shoulder into the man’s chest and struck his head against the stone wall.

Once.

The body loosened.

Silas lowered him to the floor and pressed two fingers beneath his jaw.

“Is he alive?” Alaric asked.

“For now,” Silas said, taking his fingers from the guard’s throat.

The third soldier stood at the far side of the room.

He had drawn no weapon. His eyes moved from the dead man across the table to Silas, then towards the open passage.

Silas lifted the bloodied knife.

“Choose.”

The soldier looked at the boy upon the floor.

Whatever he found there made the choice for him. He raised both hands.

“I saw nothing.”

Silas kept the knife raised. “Into the pantry. Slowly.”

The soldier obeyed. Silas dragged the unconscious man inside after him, closed the door and forced a serving knife through the outer latch.

The first blow against the wood came before he had turned away.

The kitchen beyond had fallen silent. The cook still held his knife above the ruined piece of bread. He looked at the dead soldier, then began ushering the other servants towards the rear passage. No one reached for an alarm.

Whether they were helping or merely refusing to die for men who beat children, Alaric could not tell.

“How long will that hold?” Alaric asked.

“Less time than we need.”

Silas wiped his blade upon the dead soldier’s sleeve.

He caught the body by its collar and pulled it behind the broken table. One arm remained visible beneath the splintered wood.

The boy had drawn himself against the wall. He stared at Alaric with no gratitude in his face. Only terror.

Alaric crouched.

Pain entered his ankle, but he remained where he was.

“Can you stand?”

The boy’s gaze moved to Alaric’s grey clothing.

“You’re not Faint.”

It was not a question.

Alaric looked at the arm beneath the broken table.

“I need to find a woman being moved at dawn.”

The boy’s expression closed.

“I don’t know anyone.”

Silas nodded towards the lower passage. “Water Faint. Kept below. Writes maps for the captain.”

Recognition reached the boy before he could hide it.

Silas saw.

“Where?”

The boy looked at the dead soldier again.

“If they find him here, they’ll say I did something.”

“If they find you beside him, they will blame you whether you speak or not,” Silas said. “Take the broth upstairs. Tell anyone who asks that these men sent you to the charge room. Stay there until the bells change.”

The boy looked at Alaric.

Alaric wished he had something safer to give him.

He had only Silas’s instruction and a room that already smelled of blood.

“Which way?” Alaric asked.

The boy pressed one sleeve against his mouth.

“Past the salt store. There’s a stair behind the ash door. The lower guards use it when they want food without crossing the officers’ hall.”

Silas looked towards the passage they had intended to take.

“That stair was bricked shut.”

“The first one.” The boy’s voice shook. “There are two.”

He lifted the pot again. Both hands trembled so badly that broth lapped against its rim.

Alaric reached to steady it.

The boy pulled away from him.

“Don’t.”

Alaric lowered his hands.

The child left through the rear of the kitchen without looking back.

Silas watched him go.

Then he turned upon Alaric.

“You were told to walk.”

Alaric stood carefully.

“They were going to kill him.”

“Perhaps.” Silas looked at the arm beneath the broken table. “Now a man is dead, two more are one opened door away from raising the alarm and the boy will be questioned if they catch him.”

Anger moved through Alaric, then faltered against the blood slipping from the dead soldier’s jaw.

Silas stepped closer.

“You wanted your choice to matter. Look at all of it.”

Alaric did.

The body.

The open door.

The broth cooling upon the floor where the boy had fallen.

Silas picked up the crate and shoved it back into his arms.

“Now make the time it cost us matter.”

---

The second ash door opened onto a stair scarcely wide enough for Silas’s stolen armour.

They descended beneath the kitchens. Heat withdrew one step at a time. The vibration of the Wind core remained inside the walls, but the kitchen sounds thinned until Alaric could hear only their feet and his own uneven breath.

The crate struck stone whenever his injured shoulder dropped.

Silas took it from him without asking and left it beneath the first landing.

“You said carrying something was part of the disguise.”

“Down here, the crate is only slowing you.”

They reached the bottom.

A narrow service corridor ran beneath the central keep. Lamps occupied shallow cages along one wall. Most had been turned low. The opposite wall contained closed store doors and gaps through which older passages disappeared into darkness.

Silas stopped before the corner.

Voices approached.

He drew Alaric into one of the gaps and pressed both of them against cold stone.

A guard passed close enough that the edge of his cloak entered the opening. He carried a lamp in one hand. An alarm horn hung at his belt, and he scratched beneath his breastplate with the other.

Silas waited until the footsteps reached the stair.

Then the guard stopped.

“Harl?”

The name entered the dark.

Silas did not move.

The guard returned one step.

Then another.

His lamp found the stolen helmet first. Light crossed the cheek beneath it and caught the pale scar.

The guard’s face changed.

“Silas?”

Silas struck the lamp from his hand.

Darkness closed around the sound of breaking glass.

The horn began to rise.

Alaric caught the guard’s wrist with both hands.

The man turned and drove one elbow into his shoulder.

Pain opened his grip. His back struck the wall, then the floor. The impact broke the breath inside him before it could become a cry.

Silas seized the horn.

The guard drew his sword halfway. Silas trapped the blade inside its sheath with one hand and drove his forehead into the man’s face.

Metal struck bone.

The guard staggered but did not fall.

He released the sword, drew a dagger from behind his belt and drove it towards the gap beneath Silas’s breastplate.

Silas caught his wrist.

Both men struck the opposite wall. The borrowed helmet fell from Silas’s head and rolled through the broken lamp oil.

The dagger descended.

Silas held it above his ribs with both hands.

The guard leaned his weight behind the point.

Alaric reached for the nearest leg.

His fingers closed around leather and pulled.

The guard’s boot slid through the oil. His body turned away from Silas and came down upon Alaric.

An arm struck across Alaric’s throat.

The stone beneath him entered his bruised shoulder. Something cracked against the floor beside his ear.

The dagger.

The guard saw it.

So did Alaric.

They reached together.

Alaric’s wrapped fingers closed around the hilt first.

The guard seized his wrist.

His face hung inches above Alaric’s. Blood ran freely from his nose, crossed his lips and gathered at the edge of his jaw. He smelled of lamp oil and old sweat.

He was heavier.

Stronger.

His hand began forcing Alaric’s wrist towards the floor.

Beyond the guard, Silas dragged in a breath and tried to rise.

The guard’s other forearm pressed harder across Alaric’s throat.

Darkness gathered at the edges of his sight.

Alaric twisted the dagger towards the only space between their bodies.

The point touched cloth.

Stopped.

He pushed.

Resistance travelled through the hilt into both of his hands. Cloth gave first. Something beneath it did not. Alaric’s arms shook. The guard’s eyes widened, not with pain yet, but with the knowledge of what Alaric was attempting.

Alaric pushed again.

The blade entered.

The guard’s breath left against his face.

His grip closed painfully around Alaric’s wrist. For one moment, every part of him became rigid.

Then the weight across Alaric’s throat loosened.

Not completely.

The guard looked down between them. His hand found the dagger buried beneath his ribs and closed around Alaric’s fingers instead of the wound.

“Wait,” Alaric whispered.

He did not know which of them he was asking.

The man tried to breathe.

A wet sound entered his chest and remained there.

Blood reached Alaric’s hands. It soaked through Marta’s clean bandages beneath the stained cloth, warm where the river and battlefield had been cold.

The smell returned first.

Mud against his cheek.

A dead man’s arm across his ribs.

Blood that had belonged to someone he could not name.

This blood had a face.

It was looking at him.

The guard’s mouth moved. Nothing left it but another broken breath. His fingers weakened around Alaric’s hand, tightened once more and opened.

Alaric held the dagger where it was.

Removing it felt like another violence.

Leaving it felt worse.

Silas pulled the body away from him.

The blade came free with it.

Blood crossed Alaric’s grey tunic. The guard rolled onto one side and drew his knees towards the wound, one hand pressed uselessly beneath them.

Alaric reached for him.

Silas caught his arm.

“He’s still breathing.”

“Not for long.”

Alaric tried to pull free.

Silas’s grip tightened.

“You saved me.” His voice held neither gratitude nor comfort. “Now move.”

The guard drew another wet breath.

Alaric’s body refused to rise.

Silas hauled him upright. Pain entered his ankle, ribs and shoulder at once. His knees weakened, but the wall held him until his legs remembered their work.

Silas dragged the dying man into the dark opening and retrieved the horn.

Alaric remained in the corridor.

Blood darkened the wrappings around both hands.

He had awakened beneath another man’s blood without knowing how it reached him.

This time, he knew.

Silas returned the dagger to the dead man’s belt.

“Can you walk?”

Alaric looked into the opening.

The guard no longer moved.

“Alaric.”

He forced one foot forwards.

Silas picked up the fallen helmet and pulled it back over his head.

“Good enough.”

They continued beneath the fort.

---

Voices reached them beyond the next stair.

Silas slowed and raised one hand.

The passage widened into a service gallery overlooking part of the command level. Light crossed its floor through a half-open door. Inside, paper shifted beneath someone’s hands.

“The transfer proceeds at dawn,” a man said. “The core will be ready.”

His voice was controlled and unhurried. It carried the expectation that no sentence would need repeating.

Another answered. The voice was precise, every word separated cleanly from the next. Alaric had heard that manner of speaking beneath Gerolt’s broken roof.

Natharul.

“Captain Gilmot,” the elf said, “Natharul was promised a strategist prepared for service, not a disobedient servant to be carried aboard.”

Every part of Alaric stopped except his breath.

Silas’s fingers closed over his bloodied wrist and drew him lower beside the wall.

Through the gap, Alaric saw a long table covered in maps. A man in a dark Mydean officer’s coat stood at its head. Gold crossed his collar and shoulders. One hand rested close to a candle whose flame bent towards him though no wind entered the room.

Across from him stood a slender figure in Natharul armour. Alaric saw only one pale hand, the edge of a pointed ear and gold lines moving across dark metal.

The elf did not stand like a guest.

“The strategist has refused three frontier revisions,” the Natharul officer said. “If she refuses to board?”

Gilmot straightened one corner of the transfer order.

“Darcy understands the provision attached to her stewardship.”

“Fear becomes unreliable when the threatened life belongs to the person making the choice.”

“The threatened lives are not hers.”

The candle flame rose along Gilmot’s fingers. He did not look at it.

“If she refuses service, leaves military custody or is removed by another party, the Faint quarter will be entered into the record as a district complicit in the theft of state property.”

Paper turned beneath his hand.

“Its adults will be suppressed. Fire companies will clear the structures before the second morning bell.”

The Natharul officer remained silent for a moment.

“Including the children?”

“There are always children.”

Gilmot lowered his hand. The flame settled with it.

“Evacuating them would weaken the corrective purpose.”

The words entered Alaric more slowly than the dagger had entered the guard.

Marta would be waiting beside that quarter.

Wena would be with her.

An entire quarter lived beyond Kelmend’s wall, and Gilmot had placed every life within it beneath Darcy’s name before she had been offered a choice.

Alaric’s bloody fingers tightened against the stone.

The wrapping scraped.

Softly.

The Natharul officer’s head turned.

The pointed ear angled towards the passage.

“Someone is outside.”

Silas pulled Alaric away from the door.

They moved as the light inside the room changed.

A chair crossed stone behind them.

Alaric’s ankle struck the first step. Silas caught the back of his tunic before he fell and drove him downwards into darkness.

The command-room door opened above.

“Who is there?” Gilmot called.

Silas did not answer.

The Wind core pulsed through the walls.

High above, the airship strained against its chains.

**KRRRNNNK.**

The sound filled the stairwell and covered their descent.

---

The lower cells began beneath an iron gate.

An elderly gaoler sat behind it with his chair tipped against the wall and a ledger open across his stomach. He woke when Silas tried one of the stolen guard’s keys in the lock.

“Who—”

Silas reached through the bars, caught the front of his coat and pulled his head against the iron.

The first impact opened the man’s hands.

The second closed his eyes.

Silas held two fingers beneath his jaw before taking the ring of keys.

“Alive?” Alaric asked.

“He is breathing. Keep moving.”

Silas lowered the gaoler behind the desk and opened the gate.

Cold waited beyond it.

The corridor descended between rows of cells cut directly into the stone. No guards stood between the prisoners and the stair. Iron locks, stone and exhaustion had been judged sufficient.

Faces appeared behind the bars as Silas passed.

A hand withdrew before the stolen armour reached it.

In the next cell, a woman sat against the wall with a sleeping child beneath her coat. She covered the child’s face when Alaric looked inside.

An old man crouched beside a piece of bread no larger than his palm, breaking it into portions for people Alaric could not see.

None called for help.

Hope would have required believing that anyone came below for them.

Alaric stopped beside the first occupied door.

“We have the keys.”

Silas continued comparing numbers carved into the wall with marks upon the ring.

“We came to open one door.”

“We can open these.”

“And then?”

Alaric looked along the corridor.

“They can leave.”

Silas turned towards him.

“Past the gaoler? The command level? The kitchen where one man is dead and two more are trying to break out? Through a yard filling with soldiers before dawn?”

His voice remained quiet. It did not soften.

“Half of them cannot climb the stairs. The rest will be cut down when the first horn sounds. Open those doors if you want to decide that for them.”

The woman inside the nearest cell listened without lifting her head.

Alaric’s hand remained upon the bars.

He had stopped for one child and left a dead man behind him.

He had killed another to keep Silas alive.

Neither choice had made him strong enough to open every door between them and daylight.

“Which one is Darcy’s?” he asked.

Silas held his gaze for one breath, then turned back to the wall.

“End of the passage.”

Alaric released the bars.

He did not apologise to the prisoners.

He had nothing honest enough to place behind the word.

They walked on.

---

Darcy’s cell contained a writing desk.

That distinguished it from every other cage beneath the fort.

Maps covered one wall, pinned through corners worn soft by handling. Thin columns of figures filled the spaces between them. Sealed orders waited in a wooden tray beside three broken quills. A narrow bed occupied the rear of the cell. The sleeping woman had not reached it.

She had collapsed at the desk.

One arm rested beneath her head. The other hung beside the chair, her fingers scarcely above the floor. Ink marked two of them. The skin across the back of her hand remained unbroken.

Farther up, where the sleeve had gathered near her elbow, burns crossed her forearm in deliberate bands.

Some had faded pale.

Others had not finished healing.

Silas placed the key inside the lock.

Iron turned.

The sound was small after everything required to reach it.

Darcy’s eyes opened before the door did.

She lifted her head.

Exhaustion had hollowed the skin beneath her eyes. Dark hair, threaded lightly with grey, had fallen loose across one side of her face. Her gaze found Silas in the stolen armour and sharpened before any confusion could reach it.

“Darcy,” he said.

Something passed through her expression.

Not welcome.

Not surprise alone.

For one moment, she looked at him as Marta sometimes did when anger loosened enough for the shape of an older wound to show beneath it.

Then Darcy saw Alaric.

Her attention crossed the labourer’s clothing, the swollen ankle and the blood soaking both of his hands. It returned to Silas.

Understanding settled before either man could explain.

Silas opened the cell.

Darcy rose. One hand closed around the edge of the desk until her legs steadied beneath her. The movement pulled her sleeve higher, revealing another narrow burn disappearing above the elbow.

She looked from Silas to Alaric once more.

Pity touched her face.

It did not make the warning gentler.

“Whatever Marta promised you, it was not worth coming here.”
