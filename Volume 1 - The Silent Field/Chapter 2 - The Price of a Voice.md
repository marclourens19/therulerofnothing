# The Price of a Voice

The fourth knock faded into the wood.

No fifth followed.

The boy remained behind the overturned bench, Gerolt’s hand fixed upon his shoulder. Neither of them moved. Even Wena had gone so quiet beneath the bed that, for one irrational moment, he wondered whether the animal had stopped breathing altogether.

Outside, the torch hissed. Light trembled between the shutters but did not pass on. Whoever stood beyond the door was still there, waiting.

Then the layered voice spoke again.

The words slipped over one another in that same impossible language, several tones occupying the same breath. The boy could find no beginning or end to any of it. Beside him, Gerolt gave no sign that he understood.

The voice stopped. When it returned, its strange layering was gone.

“Farmer. Open the door. Tell us what happened in your field, and no other household need answer for your silence.”

The boy understood every word. They were spoken with precise, effortless clarity, each carrying the cold distaste of someone who considered addressing those inside a courtesy already greater than they deserved.

The speaker was male. Young, perhaps, but accustomed to obedience.

Gerolt’s hand left the boy’s shoulder.

*Stay.*

The word formed silently upon his lips.

He moved away on his hands and knees, keeping beneath the narrow bars of torchlight. At the dining table, he curled his fingers beneath the edge of a floorboard and pulled.

The plank rose without a creak, revealing a hollow beneath it.

Gerolt reached inside and withdrew a length of oil-darkened cloth. It fell open across his knees.

A sword lay in his hands.

It was not a farmer’s tool hammered into the shape of one. The leather around its grip had been worn smooth by use. Nicks scarred the guard. The blade itself was narrow, dark and clean, its edge catching what little moonlight reached the floor.

Gerolt held it as though the years since he had last drawn it had meant nothing.

The boy stared.

The man who had dragged him through the wheat was gone.

Or perhaps this man had been beneath him all along.

Beyond the door, metal clicked softly against metal.

“Every moment you keep us waiting is added to your village’s debt.” The man spoke more quietly this time. The boy could still hear every word through the wood.

Gerolt said nothing. He rose beside the table and took two steps towards the door, keeping the sword low against his leg.

The air changed.

Ash lifted from the dead hearth, first in thin grey threads. Then every loose grain in the cabin rushed towards the door at once.

Gerolt saw it too.

“Down—!”

Gerolt lunged sideways and dropped against the wall beside the doorway, wedging himself behind its thick timber post. His free arm locked around the wood.

The world struck inward.

**KRAAACK.**

The sound filled the boy’s head before he saw the wood splitting.

The iron latch tore free. Hinges screamed from the frame. The door crossed the cabin whole, spinning through the darkness with enough force to crush anything in its path.

The boy threw himself flat.

Its lower edge passed close enough to comb his hair with splinters. The door struck the rear wall and burst apart, broken planks scything across the room.

Then the wind followed.

The overturned bench ripped away from his hands. The dining table rolled onto its side. Pots tore from their hooks and crossed the cabin like thrown stones. One shutter wrenched free and vanished into the night. The blanket twisted around the boy’s legs before being torn from him.

At the doorway’s edge, Gerolt’s coat snapped into the room. He clung to the post, his head bowed behind it.

The boy drove his fingers between the floorboards and held on.

For three deafening heartbeats, the cabin belonged to the wind.

Then everything dropped.

Wind『Affinity』.

This was what Gerolt had asked him to reach for—not an idea spoken beside a hearth, but a force that could tear a home apart before those inside found the breath to scream.

An iron pot struck the floor and spun in a widening circle.

The boy raised his head. A tall, slender figure stood where the door had been, framed by torchlight.

The stranger’s hair appeared silver beneath the moon, bound neatly away from a face too fine and unmarked to belong on that ruined farm. Dark armour covered his chest and limbs in fitted layers, its metal traced with curling lines that caught the torchlight like veins of gold.

Through his pale hair rose two narrow, pointed ears.

His eyes passed over the wreckage. There was no alarm in them, only displeasure.

“Human,” he called into the dark. “You were offered the mercy of answering alone.”

He stepped across the ruined threshold. Gerolt came up from behind the post at his side without warning, shout or challenge.

The sword entered the soft hollow beneath the stranger’s jaw.

Gerolt drove it upward through the neck until the sword’s guard struck flesh and the point burst out beneath the opposite pointed ear. The elf’s mouth opened around a sound that never formed. Blood spilled over his lower teeth and poured from both corners of his mouth.

The elf seized Gerolt’s wrist, squeezing until the farmer’s knuckles whitened around the hilt. His eyes widened. He stared at Gerolt, his mouth working as though he still expected an answer.

Then he tried to breathe.

Air bubbled through the wound. A thick, wet clicking filled his throat as blood ran down the blade and poured inside the collar of his armour.

The boy pressed himself harder against the floor. He could not stop watching the elf’s mouth, waiting for him to draw a breath that did not come.

Gerolt wrenched the sword sideways. Something gave with a wet, fibrous tear, and the elf’s grip opened.

A scream rose beyond him. The layered language no longer sounded patient.

A second figure stood beside the horses, one hand stretched towards the cabin. She wore the same dark armour, though hers was lighter beneath a grey travelling cloak. Pale hair had come loose across a face contorted with disbelief.

Her pointed ears lay flat against her head.

The earth answered her. The boy felt it before he understood it.

A shudder passed through the boards beneath his chest.

One plank bowed upward.

Then another.

Something travelled beneath the cabin, breaking the floor in a straight line towards Gerolt’s back.

He was still fighting to free his sword from the dead elf’s neck.

The boy saw the boards splitting behind Gerolt. The farmer had his back to them, both hands locked around the trapped sword.

Silence would keep him hidden.

“Gerolt!”

The name tore from him.

Gerolt twisted towards the shout as the floor bucked beneath him. He could not get clear.

His body folded around something the boy could not understand until blood flooded his coat.

Stone.

A jagged spear had punched through the boards and buried itself beneath his ribs. For one hideous instant, the wound opened around a white curve of bone. Then blood filled it. The stone snapped, leaving a broken length buried deep inside his side.

Gerolt screamed.

It was a raw, animal sound, nothing like the voice that had told the boy to eat or called him a heavy little shite. It filled the ruined cabin and broke into a breathless groan as his knees buckled.

The dead elf sagged across his shoulder.

Gerolt tore his blade free and staggered through the doorway beneath the body’s weight, using its fall to carry himself forward.

The woman raised her other hand. The ground split between them, but Gerolt stumbled across the opening before it widened.

His sword crossed her face.

The edge split her left brow, crushed through the eye beneath it and tore across the bridge of her nose. It left her cheek hanging open to the teeth.

Blood sheeted down her face.

Her scream climbed until the layered voices within it separated from one another.

Gerolt swung again. She raised an arm, but the blade broke through the metal bracer and bit into the bone beneath. Her hand jerked once and hung uselessly from the ruined limb.

Then the earth rose between them.

A slab of soil and stone struck Gerolt across the chest and hurled him back through the doorway.

He hit the floor hard enough to shake the cabin.

The boy scrambled towards him.

Outside, the wounded scout stumbled blindly to one of the horses. One arm swung broken at her side. With the other, she caught the saddle and tried to pull herself up.

Her boot slipped once in the blood running from her face, then again. At last she dragged herself across the horse’s back and found the stirrup.

Gerolt rolled onto his stomach and reached for his sword. His fingers found the hilt, but the strength to rise did not follow.

The horse wheeled away. Hooves tore through the wheat, carrying the woman and her ragged screams into the night.

Gerolt watched until the torchlight vanished with her. Then his forehead touched the floor.

A cough tore through him. Dark blood struck the boards beneath his mouth.

“Fucking elves,” Gerolt rasped against the boards.

The boy reached him. Gerolt tried to turn towards him, then stopped with a gasp, half upon his side.

Blood spread beneath Gerolt’s side, following the seams between the boards. A broken tooth of stone remained buried beneath his ribs, shifting with each shallow breath. Blood welled around it whenever he moved.

The boy reached for the stone.

“Don’t.” Gerolt caught his wrist with a slick, trembling hand. “Leave it where it is.”

The boy folded a loose part of Gerolt’s coat against the blood welling around the stone. Gerolt hissed through his teeth, but his hand settled over the boy’s instead of pushing it away.

“Why did they come here?” the boy asked. Blood was already soaking through the cloth beneath his fingers.

“Natharul scouts.” Gerolt swallowed, the motion tightening every line in his face. “Saw the field, maybe. Saw my light.” His breath caught against the stone, and he waited, teeth clenched, until he could speak again. “Doesn’t matter now. She got away.”

His eyes moved towards the darkness beyond the doorway.

The dead elf’s horse turned beneath its empty saddle, snorting at the blood soaking the threshold.

The boy followed Gerolt’s gaze.

“She heard me.”

Gerolt looked at him.

“She heard me call your name.” His breath began coming too quickly. “She knows you weren’t alone. Gerolt, what do I—”

“I know.” The answer broke into a cough. Gerolt curled around the stone until it passed, blood shining between his teeth.

Before the boy could force out another word, a horn sounded from the direction the wounded scout had fled—low, long and nearer than it should have been.

Another answered from beyond the battlefield.

Then a third, so distant that the note reached them thin and trembling.

Gerolt closed his eyes.

“How many?” the boy whispered.

“Enough.” Gerolt opened his eyes and looked towards the door.

Beneath the bed, Wena began to whine.

The sound broke into frantic scratching. She crawled through the wreckage on her belly, ears pressed flat, and crept towards Gerolt. Her nose touched the blood soaking his coat.

She recoiled, then pressed herself against his arm, trembling so hard that her teeth clicked.

Gerolt laid one blood-slick hand upon her head, his thumb moving once between her eyes along the path of an old habit.

Wena licked the blood from his knuckles. He turned his hand over so she could not do it again.

“No, girl,” he whispered. “Not this time.”

The first arrow entered through the open doorway.

It struck the rear wall with a hard wooden crack, level with the boy’s head.

He ducked over Gerolt, his shoulder brushing the bloodied coat.

A second arrow passed through the broken window and buried itself in the table. A third rattled across the roof.

The horses were coming now—not a faint rhythm beneath the floorboards this time, but thunder across the fields.

Gerolt forced one hand inside his coat.

“Boy. Listen.” Gerolt waited until the boy looked at him.

He pulled free a small iron token, dark with age and worn almost smooth. A split shield had been stamped into one side. On the other was a single word the boy could not read.

Gerolt pushed it into the boy’s palm. When the boy tried to reach back towards the wound, Gerolt kept hold of his hand.

“West. Across the river.” His jaw tightened around a breath that would not come cleanly. “Kelmend. Find the inn… near the southern gate.”

The boy stared at the token. Gerolt was telling him how to leave, but had said nothing about coming with him.

“Ask for Marta. Give her that.” He coughed into his shoulder. When he lifted his head, fresh blood streaked his beard. “Tell her… Gerolt sent you.”

“You’re coming with me,” the boy said. He looked towards the door, then back at the stone beneath Gerolt’s ribs. “We’ll go together.”

Gerolt released a short breath that might once have become a laugh. It collapsed into a cough, and blood ran over his lower lip.

“It’s noth—”

The lie did not survive the breath required to finish it.

Gerolt looked around the ruined cabin—the broken table, the dark hearth, the overturned stool. The boy could still see him sitting there with his mug, asking questions he could not answer.

“Can’t walk.” Gerolt looked back at him. “Look at me, lad.”

“Then I’ll carry you.” The boy eased his free arm behind Gerolt’s shoulders. He tried to lift him, but Gerolt cried out and clutched at the stone.

The boy lowered him at once.

“You could barely hold yourself upright this afternoon.” Gerolt caught his sleeve before he could try again. “Don’t, lad.”

Another arrow struck the cabin, then another.

Gerolt closed the boy’s fingers around the token.

“Take Wena.”

The dog lifted her head at the sound of her name.

“I can help you.” The boy pressed his free hand against the folded coat again, though the cloth was soaked through. “Tell me what to do.”

“You already did,” Gerolt said, his voice barely carrying above the hoofbeats.

The boy shook his head.

Gerolt had carried him from a field of corpses, washed blood from his face and fed him. Given him a place beside the fire when he possessed nothing—not even a name with which to repay him.

The boy looked towards the door, then down at the blood upon his hands. Gerolt would still be lying here after he left.

“I’m not leaving you.” His voice shook, and he pressed harder against the coat, as though Gerolt might believe him if he could only stop the bleeding.

“Wasn’t asking.” Gerolt held his gaze until the boy looked away.

The hoofbeats grew louder.

Torchlight appeared between the stalks beyond the ruined doorway. One flame. Then another.

Gerolt saw them and reached for his sword.

His hand closed around the grip. The blade scraped across the boards but would not rise.

The boy seized his arm.

“Please.”

Gerolt looked at him, and for a moment the hard lines in his face loosened.

“On that field…” Gerolt drew a thin, trembling breath. “Found one lad still breathing.”

His bloodied fingers tightened weakly around the boy’s sleeve.

“Don’t make me die watching you stop.”

Wena pressed her muzzle beneath Gerolt’s hand. He stroked her once more, slowly this time, from between her ears to the back of her neck.

“She’ll stay if you do,” he said. “That’s why you have to take her.”

The boy looked at the dog curled against Gerolt, then at the blood spreading beneath him.

“I can’t.” The boy shook his head, but Wena had begun to nose at his hand as well, searching for some comfort he did not know how to give her.

“Stubborn little bastard,” Gerolt murmured. “All that emptiness, and still no room in you for sense.”

Pain reached the boy before the voice did.

It drove through the back of his skull and hooked behind his eyes.

His spine bowed. His teeth struck together hard enough to fill his mouth with blood. His heartbeat struck behind his eyes.

**THOOM.**

He tried to scream—and heard the sound before his lips had opened.

The cabin came apart.

The door stood whole upon its hinges and lay shattered against the wall. The candle burned upon the table and died between Gerolt’s fingers. A wooden bowl rested warm in the boy’s hands, struck the floor, and was whole again.

Gerolt stood over him unwounded.

Gerolt bled beneath his knees.

The images pressed through one another, one showing through the next like bones beneath skin.

Pain twisted deeper.

Every nerve pulled in a different direction. Light forced itself beneath his fingernails, through his teeth and into the joints of his jaw. His free hand clawed at the floor until his nails split against the wood. The token bit into his other palm, but he could not loosen his grip.

White opened through the centre of everything.

A single thread of light stretched across it, trembling beneath some impossible strain.

Something moved at its furthest end.

Then a voice reached him.

Not whole.

“——ric…”

The fragment passed through him and disappeared.

The white convulsed.

Pressure swelled inside his skull until he felt his eyes strain in their sockets. His throat opened around a scream, but the sound that emerged belonged to someone impossibly far away.

“——Ala…”

The fragments tore at the emptiness inside him.

He reached for them.

Agony ripped from his fingertips to the base of his spine. His body struck the floor. Gerolt caught the back of his head before it could strike again, and the movement forced a strangled cry from the farmer’s ruined chest.

Still Gerolt did not let go.

The voice forced itself through once more.

This time, it reached him whole.

“——Alaric.”

The name entered the place where his past should have been.

For one terrible instant, he expected it to disappear like everything else.

It did not.

It caught.

He remembered no face turning towards it. No hand writing it. No voice calling him home.

Yet every scattered part of him answered.

*Alaric.*

The thread snapped.

The cabin crashed back around him.

Arrows. Hoofbeats. Wena’s frightened whine. Gerolt’s blood warm beneath his knees.

The boy braced his free hand against the floor, retching bloody saliva onto the boards. Pain pulsed behind his eyes with every heartbeat. He could barely see.

But the name remained.

He raised his head.

“My name,” he whispered.

Gerolt stared at him, one blood-wet hand still cradling the back of his skull.

“I know my name.”

The boy tightened his hand around the iron token.

“Alaric.”

For the first time since he had opened his eyes in the mud, the word *I* had somewhere to stand.

Gerolt’s mouth moved before his voice came.

“Alaric,” he repeated.

The name left him softly, roughened by blood and pain.

Then his mouth pulled into a tired smile.

“There you are.”

An arrow passed through the doorway and buried itself in the wall.

Gerolt turned towards it and planted one hand against the floor, forcing himself upright. The stone shifted beneath his ribs. Blood ran freely now, down his hip and into his boot, but he dragged the sword up with him.

Outside, the horns sounded again.

Closer.

Gerolt raised the blade in both hands.

His first attempt at breath failed. He dragged in another.

“Take Wena and run, Alaric.”
