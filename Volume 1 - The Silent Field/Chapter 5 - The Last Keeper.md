# The Last Keeper

The lock completed its turn.

The door moved inward.

Each inch drew a thin complaint from the hinges. Pale morning light entered around its edge and stretched across the floor towards Seralune’s bare feet.

She waited for Thaeroval to appear behind it.

He did not.

An elderly man stood in the opening. Grey hair lay thin against his scalp. Deep lines crossed a face Seralune had seen only once, bent above linen scattered across the palace floor.

The servant who had fallen to his knees and cried when he saw her.

He carried another bundle now, though no white linen showed between his arms. The cloth was dark, plain and tied with a narrow cord.

Behind him, metal caught the pale light from something lying across the passage.

An armoured boot.

Seralune stepped back.

The old man entered, pushed the door almost closed behind him and bowed.

“Your Highness. If you wish to remain awake, you must come with me.”

Seralune stared at him.

“Who are you?”

“Cyrandor, Your Highness.”

“What happened outside?” Seralune looked past him towards the boot.

“The guards are alive,” Cyrandor said. “I had to put them to sleep. We need to leave before someone finds them.”

Seralune crossed the room. He lifted one hand as though to stop her, then lowered it before she reached him. The door opened beneath her palm.

Two guards lay in the passage.

The first had fallen beside the wall, one shoulder pressed against the stone and his chin resting upon his breastplate. An overturned metal cup lay near his hand. Dark liquid had spread between the joints of the floor.

The second lay closer to the door. His sword remained half-drawn from its sheath. A length of folded linen was caught beneath his cheek.

Seralune crouched beside him.

“Your Highness—” Cyrandor looked along the passage again, but Seralune was already bending over the guard.

She placed two fingers beneath the guard’s jaw.

A pulse answered, slow and steady.

She moved to the other man and found the same.

“You poisoned them.” Seralune looked up at the old man, keeping her fingers against the guard’s throat.

“A sleeping draught in their bitterroot.” Cyrandor glanced along the empty passage. “I bring the final watch their tonic most mornings. They had no reason to question it.”

His gaze dropped to the half-drawn sword. “One drank less than the other. I had to stop him from calling out.”

The cloth beneath the guard’s face shifted with his breath. Seralune understood the short struggle she had heard: metal against stone, then the boot striking once.

She looked at Cyrandor’s narrow shoulders and age-thinned hands. One sleeve was torn at the wrist.

“Will they wake?” Seralune asked.

“They should.” Cyrandor looked at the guard beneath her hand. “I measured what I gave them. I cannot promise more than that.”

Seralune kept her fingers against the man’s throat for another moment before standing. Cyrandor stepped back into the room, and she followed, drawing the door almost closed behind her.

“And if I refuse to go with you?” she asked. “Will you let me stay?”

Cyrandor stopped beside the bed. His eyes moved to the bundle in his arms before he answered.

“Yes, Your Highness. I will not force you.” He set the bundle down. “But please hear me before you decide.”

The cord came loose beneath his fingers, revealing a plain grey overdress and a dark hooded cloak.

There was no royal green or branching gold upon either garment. Nothing that belonged to Seralune.

“The wardwrights will return this morning,” Cyrandor said. “If you stay, you will be here when they bring that chain back.”

Cold moved beneath Seralune’s skin.

“Thaeroval forbade them from touching me.”

“He did.” Cyrandor looked towards the door. “But his order did not stop them from coming in last night.”

The words struck harder for being quietly spoken.

Seralune looked towards the passage. The guards had been stationed outside for her protection. Thaeroval had said so.

He had not said whose protection required the lock.

“Did he send you?” Seralune asked, trying to find some part of this her brother might have intended.

“No. He does not know I am here.”

“Then why are you helping me?”

Cyrandor reached beneath his collar. A fine chain appeared between his fingers. Something small hung from its end, hidden beneath his shirt. He drew it free and held it towards her.

A silver signet rested in his palm.

Seralune stopped breathing.

The ring bore no royal tree. A single closed lily had been carved into its dark green stone, its petals folded so tightly that the centre nearly disappeared.

A shallow cut crossed the inside of the band.

Seralune had made that mark.

She had been seven and convinced anything her mother wore must fit her if she pressed hard enough. The ring had slipped from her thumb and struck the edge of the south garden fountain. Her mother had stared at the damage for a long, terrible moment before laughing so hard that the nearest guards had looked away to preserve her dignity.

No one outside the family had known. Cyrandor lifted the chain over his head and placed it in her hands, the ring still warm from his skin.

“Her Majesty left words with it,” he said.

Seralune waited.

“Two knocks are a warning. They are not permission.”

Her gaze returned to the door.

Her mother had spoken those words after Thaeroval entered a council lesson without waiting and found Seralune imitating their father before a room of empty chairs. No one else had heard them.

“Did my mother give you this?” Seralune asked. She held the ring so tightly that the damaged edge pressed into her skin.

“Not to me. The woman who kept it before me placed it in my hands.” Cyrandor’s fingers had begun to tremble. He closed them, but the motion did not stop. “My family served the queen. Before she left Natharul, she gave them one command. If you ever awakened, they were to reach you before the court decided what should become of you.”

“Before she left?”

The words barely crossed Seralune’s lips.

“Where did she go?”

“I do not know where she is now.” Cyrandor kept his voice low. “The trail she left us begins in Mydea.”

The name did not belong inside the room.

The human kingdom.

Seralune looked down at the ring again.

“My mother went to Mydea?” Seralune’s voice rose despite her attempt to keep it quiet.

“There was someone in Favale, the Holy Capital, who could help us find her. That is the direction passed down to me.” Cyrandor paused. “I cannot promise that person is still waiting, Your Highness.”

“Is my mother alive?”

The tremor in Cyrandor’s hands reached his shoulders.

“I do not know.” Cyrandor’s voice faltered. “I wish I could tell you she is.”

Honesty should have hurt less than another evasion, but it did not.

“Did she leave an instruction for me?” Seralune asked. “Am I supposed to go there?”

Cyrandor’s eyes lowered to the signet in her palm.

“She left no command for you. Only that the choice be yours.”

Seralune looked around the room. The bed waited behind her, and the fire had almost died. Beyond the window, the city she did not recognise continued brightening. She remembered the wardwright standing here with his chain, the blades between her and the hearth, and Thaeroval asking her to trust him.

Two unconscious guards lay outside her door. Cyrandor stood beside the bed, waiting for an answer she had not yet given.

Seralune closed her fingers around the ring.

“Why were you crying when you saw me in the corridor?” Seralune asked.

Cyrandor went still.

His eyes closed for a moment.

“Every keeper before me died waiting for that door to open.”

He opened them. “I had begun to believe I would do the same.”

Seralune looked at his lined face, the torn sleeve and the hands that had carried linen through halls where kings and princes passed without seeing him.

She wanted Thaeroval to return—to open the door himself, dismiss the guards and give her one answer that did not create three more.

He had promised first light.

First light had come.

Cyrandor had come with it.

“Give me the cloak,” Seralune said.

---

The grey overdress was too broad across Seralune’s shoulders and too short at the wrists. She pulled it over the pale gown rather than lose more time undoing the unfamiliar fastenings at her back. The cloak covered both and fell almost to the floor.

Cyrandor turned his back while she changed.

When she had finished, he took the cloak’s plain wooden clasp between his fingers. It slipped once before he secured it beneath her throat.

“You are frightened,” Seralune said, watching his hands fall from the clasp.

“Yes, Your Highness.” Cyrandor pressed them against his sides until the trembling eased enough for him to reach for the door.

Seralune slipped the chain over her head and hid her mother’s signet beneath the grey cloth.

“How do we leave?”

“The old southern wing. There is a servants’ stair beneath it that descends into the drainage chambers. The oldest channels pass below the outer wall.”

“Will they take us out of Natharul?” Seralune asked.

“To the lower city. From there, we must try to reach the southern shore.” Cyrandor opened the door and listened before stepping outside.

Seralune paused beside the nearest guard. His chest still rose beneath the armour. She watched it rise once more before following Cyrandor.

“Lead me.”

Cyrandor moved. The first turn took them away from the marble corridors and into a narrow space behind the walls. The stone changed beneath Seralune’s feet. Rough blocks replaced polished floors, worn shallow at their centres by generations of steps no court historian would have thought to record.

Shelves crowded both sides of the passage. Folded cloth rose upon them in pale stacks. The air smelled of soap, dust and heated water.

Cyrandor passed through it without hesitation. At the far end, he lifted a hanging sheet and revealed a wooden door scarcely wide enough for his shoulders. He took an iron key from his pocket and turned it inside the lock.

“How do you know your way through here?” Seralune whispered.

“I carry linen through these rooms every morning.” Cyrandor opened the door onto darkness and listened. “Stay close until we reach the southern wing.”

They descended three shallow steps and entered another corridor Seralune almost recognised.

An old arch remained within its western wall, half consumed by newer stone. Once, it had opened towards the winter kitchens. She remembered flour drifting through afternoon light and servants pretending not to see a princess steal honey from cooling bread.

Now pipes crossed the arch where sunlight had been. Seralune touched the older stone as they passed.

“The winter kitchens were here,” Seralune said. She left her hand against the arch after Cyrandor had passed it. “When did they close them?”

“Before I entered service, Your Highness.” Cyrandor looked back at her. “I have only ever known it like this.”

Seralune let her hand fall and followed him.

Her legs began to shake before they reached the next turning. She ignored them.

The passage inclined upwards. Each step tightened something behind her knees. Her breath grew loud inside the hood, warm against her mouth and cold again when it escaped.

Cyrandor slowed.

“Do not slow down for me,” Seralune said. “I can manage.”

She took another step. Her knee almost folded, and she caught the wall before Cyrandor could reach her.

The old man waited without commenting. Seralune could feel the heat rising in her face.

“Just a moment,” she said, her palm pressed flat against the stone. “Then we go.”

Cyrandor watched the passage while she steadied herself. When she took her hand from the wall, he continued at a pace she could follow.

They crossed behind a metal grille overlooking one of the palace’s larger halls. Servants moved below with baskets and covered trays. Guards passed among them in pairs, green cloaks shifting behind polished armour.

No one looked towards the grille.

Seralune followed Cyrandor until a bell struck somewhere above them. The note shuddered through the walls, came again, then again faster.

The servants below stopped as orders broke across the hall. Covered trays were abandoned. Guards divided between the doors. One pointed towards the northern passages—the direction of Seralune’s room.

Cyrandor’s hand closed around the edge of the grille. “They found the guards.”

Boots entered the service corridors, near and too numerous to belong to one patrol.

Cyrandor turned away from the grille.

“We need to move.”

This time, Seralune did not object when he took her arm.

---

The palace narrowed around them. Every open passage carried voices. Every closed door seemed to hold someone waiting on the other side.

Cyrandor led Seralune through a storage chamber filled with empty lamp cages, then behind a row of cupboards whose false backs opened into another servants’ hall.

They waited there while four guards ran past, white light travelling with them from one caged crystal to the next before fading behind their footsteps.

Seralune felt the lamps answer somewhere beneath her skin.

Mana stirred.

She pressed one hand against her ribs and held it still. The nearest crystal brightened by the smallest amount.

Cyrandor saw but said nothing. The light dimmed, and they continued.

At the next crossing, Cyrandor stopped so suddenly that Seralune struck his back.

Voices approached from the passage ahead.

“Lord Leorin asked for what you witnessed. You will give it to him.”

“I have already told you.”

Seralune knew the second voice.

Nereth.

Cyrandor guided Seralune into the shadow behind a linen press as two guards entered the crossing with Nereth between them.

She wore the same grey and green clothing as before. One guard’s hand closed around her upper arm. The cloth stretched pale beneath his fingers.

“I told you what happened,” Nereth said. “The lamps brightened, and the guards came in with swords.”

“And you stood in their way,” said the man holding her arm.

“They were pointing them at her. She had not hurt anyone.” Nereth tried to loosen his fingers without drawing attention to the movement.

The guard beside her laughed once.

“You were brought here to heat washing water. Keep to that, and leave the wardwrights to their work.”

Nereth lowered her eyes. The hand upon her arm tightened, and Seralune moved.

Cyrandor caught the edge of her cloak.

“No.” The word was scarcely breath.

Nereth looked towards the sound and found Seralune beneath the hood. Shock widened her eyes before she shook her head once, small enough that the guards did not notice.

*Go.*

Seralune looked at the fingers digging into Nereth’s arm, then at her lowered face. The men spoke around her as though she were something being carried between rooms.

Cyrandor’s grip tightened upon the cloak.

“They will close the southern passages if they see you,” Cyrandor whispered. “Please, Your Highness.”

Seralune understood him and stepped from concealment.

“Release her.”

The command crossed the passage. Both guards turned, and the hand on Nereth’s arm loosened. It did not let go.

“Your Highness?”

Seralune pushed the hood back from her face. “I said release her.”

The second guard recovered first. His hand moved towards the horn at his belt.

“Your Highness, the attendant is under order—”

“Her name is Nereth. Use it.”

Silence held them for one breath. Nereth looked at Seralune, fear entering her face before gratitude had any opportunity to appear.

“She acted above her station,” the first guard said.

“She stood between me and drawn weapons,” Seralune said. Her eyes returned to the hand still gripping Nereth.

“Lord Leorin requires her testimony.”

“Then Lord Leorin may ask her for it without bruising her.”

The second guard’s fingers touched the horn.

Seralune met his eyes. “Do not.”

His hand stopped.

For a moment, the authority she remembered still existed.

Then footsteps sounded behind them.

The guard lifted the horn.

Cyrandor’s small glass vial struck the wall beside the guard’s head and shattered. A bitter smell filled the passage as pale vapour burst across the stone.

“Hold your breath!” Cyrandor caught Seralune’s cloak and pulled her back from the vapour.

The first guard coughed. Nereth drove one shoulder into his chest and tore free. The second managed a single broken note through the horn before his breath became another cough.

The sound carried, and answers rose from both ends of the corridor.

“South crossing!” someone shouted behind them.

Nereth seized Seralune’s hand, and they ran.

Seralune pulled the hood back over her head with her free hand.

The bitter smell followed them around the first corner. Seralune’s lungs burned from holding air. When she finally breathed, she coughed against the inside of her hood.

Cyrandor closed a door behind them and turned the key. Fists struck its other side before he had withdrawn it.

Nereth stopped at the head of a short flight of steps and looked back at Seralune.

“You should have left me.” Nereth’s voice shook with anger, though she kept it low. “They know where you are.”

“They were hurting you.” Seralune loosened her grip, allowing Nereth to draw her hand away. “I could not leave you with them.”

The door shook in its frame. Nereth looked towards it, then down the steps.

“We are trying to get out of the palace,” Seralune said. “Do you want to come with us?”

Nereth turned to Cyrandor. “Are you taking her to the old south stair?”

He nodded, and the colour left her face.

The door jumped again, and wood split beside the lock. Nereth turned south.

“This way.” Nereth reached for Seralune’s hand again and led them down the steps.

---

Nereth caught Cyrandor’s arm when he turned towards a narrow gallery.

“Not there. They use it for the wardwrights’ stores now.” She pointed towards a low door beside the laundry. “We can go through here.”

Cyrandor looked towards the gallery, then followed her. At the next turning, he stopped before a stairwell filled with stone and metal pipes. He looked towards Nereth, who took them through a room where steam hung too thick to see through.

Servants flattened themselves against the wall as the three of them passed. Overhead, the alarm continued.

Seralune kept the hood low, though she could no longer hide her breathing.

Each step drove pain higher through her legs. The hand Nereth held became the only part of the corridor that remained steady.

“How far?” Seralune asked.

“The next court,” Cyrandor said. He looked towards Nereth before continuing. “If that stair is still open.”

“It is.” Nereth’s grip tightened around Seralune’s hand. “But we must cross where the guards can see us. Keep your hood down.”

Then the floor trembled—not from running guards, but from something striking stone several passages behind them. Shouts followed the crash.

Cyrandor looked back.

“Faster.”

Nereth led them across the edge of the court and through a low doorway into the southern wing.

The change was immediate. Heat left the walls. Dust gathered in the corners, disturbed by too few feet. The crystals overhead were older and dimmer, their light trapped behind cloudy surfaces.

Seralune recognised the shape of the windows. They had once looked over the lower orchards; stone filled them now.

At the end of the passage stood a room crowded with broken furniture. Nereth released Seralune’s hand to help Cyrandor push aside a tall linen cupboard. Its legs screamed across the floor.

Behind it waited a wall and nothing more. Nereth stared.

“There is no door.” Nereth pressed one hand against the wall and looked at Cyrandor.

“The opening is hidden. Give me room.” Cyrandor dropped to one knee and searched the base of the wall until his fingers found a narrow crack. From inside his sleeve, he drew a strip of blackened metal no longer than his palm.

He fitted its end into the crack. No keyhole was visible even now that Seralune knew where to look.

Cyrandor turned it.

Something answered deep inside the wall, a heavy scraping that Seralune felt through the soles of her feet.

The stone divided along a line too fine to have been visible a moment before. Cold air reached through it as Nereth placed both hands against the opening and pushed.

The hidden door began to move.

“Seralune.”

Her name stopped everything.

Thaeroval stood at the far end of the passage. No guards accompanied him. He did not need them.

His dark armour carried dust across one shoulder. The sword rested unsheathed in his right hand, its edge lowered towards the floor.

His gaze moved from Seralune to the cloak, then to the old servant kneeling beside the opened wall.

“Come here,” Thaeroval said, his voice low beneath the distant alarm.

Seralune’s body leaned towards him before thought could stop it.

One step.

Almost.

Thaeroval raised his empty hand.

The same hand that had held hers through the unfamiliar palace. The same hand that had rested against her door a hundred evenings before entering anyway.

Seralune remembered the silver chain.

She stopped.

“Did you order them to lock that door?” Seralune asked. She stayed beside the opening in the wall.

Thaeroval’s hand remained extended.

“No.” He looked at Cyrandor, then back at her. “But I knew they had.”

“You left me there.” Seralune’s voice shook, and she hated that she could not stop it. “You asked me to trust you.”

“I was trying to find out what broke in the lower chamber. You should have been safe in your room.” Thaeroval took a step towards her. “I asked you to wait for me.”

“I did. Morning came.”

The hidden door opened another hand’s width behind Seralune, stone grinding against stone.

“Were you going to let them use that chain on me?” Seralune asked.

“I would never let anyone hurt you.” The words came quickly, and Thaeroval reached farther towards her.

“I know you do not want to hurt me.” She could barely make herself look at his open hand. “I am asking whether you would let them put it on.”

His hand lowered by the smallest amount.

“Come back with me,” Thaeroval said. “I will tell you what I can.”

What he could. The same boundary.

Seralune felt her mother’s ring against her chest.

“You promised me that last night.” Seralune held the ring through the cloth. “I cannot go back to that room and wait for them again.”

“You are following people you do not know.”

“I know.” Her fingers tightened around the signet. “I do not want to be afraid of you, Thaer. But you will not tell me what is happening. He has told me what he knows.”

Thaeroval’s face did not harden. That would have been easier. It opened around something wounded.

“Please, Seralune.” Thaeroval’s voice had roughened. “Come to me.”

Seralune wanted to go to him, and the want hurt more than the running had.

She closed one hand around the cloak.

“I am not going back.” Seralune forced the words past the tightness in her throat.

For a moment, no one moved.

Cyrandor pulled the old key from the wall. The sound was small.

Thaeroval’s gaze left Seralune.

“You will go no farther, old man.” Thaeroval’s voice hardened as he addressed Cyrandor.

Cyrandor rose on shaking knees and placed himself between Thaeroval and the hidden door.

“Nereth,” Cyrandor said, looking towards the stair.

The attendant understood. She drew Seralune towards the opening, beyond which a narrow staircase curved steeply down through the dark.

Thaeroval moved.

Cyrandor pressed the blackened key against his own palm.

Silver lines ignited across the stone.

They raced from the hidden doorway, crossed the floor and climbed the walls in a single blinding pattern. The passage closed between two breaths. A veil of white metal hung from floor to ceiling, every thread trembling beneath a force Seralune felt in her teeth.

The light entered Cyrandor’s hand and travelled beneath his skin. Thin silver branches spread across his wrist and disappeared beneath the torn sleeve. He gasped.

Thaeroval stopped before the barrier, his gaze fixed upon the silver spreading beneath Cyrandor’s skin.

“Release the anchor,” he said. “You know it will not hold me.”

Cyrandor’s legs shook harder.

“I know.” Cyrandor’s voice thinned as he drew breath. “I cannot let you take her back.”

Nereth stepped onto the stair. Seralune remained upon the landing.

“Cyrandor.”

He looked back at her. For the first time since entering her room, the fear upon his face was uncovered.

He bowed.

Thaeroval lifted his sword without advancing. The length of the passage remained between them, the silver barrier trembling at its centre.

His wrist moved, and the blade passed once through empty air.

It crossed none of the distance.

The cut did.

Cyrandor’s body jerked.

His arm separated below the elbow.

The forearm struck the stone, its fingers still closed around the blackened key.

For one heartbeat, nothing else moved.

Then blood came.

It struck the floor and crossed the hem of Seralune’s cloak.

The silver branches beneath Cyrandor’s skin went dark. Across the passage, every line of the barrier vanished with them.

A sound tore from the old man as he dropped to one knee. His remaining hand clamped around the ruined sleeve above the wound, blood forcing itself between his fingers.

“Cyrandor!” Seralune moved towards him, but Nereth caught her hand from the stair.

“Your Highness, come.” Nereth pulled at her, and Seralune resisted without looking back.

Cyrandor looked up.

Pain had emptied the colour from his face, but his eyes found hers.

Alive.

He shook his head once.

Thaeroval crossed the place where the barrier had been.

The severed forearm lay between them, its hand still holding the key.

The stroke could have taken Cyrandor’s head. Seralune knew that. She watched him trying to stop the blood and could not find relief in what her brother had spared.

Cyrandor looked towards the open stair.

Seralune stopped pulling against Nereth’s hand.

“Go,” she whispered.

Nereth descended, holding Seralune’s hand until she followed. Cyrandor was still watching her when she turned away.

Behind them, the hidden mechanism groaned, and the stone door began to close.

Thaeroval reached it before the opening narrowed by half.

His sword rose.

Seralune saw the angle of the blade and the line it would cut through the stone beneath her feet.

Through the stair.

Through her.

Thaeroval’s gaze dropped towards the step upon which she stood.

Their eyes met through the narrowing space.

The sword did not fall.

Nereth pulled a rusted lever beside the wall.

The upper stairs dropped away.

**CRRRAK—!**

Stone folded into darkness. Dust struck Seralune’s face before she could draw breath. The remaining steps lurched beneath her feet and threw her against the wall.

The door slammed shut above them.

Darkness returned, broken only by green light trembling across moving water far below.

Seralune pushed herself upright. Her knees burned. One palm had opened against the stone, and blood gathered inside the shallow cut Nereth had washed only hours before.

Above them, something struck the sealed doorway, and the stair shook.

Thaeroval could cut through it.

He could cut through anything.

The next blow did not come.

Nereth stood several steps below, breathing hard.

“Can you walk?” Nereth asked, reaching back towards her.

Seralune looked at the blood along the cloak’s hem, then up at the stone separating her from the only person she had trusted without question.

“Yes.” She pressed her bleeding palm against the cloak and steadied herself.

She descended.

The staircase ended in ankle-deep water. It closed around Seralune’s feet, cold enough to steal the first breath she drew. A low passage stretched south beneath the palace, its curved ceiling furred with moss and its walls cut by channels older than the city above.

The alarm reached them through the stone, muted by the distance. Every bell in Natharul seemed to be calling for someone Seralune no longer recognised.

Nereth entered the water beside her.

“Which way?” Seralune asked.

Cyrandor had carried the old route. Nereth knew what remained of it.

The attendant crouched and let the current move across her fingers. “The old south channel was sealed before I entered service. This runoff feeds the eastern culvert before turning beneath the lower city.”

“How do you know?”

“We are sent down to clear the drains when they back up.” Nereth looked towards the passages ahead. “I know the laundry channels. I have not been farther.”

Nereth rose and entered the right-hand passage first. Seralune touched the signet beneath her cloak and followed, its lily pressing into her palm.

Towards the lower city.

Towards Mydea.

Towards a mother who might no longer be waiting.

Seralune turned with the current.

Behind her, beyond stone and distance, her brother’s voice entered the passage.

“Seralune!”

She heard him.

She kept running.
