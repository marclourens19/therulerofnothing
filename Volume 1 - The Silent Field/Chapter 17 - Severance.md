# Severance

Cyrandor woke while the physician was changing the linen around what remained of his arm.

He tried to sit. Pain pulled a sound through his teeth and darkened the innermost fold of the bandage before the physician pressed him down again.

“You’ll open it.”

“Then stop touching it,” Cyrandor managed.

The physician stared at him. “I am trying to keep you alive.”

“You’ve succeeded. Let an old man enjoy the surprise.”

His voice carried little farther than the bed.

Four lamps burned around the infirmary, their crystals turned low against the final hours of night. Clean bowls had been stacked upon a side table. A curved needle rested in one of them beside thread stained red for half its length.

The third night since Seralune entered the southern stair had nearly ended.

Lord Leorin stood near the locked door. His court robes had been exchanged for a dark working coat, though the silver branch at his collar still marked his office. Thaeroval waited upon the other side of the bed.

Cyrandor found him last.

The physician laid another strip of linen across the wound.

“Prince Thaeroval.” Cyrandor settled into the pillow. “Did you come back for the rest of me?”

“No.”

“Then you should let him work. It appears you left him plenty to do.”

The physician reached for the bowl. Thaeroval stopped him with a slight motion.

“Can he answer questions?”

“He has been awake twice,” the physician said. “The first time, he did not know where he was. The second, he tried to leave.”

“I was thirsty.”

“You were halfway out of bed.”

“The water was across the room.”

The physician turned towards Thaeroval. “A few questions. No longer. If he begins bleeding again, I end this whether you are the prince or the First Blade.”

“Give him something for the pain.”

“Anything strong enough to help will cloud him.”

“Afterwards, then.”

Leorin stepped away from the door. “Your Highness, this man opened a forbidden royal passage, attacked palace guards and aided the princess in fleeing Natharul. His comfort can wait until he tells us where she is.”

“Cousin.” Thaeroval faced him. “Wait outside.”

Leorin stayed beside the door.

“You asked me to witness the questioning.”

“I no longer require a witness.”

The physician placed the bloodied linen in a bowl. “Nor do I require half the royal court standing over my patient.”

Leorin gave the man a look that would have emptied most rooms. The physician reached for another bandage without appearing to notice.

“Five minutes,” Leorin said to Thaeroval. “The king will return before dawn. I won’t have him learn we questioned the traitor without anyone present to verify what was said.”

He opened the door. Two guards straightened in the passage beyond it.

“Physician.” Thaeroval waited.

The man finished securing the fresh linen, checked Cyrandor’s pulse and pointed towards the widening stain.

“If that reaches the outer fold, call me.”

“I will.”

Only after the physician had joined Leorin in the passage did Thaeroval close the door.

The lock turned.

Cyrandor looked at it. “Of course you locked it.”

Thaeroval returned to the bedside. His sword stayed sheathed. He had left his cloak elsewhere, and the dark armour beneath it carried pale dust along the joints. A dried line of Cyrandor’s blood marked one groove of the right vambrace.

“You betrayed the royal family.”

“I served its queen.”

“You drugged two guards.”

“They’ll recover.”

“You raised an anchored ward against me.”

“It almost delayed you.” Cyrandor’s mouth bent without becoming a smile. “I had hoped for longer.”

Thaeroval drew the chair nearer and sat. He did not remove the sword at his hip.

“I am sorry for your arm.”

Cyrandor turned his face towards him.

“Are you?”

“I chose a wound you might survive. Breaking the anchor any other way would have killed you.”

“That is an explanation.”

“It is the truth.”

“I did not say it wasn’t.” Cyrandor’s remaining hand moved across the blanket and stopped near the bound stump. “You looked at her before the blood reached the floor. Not at me.”

Thaeroval’s answer took time.

“She should never have seen it.”

“No,” Cyrandor said. “She should not have.”

One of the lamps gave a low glass hum. The light beneath its cage thinned and returned.

Thaeroval leaned forwards, keeping his voice below the guards outside.

“Where did you mean to take her?”

“Away from that room.”

“Do not spend what strength you have avoiding me. Which country?”

Cyrandor closed his eyes briefly. “Mydea.”

Thaeroval sat back.

“Where in Mydea?”

“I don’t know.”

“You carried her through a passage beneath the palace with no destination beyond another continent?”

“I was supposed to get her beyond Natharul. Someone else was meant to help after that.”

“Who?”

“That name was not passed to me.”

“Whose order were you following?”

Cyrandor looked towards the covered window. No dawn showed around its edges yet.

“Your mother’s.”

Thaeroval did not rise. The fingers resting against his knee became still.

“My mother left Natharul one thousand years ago.”

“She prepared for more than one lifetime.”

“Explain.”

Cyrandor shifted against the pillow. Colour drained from his face before he found a position that did not pull at the bandage.

“The queen gave an instruction to a family in her service. One keeper passed it to the next. We were to watch the lower seal. If it ever broke and Princess Seralune awakened, one of us was to reach her before the court decided what should happen to her again.”

“Again.” Thaeroval repeated the word without emphasis.

“That was how she gave the order.”

“She built a hidden service around my sister and told no one in her own house.”

“She had already seen what her own house chose.”

The lamp nearest Cyrandor rattled when his shoulder struck the bedframe. Thaeroval rose and adjusted the pillow behind him before the old man could force himself higher.

Cyrandor did not thank him.

“My mother abandoned Seralune,” Thaeroval said. “Whatever order she left does not alter that.”

“No. It only proves she knew she might never be allowed to return.”

“Where is she?”

“I have never met the queen.”

“You said the trail began in Mydea.”

“That is what I inherited.”

“And nothing more?”

Cyrandor met the question without answering it.

Thaeroval’s attention moved to the open collar of the old man’s nightshirt. A pale line crossed the skin at the base of his throat where a chain had rested for years.

“You wore something beneath your uniform.”

Cyrandor’s chin lowered by the smallest amount.

“Every day I saw you in these halls,” Thaeroval continued. “The same chain. It is gone.”

“It reached the person it was kept for.”

“My mother’s lily signet.”

Cyrandor’s silence confirmed it before his voice did.

“Yes.”

Thaeroval stood. The chair legs made no sound against the stone.

“Seralune has it.”

“It belongs to her.”

“Someone may have seen it.”

“Then perhaps you should ask why a woman who ran into your arms when she woke chose to run from you the next time you met.” Cyrandor’s voice weakened in the middle, but he forced the rest through.

Thaeroval crossed to the foot of the bed.

“She does not remember why the seal was necessary. She does not remember what happened when her mana became unstable. Returning those memories before I understand what woke her could destroy more than this palace.”

“Did you tell her that?”

“I told her enough to keep her safe.”

“You told her to wait in a locked room.”

“I was trying to learn what had broken beneath her.”

“And she still had no answer when morning came.”

The stain reached the outer fold of Cyrandor’s bandage.

Thaeroval pulled the bell cord beside the bed once. Footsteps answered from the passage.

“She was safe here,” he said.

Cyrandor’s remaining hand pressed the linen above the wound as the door opened.

“She did not believe you.”

The physician entered with Leorin behind him. Thaeroval moved aside at once.

“You said five minutes,” the physician snapped.

“It has been four.”

“Then you have managed a great deal of bleeding in less time than most.” He cut through the fresh linen and reached for the curved needle. “Out.”

Leorin waited for Thaeroval in the doorway.

“Did he give you anything useful?”

“Seralune is looking for the queen in Mydea. She carries the queen’s signet.”

Leorin glanced back at Cyrandor. “That is more than he gave me.”

Thaeroval looked towards the bed one last time.

“Keep him alive.”

The physician did not look up. “That has remained my intention despite everyone’s interference.”

Cyrandor’s face had turned towards the wall.

Thaeroval left him there.

---

The medicine bottles began ringing against one another before the infirmary door closed.

A low vibration entered the floor, climbed the walls and set every suspended lamp swaying upon its chain. The guards looked towards the ceiling. Somewhere deeper in the palace, metal doors groaned inside their frames.

Leorin crossed to the nearest window and pulled the covering aside.

Night had disappeared behind the royal warship.

The vessel replaced the sky above the palace. Its armoured underside stretched beyond the northern towers, drawing loose leaves from the gardens and flattening every banner still raised beneath it.

At the far end of the corridor, servants abandoned a cart against the wall and hurried towards the landing terraces. Guards followed, fastening cloaks and speaking into black communication crystals as they ran.

“He returned early,” Leorin said.

A royal messenger arrived before Thaeroval could respond.

The young man stopped several paces away and bowed too fast to control the fall of his cloak.

“First Blade of Natharul. His Majesty requires you aboard immediately.”

Leorin released the window covering. “Has the king disembarked?”

“No, my lord.”

“Did he give a reason?”

“He said the prince was to come alone.”

Leorin looked towards Thaeroval.

“You had better not keep him waiting, cousin.”

Thaeroval followed the messenger.

The royal landing terrace occupied the highest court on the northern side of the palace. By the time they reached it, the warship’s shadow had swallowed every wall. Wind tore between the towers, carrying burnt wheat, engine heat and something fouler from the open bay in the vessel’s side.

A boarding bridge lowered through that darkness.

Its first section reached the terrace. The stone beneath it cracked before the braces distributed the weight. Royal guards descended in pairs, their armour carrying grey ash across the boots and greaves. None took the time to clean it away.

The messenger stopped at the bridge.

“His Majesty is in the forward chamber.”

Thaeroval stepped aboard alone.

The interior of the royal warship held enough room for mounted soldiers to pass one another without lowering their standards. Corridors branched between armoured pillars. Caged crystals burned along their faces, their light shivering each time the engines corrected the ship above Natharul.

No welcoming line had formed. No court representative waited to receive the king. Soldiers moved crates away from the forward chamber while attendants carried sealed cases in the opposite direction. Every order arrived before the last had finished.

Two guards opened a pair of metal doors as Thaeroval approached.

The king stood inside with his back to them.

He still wore the armour in which he had crossed the battlefield outside Kelmend. A long dark mantle hung from his shoulders, its lower edge stiff with mud and ash. Pale hair had fallen loose from beneath the narrow gold band across his brow. When he turned, a small black mark crossed one cheek where a gloved hand had failed to wipe it clean.

“Close the doors,” he said.

The guards obeyed.

“Lock them.”

One guard hesitated. Thaeroval reached behind him and turned the inner wheel himself. Metal bars entered the walls with three heavy impacts.

The king waited for the last one.

“When did Seralune wake?”

“Three nights ago.”

“Before the report from Mydea?”

“Yes.”

“How long before?”

“The lower seal broke near midnight. The first message from Kelmend arrived several hours later.”

The king crossed to the black communication crystal fixed above the chart table. Lines of green writing covered its surface. He cleared them with the side of one hand, and new lines began forming beneath the glass.

“Several hours,” he repeated. “Not days. Not months.”

“What did you find?”

The king faced him.

“An army we watched die one thousand years ago.”

The ship shifted around them. A metal joint complained somewhere beyond the wall.

Thaeroval did not speak.

“Their standards belonged to houses that have not existed for a thousand years,” the king continued. “Their armour carried seals outlawed after the Remaking. The war machines were built before half the foundries in Natharul had names, and they appeared in Mydea without crossing a border, without a supply road and without one scout seeing them approach.”

“Could they have been preserved beneath the field?”

“They were not buried.” The king’s voice rose, then fell at once. “They stood in battle lines. Some were still mounted. Thousands died in the same moment, and no one in Kelmend heard them arrive or fight.”

He pressed both palms against the chart table. Ash broke from one glove and marked the map beneath it.

“I touched one of the machines.”

“And?”

“I remembered the day its line broke against our western host.” The king looked past Thaeroval towards the locked doors. “I remembered the captain who commanded it. He had a gold plate fixed over the left side of his jaw because your blade had taken the bone beneath. His body was there. The plate was still in place.”

Thaeroval’s head lowered once.

“You believe the field and Seralune are connected.”

“I believe nothing from that age returns by accident.”

“She remembers none of it.”

“Not yet.”

The king left the table and stopped close enough that the dirt upon his mantle reached Thaeroval’s boots.

“Where is she?”

“Her trail ended at Waluna. She is no longer in the settlement.”

“Then she took a ship.”

“Most likely.”

“To where?”

“Mydea.”

The king turned away. The distance to the chart table was only several steps, yet he reached it as though the room had forced him back there.

“How do you know?”

“Cyrandor said the route prepared for her began there. Seralune carries Mother’s lily signet.”

The king stared at the map between the two continents. Shipping lines crossed the sea in narrow inked curves.

“Who prepared it?”

“He gave me no name.”

“And you permitted him to live?”

“He survived the wound.”

“That was not what I asked.”

“He knows no exact location.”

The king struck the table once. The communication crystal jumped inside its silver frame.

“We have an ancient army appearing around a human farm, a destroyed seal beneath my own palace and a daughter whose power once helped tear countries out of Lazaran. Do not offer me measured answers as though any part of this remains ordinary.”

Thaeroval stood without answering.

The king pointed across the table towards Mydea.

“You will follow her.”

“Yes.”

“You will return her to Natharul before whatever began in that field reaches her.”

“I will bring her back.”

“Anyone who knowingly keeps her from you has chosen what follows.”

Thaeroval’s expression gave the king nothing.

“Be precise, Father.”

“Kill them.”

The word travelled no farther than the chart table.

“The servants who took her?” Thaeroval asked.

“Anyone who obstructs her recovery. I will not lose a kingdom because you mistook accomplices for innocents.”

“And Seralune?”

The king looked towards the door before answering, though no sound came from beyond it.

“Restrain her. Bring her home. We restore the upper seal before her mana stabilises.”

“If she refuses?”

“You are the First Blade of Natharul. Her refusal is not beyond you.”

Thaeroval’s voice stayed level. “That was not my question.”

The king crossed the narrow space between them.

“If her memories return, and if you cannot contain what follows, you end it before another city disappears.”

Thaeroval did not move.

“Can you do that?” the king asked.

The engines carried their low vibration through the chamber.

“I will find her,” Thaeroval said. “I will bring her back, and I will restore the seal.”

“Can you kill her?”

Thaeroval reached past him and unlocked the door.

The king caught the metal handle before it opened.

“Answer me.”

“You gave me an order.” Thaeroval looked down at the hand upon the door. “I heard it.”

A fist struck from the other side.

“Your Majesty!”

The king released the handle. One of the guards pulled the door inwards, and a soldier staggered across the threshold. Blood covered the front of his armour. More had dried beneath one ear.

“The southern terraces,” he said. “Something came out of the old channels.”

The king took in the blood.

“How many?”

“We don’t know.” The soldier swallowed before forcing himself upright. “The first company stopped answering. The second attacked one another before they reached the lower court.”

Another impact shuddered through the ship.

This one came from below.

The soldier looked towards the floor.

“It’s inside the wards.”

The king took one step towards the communication crystal.

“How close to the palace?”

“The lowest terrace is already lost. It crossed the southern watch court before I reached the ascent.” The soldier’s voice roughened. “Your Majesty, the men aren’t only fighting it. They’re seeing things. Hearing people who aren’t there.”

“How many companies remain between it and the inner wall?”

“Two. Perhaps one by now.”

The king turned towards Thaeroval. “Take the palace guard.”

“No.”

The refusal stopped the wounded soldier more completely than it stopped the king.

“It turns armed men against whoever stands nearest,” Thaeroval said. “Send another company and it will turn those soldiers too.”

“You have not seen what it is.”

“Neither have you.”

Thaeroval stepped past the messenger. The king followed him into the corridor.

“Thaeroval.”

He stopped.

“Nothing reaches the upper city,” the king said. “Do you understand me?”

“Clear the terraces above it. Keep every soldier who has not heard the voices beyond the inner wall.”

“That was not an answer.”

Thaeroval looked back at him.

“It will not reach the city.”

The boarding bay stood open ahead. Beneath its green-white lights, a landing craft had not yet been secured from the journey to Mydea. Ash covered one short armoured fin. Two crewmen were fastening the forward brace when Thaeroval approached.

“Release it.”

The nearer man looked towards the officer above the bay.

“First Blade, the core is still hot from descent.”

“How long before it can fly?”

“It can fly now, First Blade. But the core needs watching.”

“Have the pilot watch it. Release the braces.”

Both crewmen moved.

Thaeroval entered the craft as the outer braces withdrew. The pilot barely reached his seat before the deck dropped away beneath them.

The royal warship filled every window until the craft turned south. Then Natharul opened below.

The palace occupied the mountain’s broadest shelf, its towers and courts descending through terraces cut into white stone. Lights had awakened across the upper levels. Guards crowded the inner walls while servants crossed the courtyards towards the northern shelters.

The southern slope lay dark.

One watchtower burned beside the broken aqueduct. Beyond it, brief spells opened and vanished among the lower courtyards—orange fire against a wall, a white stroke of Wind, the blue glare of Water striking stone.

None travelled in the same direction.

The pilot saw it too.

“Where do I put you?”

“The third terrace.”

“The third is within bow range of the lower court.”

“Land behind the western stair and return to the ship.”

“I was ordered to remain with the First Blade.”

“You were ordered before we knew what killed the first company.” Thaeroval fastened the sword more securely at his hip. “Return to the ship.”

The pilot brought them down.

Stone struck the landing struts with a force that threw sparks past the windows. Thaeroval opened the side before the craft settled. He stepped into smoke, and the pilot lifted away at once.

No line of soldiers waited to receive him.

The third terrace had been built around a long reflecting pool. Bodies filled it now. Green cloaks moved beneath water reddened by the lamps along its edge. A soldier knelt upon the paving with both hands around a spear driven through another man’s armour. He was whispering apologies while continuing to press.

Thaeroval caught the shaft and pulled it free.

The kneeling soldier turned. One pupil had widened until almost no colour remained around it.

“You put her in there,” he said.

Thaeroval struck him once beneath the ear with the sword’s sheathed hilt. The man collapsed beside the pool.

A woman in broken armour backed from the western colonnade. Blood ran from her hair into her collar. She raised a short blade when Thaeroval approached.

“Stay away from me.”

“Drop it.”

“You’re wearing his face.”

“Who?”

Her answer came from the dark arch behind him.

“The one who left us.”

The voice belonged to an older man. It spoke gently, with the patience of someone calming a frightened child.

The wounded woman made a sound that broke between anger and grief. Her blade turned from Thaeroval towards the arch.

“Father?”

Black roots slid over the stone inside it.

Thaeroval moved between the woman and the dark. He pushed the blade down before her arm could rise again.

“The man speaking is not there. Go to the landing court.”

“He knows my name.”

“Go.”

She stared beyond him until another voice called from the upper stair.

“First Blade!”

Three soldiers stood behind an overturned stone bench. One supported a fourth whose leg bent beneath the knee at an angle no armour could conceal. Relief struck all three faces at once.

“First Blade, over here!”

Thaeroval took the wounded woman by the uninjured arm and brought her to them.

“Get behind the western stair. Do not answer anything that speaks from a shadow.”

The eldest soldier looked past him towards the lower terrace. “There are men alive below us.”

“How many?”

“We heard at least twelve. Then they started killing each other.”

“Take these two up. I will bring whoever remains.”

The injured man tried to place his foot down. Bone moved beneath the greave.

Thaeroval crouched, lifted him without touching the ruined leg and carried him behind the stair. The soldiers followed with the woman between them. When they reached the shelter, he lowered the man against the wall and removed the clasp strangling the swollen limb.

“The next landing craft will take you.”

The man caught the edge of Thaeroval’s vambrace.

“It uses them,” he said. “The dead. It sounds like the dead.”

“Then give it no answer.”

Thaeroval rose and entered the lower court.

The killing there had nearly finished.

Hundreds had descended through the southern terraces since the first alarm. They lay among shattered weapons and roots thick enough to split paving stones. Some had died facing the dark at the end of the court. Others faced one another.

A line of archers had fallen beneath their own arrows. Two still lived. One dragged herself between bodies with a shaft through her side. The other sat against a pillar, sawing a knife across the leather beneath his breastplate while begging someone named Arilen to forgive him.

Thaeroval took the knife from his hand.

The soldier clawed after it.

“She’s inside me.”

“No one is inside you.”

“I heard her.”

“You heard what wanted you to open your armour.” Thaeroval pulled him upright and placed the crawling archer’s arm across his shoulders. “Carry her to the western stair.”

The soldier looked towards the darkness beneath the eastern wall.

“It will follow.”

“Then do not stop.”

Training survived where reason had failed. The man obeyed.

Something laughed inside the empty pillar behind them.

It used Cyrandor’s voice.

“The next time she saw you, she ran the other way.”

Thaeroval continued across the court.

The laugh moved ahead of him. It entered the shadow beneath a fallen balcony and emerged from a dead soldier’s open mouth.

“And she still had no answer when morning came.”

His pace did not change.

The roots changed instead.

They had entered the bodies nearest the eastern wall through gaps in armour and open wounds. Black-purple threads travelled beneath exposed skin. Larger growths held the dead upright where they had fallen, turning them towards Thaeroval as he passed.

A hand closed around his ankle.

The soldier attached to it had no strength left to lift his head. Dark veins covered one side of his face.

“Please,” he said.

Thaeroval bent and cut the root around his waist with the narrow blade hidden inside his gauntlet. The growth recoiled from the metal. He dragged the man clear and laid him beyond its reach.

“Can you stand?”

“I don’t know.”

“Try.”

The soldier reached for Thaeroval’s offered forearm. His legs held long enough for two survivors to run from behind a broken wall and take him between them.

“Upper terrace,” Thaeroval told them. “Keep to the lit side.”

One of the survivors bowed while still supporting the man.

“You came,” she said.

Relief broke her voice upon the second word. Behind her, another soldier tried to bow and nearly dropped the wounded man between them.

Thaeroval turned towards the eastern wall.

The creature unfolded from its shadow.

It stood slightly taller than a man, though the shape refused every proportion that might have made it one. Its arms hung beyond its knees and ended in hooked black fingers. A narrow head leaned from shoulders woven out of smoke, root and wet strands that shifted without wind. Two white eyes opened inside a face without skin.

The mouth opened last.

Pale teeth crowded it in several uneven rows.

Behind the creature, its shadow covered half the wall.

The arch through which it had entered was no larger than a door.

“First Blade,” whispered the soldier beside Thaeroval.

Five survivors remained close enough to hear him. One carried no weapon. Another held a sword in both hands because neither could keep it steady alone. They had gathered behind Thaeroval without being told and now watched the creature over his shoulders.

The white eyes passed across them.

The court filled with voices.

A mother called for her son from beneath the pool. A dead commander ordered the archers back into line. Someone wept behind the eastern wall. Someone else whispered that the woman with the sword had betrayed them and would do it again if they let her live.

The woman turned towards the man beside her.

Thaeroval stepped back between them.

“Place your weapons on the ground.”

“It will kill us,” the man said.

“Put them down.”

The creature’s shadow reached towards their feet.

“He wants you defenceless,” the dead commander warned from the pool. “Look at his sword. He came to finish what we began.”

The man raised his blade towards Thaeroval.

Thaeroval caught the flat between two armoured fingers and turned it aside.

“If I meant to kill you, you would not be holding that.”

The sword slipped from the man’s grasp and struck the paving.

The others followed.

The creature tilted its head.

Its shadow moved over Thaeroval’s boots.

Voices emerged from it one after another—Cyrandor, Leorin, the king, soldiers who had died a thousand years before. Each accused him of something different.

None used Seralune’s voice.

None drew an answer from him.

The creature’s mouth closed.

For the first time since Thaeroval entered the terrace, it moved backwards.

He placed himself fully between it and the survivors.

“Go to the western stair.”

One woman stooped for her sword.

“Leave it.”

“First Blade, there may be more below.”

“There will be nothing below when I finish.”

They ran.

By the time they reached the western stair, no living soldier remained between Thaeroval and the eastern wall.

The creature sank into the wall.

Its white eyes appeared inside the shadow of a ruined tower thirty paces to Thaeroval’s left. One hooked arm followed. The rest remained stretched across the distance between both shadows, too long and thin to belong within the court.

Thaeroval drew his sword.

The blade cleared the sheath by a hand, then came free. The nearest lamp continued burning without disturbance.

Thaeroval turned the edge towards the creature. Black light travelled from his wrist into the metal, swallowing each reflection along its length.

Pressure struck the terrace.

Every other living person upon it lost the strength in their knees.

The survivors fell against the western stair. Farther above, soldiers dropped spears and caught at the walls. The landing craft descending through the smoke lurched as its pilot fought the controls.

The creature abandoned the tower. White eyes opened in the shadow beneath the pool, then beneath a body, then within the broken aqueduct beyond the court.

Distance did not move it beyond the edge.

Thaeroval cut once.

A line crossed the world.

The creature separated first.

Its body remained standing until the upper half slipped from the lower and struck the paving. Every pale tooth divided along the same perfect height. No blood left it. Black vapour poured from both halves and ended before it reached the ground.

Beyond the court, roots came apart inside stone.

The cut travelled through the broken aqueduct. From chambers no soldier had reached came the scrape of claws and a succession of bodies striking stone. The growth knotted around the mountain’s buried wards came apart with them. No flame or burst of mana followed. Things connected a moment before simply ceased to meet.

The southern mountainside opened.

Stone hundreds of feet thick shifted across a level line. Trees leaned together as the earth beneath them separated. The remaining face of the crater slid towards Waluna, carrying terraces, dead roots and the foundations of the lowest watchtower.

Far above it, the clouds divided.

Moonlight and approaching dawn met inside the opening.

The line reached the coast south of Waluna’s outer headland.

Water stood apart.

For several seconds, the sea formed two dark walls beyond the headland. The exposed floor ran towards the horizon between them, divided by the same line beneath fish, broken anchors and the ribs of vessels lost before the oldest pier had been built.

No wave crossed the division.

No sound did either.

Then the world moved again.

The mountain fell.

**KRRRRAAAAAAM.**

The southern slope struck the lower terraces. Stone climbed through the air. The watchtower vanished inside it.

The sea returned with a roar large enough to reach the palace.

Water collided along the severed line and struck the outer face of the headland. The stone took the first impact. A broken surge curled around it and entered the harbour, pitching ships against their ropes and turning every seaward window silver.

Thaeroval lowered his sword.

Beyond the court, the creature’s remains lay beneath the broken aqueduct.

The people behind him remained whole. Not one new wound had opened among them.

All across the court, the voices had stopped.

The soldier whose face carried dark veins passed upon a stretcher. The roots around him had died, but the black-purple marks beneath his skin continued pulsing. A physician diverted him from the other wounded and ordered the landing craft cleared around him.

One soldier began laughing from behind the western stair. The sound lasted only a few breaths before becoming sobs. Others lifted their heads and looked around at the people they had wounded.

The wounded woman from the third terrace had reached the court above. She tried to stand when she saw him.

“First Blade.”

Thaeroval sheathed the sword.

The pressure released. Survivors rose carefully, touching walls and one another for support.

A man nearest Thaeroval reached for the edge of his armour.

“You saved us.”

Thaeroval stepped beyond the hand.

“Move everyone above the fourth terrace. The slope is still failing.”

The man remained upon his knees.

“Did you hear me?”

“Yes, First Blade.”

“Then move.”

Thaeroval crossed the court without looking at the wounded beside the pool.

Above the palace, the royal warship had turned broadside to the coast. Figures lined the forward windows.

The king stood apart from them.

When Thaeroval looked towards the ship, his father stepped back from the glass.

Dawn found the dead roots inside a maintenance channel beneath the fourth terrace.

The mountain continued settling beyond the cordon while physicians worked beneath the western colonnade. Inside the narrow passage, Thaeroval stood behind a kneeling wardwright as Leorin held the lamp.

The black growth had hardened around a bundle of silver conduits. Fibres entered through their joints and emerged farther along, fused so completely that the wardwright’s knife tore pieces from both.

The wardwright exposed another section and stopped.

“This should not be here.”

“The root?” Leorin asked.

“The junction.” The man scraped centuries of mineral stain from a silver plate. A branching seal appeared beneath his thumb. “This conduit belonged to the lower circle. It carried the excess away from the central chamber.”

Leorin brought the lamp closer. “Away to where?”

“The lower channels carried it to an outlet beneath the southern slope. It should have dispersed into the sea. Nothing should have remained here.”

“Yet the roots found it.”

“My lord, this growth may have been feeding upon the lower ward for——”

“Enough,” Thaeroval said.

Thaeroval held out one hand. “Your report.”

The man surrendered a folded sheet covered in diagrams and hurried writing.

“Who else has seen this junction?”

“One other from my order. Lord Leorin. The recovery captain.”

“All four will report to me. No copy enters the court record until I return.”

The wardwright glanced at Leorin, who offered no objection.

“Yes, First Blade.”

Thaeroval folded the sheet and placed it beneath his armour.

“Seal the channel. No one enters without my authority.”

The wardwright gathered his tools and left. Leorin stayed inside the channel.

“The king should see that,” he said once the man had gone.

“He saw what came out of it.”

“That is not the same as seeing where it grew.”

“Father has already joined Seralune’s awakening to the army in Mydea. If he learns these roots fed from her seal, he will join them to every death upon the terrace as well.”

“Perhaps he should.”

“You watched her wake.” Thaeroval’s voice carried no anger into the cramped passage. “Did she send this creature against anyone?”

“No.”

“Then the report waits.”

“You are hiding evidence from the king.”

“I am stopping him from deciding what happened before the wardwrights finish their work.”

“And if he asks me directly?”

“Tell him what you saw.”

“I saw you take it.”

“Then you will have something true to tell him.”

Thaeroval left the maintenance channel.

Leorin followed several paces behind.

Neither mentioned how far the sea had opened.

---

The king waited aboard the warship with a black case upon the chart table.

He had cleaned the ash from his face. The armour and stained mantle had not changed. Two guards stood outside the chamber this time, and the doors stayed open until Thaeroval entered.

The king dismissed the guards.

He did not lock the doors.

“Waluna still stands,” he said.

“Yes.”

“The harbour master reports two piers torn loose, flooded storehouses and nineteen people injured. No deaths reported.”

“Then they moved quickly.”

“Your cut crossed a mountain and divided the sea.”

Thaeroval waited beside the table.

The king kept watching the sword at his hip.

“The southern terraces would have fallen within the hour if that corruption continued through the wards,” he said. “You stopped it.”

No praise entered the words.

Thaeroval opened the black case.

Four dark metal bands rested inside, joined by fine silver chains. Curved lines covered their inner surfaces. When laid together, the bands formed the same broken circle that had surrounded the hollow where Seralune awakened.

“These were prepared after the lower seal failed,” the king said. “They will interrupt the flow between her mana and the space around her. Not for long. Long enough to return her.”

Thaeroval lifted one band. It opened beneath his thumb and closed again with a soft metal click.

“Who made them?”

“The three wardwrights who remember the upper design.”

“Who tested them?”

“No one can test a restraint made for Seralune.”

Thaeroval replaced the band.

“If they harm her, I remove them.”

“If you remove them too soon, she may harm everyone around her.”

“Then I will choose the moment.”

The king’s mouth opened. Whatever argument had gathered there did not leave it.

Thaeroval closed the case.

“The royal courier is ready,” the king said. “Waluna first. The harbour records will narrow which vessels departed after the mountain opened. If she showed your mother’s signet to anyone, they will remember it.”

“And after Waluna?”

“Mydean waters. Every northern harbour if necessary.”

“I will go alone.”

“You asked for no company at the terraces.”

“Men beside me would only give Seralune more lives to fear for.”

The king moved away from the table as Thaeroval secured the case at his side.

“Then go alone.”

Thaeroval walked towards the open doors.

“Bring your sister home,” the king said behind him.

He did not answer.

Thaeroval crossed the boarding bridge back into the palace and turned along the infirmary corridor.

Cyrandor’s door stood open while two attendants carried away bowls of bloodied water. The old man lay awake beneath clean blankets. Fever had left a grey sheen across his face, but his remaining hand no longer pressed the bandage.

Thaeroval stopped outside.

Cyrandor saw the black case. His attention moved from it to the sword and finally to Thaeroval’s face.

No guard ordered the prince onwards. No physician denied him entry.

Thaeroval continued past the door.

Cyrandor watched him go.

Thaeroval crossed back to the warship and descended to the lower bay.

The royal courier waited alone at the edge of the lower bay. It was narrow beside the landing vessels, built around a single enclosed seat and two swept armoured fins. Three Wind cores burned beneath its frame, their pale light forcing mist across the floor.

Thaeroval placed the case behind the seat and fastened it to the inner brace. He kept the sword at his hip.

Leorin stood beyond the marked line while the bay crew withdrew the clamps.

“What if Waluna’s records tell you nothing?” he called over the rising Wind.

“Then I ask who lied.”

“And when Mydea asks why the First Blade crossed its border?”

Thaeroval closed the courier’s side.

“Tell them I am looking for my sister.”

The final clamp released.

Wind struck the bay hard enough to drive every loose chain across the floor. The courier slid forwards, dropped beneath the warship and turned south before the palace could rise behind it.

Morning reached the coast.

The sea had closed over its wound. Ships moved again beyond Waluna, leaving white paths upon water that gave no sign it had stood divided before dawn.

The mountain kept the cut.

It ran through the southern slope in one unbroken line, too straight for any fault and too deep for the returning light to reach its end.

Thaeroval flew above it towards the harbour.
