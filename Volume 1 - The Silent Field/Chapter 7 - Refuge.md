# Refuge

Alaric did not knock.

The weathered door stood only a few paces away. No light showed beneath it. The painted cup above the entrance moved upon its chain whenever the wind reached the yard, answering with a faint wooden complaint.

Behind him, Kelmend was waking.

Cart wheels crossed the street beyond the lane. Somewhere near the southern wall, a bell continued sounding at measured intervals, each note followed by the distant movement of armed men.

Alaric looked towards the edge of the yard.

The stranger remained there with his cloak drawn around him, as though crossing the last few steps would place him in greater danger than anything they had passed outside the walls.

“Well?” he asked. “What are you waiting for?”

Alaric looked back at the door.

Gerolt had sent him here.

The thought should have made lifting his hand easier. Instead, knocking would mean he had reached the place Gerolt sent him—and that Gerolt would never follow.

Wena stood beside Alaric with her head hanging low. Water had gathered beneath her paws. A cough moved through her body, small and wet, before she swallowed it.

Alaric knocked.

Nothing answered.

He struck the wood again, harder this time.

A floorboard shifted somewhere inside.

“If you think this is noon, the ale has left you with piss for brains,” a woman called through the door. “Find a wall to sleep against and come back when the sun is high.”

The lock turned.

The door opened only far enough for one watchful eye to appear beyond it.

It passed over Alaric.

The opening widened.

The woman on the other side held herself with quiet authority. Dark red hair fell in a loose braid over one shoulder. She wore a long black robe closed neatly at the throat, and even before sunrise, with sleep still faint beneath her eyes, there was something striking about her that the plain doorway failed to diminish.

Her attention moved across Alaric’s wet hair, the mud dried along his face and the charcoal-black messenger’s coat hanging heavily from his shoulders.

Suspicion reached her before concern did.

“The inn is closed.”

Wena lifted her head.

For one heartbeat, she stared.

Then she ran.

She did not run well. Her rear legs slipped upon the wet stone, and another cough broke from her before she reached the doorway.

She ran anyway.

The woman’s expression opened.

“——Wena?”

She dropped to one knee as the dog struck her. Wena pressed her head beneath the woman’s arm and released a sound Alaric had not heard from her before—too low for a bark and too broken for a whine.

Both of the woman’s hands disappeared into the wet fur around her neck.

“Easy, girl. Easy.”

Wena leaned against her until the woman had to brace one hand upon the doorframe.

Her gaze rose to Alaric.

Alarm had replaced suspicion. It had not become welcome.

“Where is Gerolt?”

Alaric opened his mouth.

Before he could answer, the woman looked past him.

The stranger had not moved from the edge of the yard.

Something in her face went cold.

“Silas.”

The name settled across the distance between them.

The stranger inclined his head once.

“Marta.”

The hand buried in Wena’s fur tightened.

“I told you to stay out of Kelmend.”

Silas did not answer.

“Get out.”

Still, he said nothing.

Her gaze returned to Alaric. Whatever Wena’s recognition had earned him disappeared beneath Silas’s name.

“And if he brought you here, you can leave with him.”

Marta rose with one hand remaining upon Wena’s neck and began to close the door.

Alaric pulled the iron token from beneath his coat.

“Gerolt sent me.”

The door stopped.

Marta looked at the split shield stamped into the metal.

She did not reach for it at once.

Her gaze travelled from the token to Alaric’s wrapped hands, then returned to his face.

“Your name.”

“Alaric.”

She held out her hand.

Alaric placed the token into it. Her thumb passed once across the worn divide in the shield.

“Where is my uncle?”

The question left no place for a kinder answer.

“He…” Alaric swallowed. “He’s dead.”

Wena pressed harder into Marta’s side.

Alaric forced himself to continue.

“There were two riders at the farm,” Alaric said. His voice had begun to tremble. “Gerolt killed one. The other got away and brought more of them back. He made Wena and me run, and he…”

The burning cabin returned behind his eyes.

“He stayed behind. He died protecting me.”

Marta’s fingers closed around the token.

Her eyes shut.

“You old fool.”

The final word trembled by the smallest amount.

Then her eyes opened, and everything the tremor had revealed was gone.

She looked towards Silas.

“Gerolt sent the boy. He did not send you.”

Silas accepted the words without moving.

“Get out of Kelmend! NOW!”

Her voice trembled with anger. Beneath her hand, Wena’s ears flattened.

“Wena and I wouldn’t have reached the wall without him,” Alaric said.

Her gaze cut back to him.

The strength of it almost made him step back. He stayed where he was.

“Then you owe him,” Marta said. “I do not.”

Bootsteps entered the lane.

Silas turned his head first. Marta looked towards the lane a breath later.

Metal touched stone beyond the stable wall. Several men were approaching at speed.

Marta’s hand tightened around the edge of the door.

“Alaric. Take Wena inside.”

Alaric crossed the threshold with Wena pressed against his leg.

Silas took one step forwards.

Marta moved into the doorway.

“Not you.”

A soldier shouted from the lane.

“Search the next yard!”

The approaching boots changed direction.

For one moment, Marta did not move. Silas waited before her without attempting another step.

Her jaw tightened.

“Damn you.”

She moved aside only far enough to let him pass.

“Inside. Now. The moment they’re gone, so are you.”

“I know.”

Silas entered without touching her. Marta closed the door hard behind him.

Darkness swallowed them.

The inn took shape slowly around Alaric: overturned cups drying beside a washbasin, chairs resting upside down upon wooden tables and a soot-darkened hearth beneath a stone chimney. The air smelled faintly of yesterday’s bread, ash and something sweet left inside an open bottle.

Marta pointed to the low door beside the hearth.

“Cellar.”

Silas crossed the room without speaking.

“If that latch moves before I open it,” Marta said, “I will put you back in the yard myself.”

Silas paused with one hand upon the door.

“Understood.”

He disappeared beneath the inn. Marta waited until the latch settled before turning to Alaric.

“You and Wena. Pantry.”

She opened the first door beneath the stairs. Flour sacks occupied most of the cramped space, with dried herbs and strings of onions hanging above them.

“Touch nothing. Keep her close, and if she coughs, muffle it. Neither of you comes out until I open this door.”

Alaric crouched beside Wena and drew her against him.

Marta pulled the pantry door almost closed.

The knocking began before she had crossed the inn.

“Marta!” a man called. “Open the door. Captain’s orders.”

“Your captain knows when this inn opens.”

The lock turned. Marta opened the door but remained squarely within its frame.

Cold air reached the pantry. Through the narrow gap, Alaric saw four Mydean guards crowded beyond her. The oldest stood in front with one hand resting upon his belt. A younger man behind him met Marta’s eyes and straightened so quickly that the plates upon his shoulders clicked together.

“Morning, Marta,” he said.

The older guard looked back at him. Colour rose into the younger man’s face.

Marta folded her arms.

“What does your captain want?”

“We need to search the inn,” the older guard said. The order carried less force now that Marta stood in front of him. “A boy came through the southern wall. Young. Dark hair. Unfamiliar black clothing. He has a large farm dog with him.”

Wena began to raise her head. Alaric closed one hand gently around her muzzle and drew her against his chest. She trembled but did not resist.

“And why are four armed men searching for a wet boy and his dog?” Marta asked.

The older guard glanced back towards the lane before lowering his voice.

“He came from Gerolt’s farm. The captain does not want that repeated.”

Marta glanced towards the neighbouring shutters. “Then lower your voice.”

“I shouted your name, not his business.” The guard’s mouth tightened. “Natharul wants him found before the next bell.”

Marta’s mouth flattened at the name. “And your captain has decided to obey them.”

“We are trying to prevent a larger problem.”

“You have already allowed him through your wall.”

The younger man choked back a laugh. His commander silenced it with a glance.

“Have you seen them?” the older guard asked.

“No. You woke me.”

“Then we need to look inside.”

“No.”

Marta remained in the centre of the doorway.

The guard’s expression hardened. “Every property near the southern gate is being searched.”

“Every market night, I remove drunken brutes twice his size from this room.” She nodded towards the youngest soldier. “A frightened boy and a farm dog will not get past my door. If they try, I will hold them here and send for your captain.”

The young guard looked as though he could not decide whether he had been insulted or complimented. His eyes returned to Marta’s face before dropping quickly to the ground.

“We still have our orders,” the older man said, though less certainty remained in them.

“Then use your men where they are needed. A boy who climbed through the wall will choose an empty building before an occupied inn.” Marta pointed west. “Search the old grain sheds. Half have broken doors, and none have owners awake enough to object.”

The guard looked towards the lane.

“Why west?”

“The barracks are east, the market is already waking to the north and you have filled the southern roads with armour. Where would you hide?”

The youngest guard cleared his throat.

“She’s right, Sergeant.”

The sergeant’s gaze cut towards him.

“I did not ask, boy.”

Colour climbed the young man’s face again.

The sergeant looked back at Marta. She had not moved an inch.

“If they come here—”

“You will find them waiting for you.”

He held her gaze for another moment, then stepped away from the door.

“Grain sheds first,” he ordered. “Then the western wall.”

The younger guard lingered until Marta raised one eyebrow.

“Move,” the sergeant snapped.

He hurried after the others.

“We may return,” the sergeant warned.

“After noon.” Marta began closing the door. “Perhaps by then you’ll remember how one works.”

The soldiers left.

The front door closed. Marta turned the lock but left Alaric in darkness.

The patrol crossed the stable yard. Their footsteps entered the lane, then thinned towards the western wall. She waited until the last of them had faded before opening the pantry.

Light fell across Wena’s wet coat.

“Bring her to the hearth,” Marta said. “I’ll have neither of you freezing to death in my pantry.”

---

Marta laid two pieces of wood across the remaining coals and extended her fingers. Air gathered in a narrow current, too controlled to disturb the ash around it. The coals brightened. Flame travelled along the bark and closed around the wood.

Wind『Affinity』.

Alaric recognised it before he decided to.

Marta placed a pot above the fire. She did not ask whether he had noticed.

Marta crossed to the cellar and lifted the latch.

Silas stood upon the first step below, waiting. He had not touched the door. Water still fell from the edge of his cloak and gathered around his boots.

Disgust tightened Marta’s mouth.

“Far end of the table. Cloak on the chair. Hands where I can see them.”

Silas climbed into the inn with his eyes lowered. He crossed to the chair she had chosen, removed his cloak and sat without argument. Both hands settled upon the table.

Only then did Marta turn away from him.

Wena lay beside the hearth. Marta spread three rough towels beneath her, then crouched and worked another through the wet fur around her head and chest. Wena leaned heavily against her knees.

A cough shook the dog. Marta waited for it to pass before placing a shallow bowl of water within reach.

Alaric sat nearer the fire with a blanket around his shoulders. Marta placed bread and a bowl of broth before him.

Hunger carried the first spoonful into his mouth before he tasted it. His hand was already returning to the bowl when Marta spoke.

“Slowly.”

Alaric stopped.

“I’m trying.”

“You’ll make yourself sick eating like that.”

He lowered the spoon and did as she said.

Marta cut cooked meat into a second bowl and carried it to Wena. The dog smelled it without raising her head. Marta held one piece against her mouth.

“Come on, girl.”

Wena took it.

Marta waited until she accepted another before returning to the table.

“She knows you,” Alaric said.

“Gerolt brought produce for the inn. Wena came often enough to decide the yard belonged to her.”

Marta drew Gerolt’s token from a pocket in her robe and set it between them with the split shield facing upwards.

“Do you know what Gerolt gave you?”

Alaric looked at the worn iron.

“No. He only told me to take it to Marta.”

“You did not know who Marta was?”

“No.”

“Yet you came.”

“It was the last thing he asked me to do.”

Marta watched him for a moment longer before looking down at the split shield.

“This belongs to the Broken Shield.”

“What is that?”

“A promise.” Marta sat across from him. “It tells me Gerolt trusted you enough to send you here. That earns you food, warmth and one night beneath my roof while I hear what happened.”

She tapped the broken line with one fingernail.

“It does not buy trust, and it does not make this inn yours.”

“I understand,” Alaric whispered.

He understood only that Gerolt’s token had bought him one night. Everything else remained beyond him.

Marta’s gaze shifted to Silas.

“It buys you nothing.” The edge returned to Marta’s voice.

Silas kept both hands flat upon the table.

“I know.”

Marta looked back at Alaric.

“You said he got you through the wall.”

“One of those riders found us near the river,” Alaric said. “Silas killed him before he found where Wena and I were hiding. Then he brought us here.”

Marta kept her eyes upon Silas.

“What were you hoping to take from him?”

“Nothing from him,” Silas said.

Marta’s mouth tightened.

“Then why bring them at all?”

Silas glanced once towards Wena. His fingers pressed flat against the table.

“The riders had reached the river. If I left them there, Natharul would have found both before morning.”

“That explains the river.” Marta’s gaze did not leave him. “It does not explain why you used them as an excuse to ignore me and come here.”

Silas offered no answer.

She turned the token once upon the table and pushed it towards Alaric.

“Start with where Gerolt found you.”

Alaric closed his hand around the iron.

“On his farm,” Alaric said.

Marta waited.

“I woke beneath a dead man.” Alaric’s voice weakened. His throat had begun to close around the words.

The bowl warmed his hands, but mud pressed cold against his cheek again. A dead hand lay inches from his face, its fingers curled around a broken spear.

“I was covered in blood.”

“Was it yours?”

Alaric shook his head. “Gerolt searched me. He couldn’t find a wound.”

“What do you remember before waking?”

“Nothing.”

Marta’s gaze sharpened.

“Nothing at all?”

“I remember how to speak. I know what things are when I see them.” Alaric stared into the broth. “But I don’t remember learning any of it. I don’t remember where I lived. I don’t remember anyone.”

The admission was harder in a warm room. Nothing here allowed him to pretend that his missing life would return when the danger passed.

For the first time since sitting down, Marta forgot to look towards Silas.

“How many bodies?” she asked.

“I couldn’t count them.” Alaric stared into the broth. “They covered the farm. Thousands, Gerolt thought.”

“Mydean?”

“The banners were wrong. Gerolt didn’t know them.”

He told her about the beastfolk because leaving them out felt too close to lying. Horns broken beneath armour. Clawed hands closed around weapons. Bodies too large to belong to any human.

Marta’s expression tightened.

“They cannot enter Mydea.”

“They were there.”

He told her about the silver machines next. Even describing them felt impossible. Metal without rivets or seams. Great hollow bodies built to carry people through war, though he could not remember ever seeing one move.

“How did you know what they were for?” Marta asked.

“I don’t know.”

“You must know something.”

“The same way I knew what a sword was.” His fingers tightened around the spoon. “The knowledge is there. Nothing around it is.”

Marta sat back, but she did not interrupt again.

Alaric tried to tell the rest in order. The untouched wheat surrounding the destruction. The absence of any road or camp that could explain how an army had arrived. Gerolt going to sleep with crops outside his window and waking to thousands of dead.

“He heard nothing during the night,” Alaric said. “No horses. No fighting. It was simply there when morning came.”

The fire cracked softly beside them.

“There was a circle,” he said.

Marta leaned forwards. “What kind?”

“Perfectly round. The grass inside was still green, but frost covered it. Everything outside had been burned or broken, and nothing inside had been touched.”

He could see it waiting among the dead.

“All the grass pointed towards the centre,” he said. “There was nothing there.”

Marta looked towards Silas.

“You saw this?”

“I saw enough bodies to stay away,” Silas answered. “I did not see the circle.”

“Gerolt thought the riders had,” Alaric said. “He said that might be why they came to the cabin.”

Marta’s attention returned to him.

“And the rider who escaped saw you alive inside Gerolt’s cabin.”

Alaric nodded.

“The one who escaped heard me call Gerolt’s name,” Alaric said. “She knew he wasn’t alone. By the time we reached Kelmend, the guards were already looking for me.”

Marta went quiet.

“Do you know what happened there?” Alaric asked.

“No.”

The answer came too quickly to contain comfort.

“But I know someone who may recognise where the questions lead.”

She rose before Alaric could ask for the name.

“Coat and shirt.”

He stared at her.

“You can be embarrassed after I know whether the river broke anything.”

Silas stood.

Marta turned on him immediately.

“Stable store. Second door across the yard.”

“The patrol may circle back.”

“Then keep your head below the windows and do as you’re told. You’re already blackening my floorboards with whatever filth the river left on you.”

He collected his cloak and left without looking at Marta or speaking to Alaric.

Alaric set the token beside the bowl.

He removed the messenger’s coat. It resisted where filth had dried beneath the collar, then released him all at once. Marta hung it near the hearth.

The black clothing beneath remained whole.

Mud stained it. Gerolt’s blood had dried almost brown across one side. The river and tannery had pressed their smell into every fold.

Not one seam had opened.

Marta caught the edge of a sleeve and turned it beneath the firelight.

“Where did this come from?”

“I woke in it.”

“Of course you did.” A faint edge of irritation sharpened the words.

She released the fabric.

Alaric pulled the shirt over his head. Pain caught beneath his ribs and shoulder, holding one arm halfway raised until Marta guided the cloth around it.

Bruises crossed his side in dark bands. A shallow tear ran along his ribs where stone had found him beneath the river. His right shoulder had begun to swell. Both knees were marked, one by the riverbed and the other by the settling chamber floor.

Marta pressed carefully along the ribs.

Alaric drew away when her fingers reached the darkest place.

“Breathe.”

He did.

The breath reached its end with pain but without the grinding he feared she was searching for.

“Again.”

He obeyed.

“Nothing moves where it should not,” she said. “That is not the same as nothing being damaged. If breathing worsens, you wake me.”

She cleaned the cut and bound it, then examined his shoulder, ankle and knees. The ankle had swollen around the place it had folded in the drainage cut, but it carried his weight when she made him stand. She wrapped it without promising that the cloth would make walking pleasant.

His wet shoes came away last.

Skin had rubbed open along both heels.

Marta looked at the wounds. Then at him.

“You walked from the river like this?”

Alaric glanced towards the stable yard.

“He said he would leave me if I stopped.”

“And would have.”

The certainty in her voice carried older knowledge. She offered none of it.

Marta washed the blood and tannery filth from Alaric’s palms. The loosened wrappings had dried into the torn skin. Removing them hurt more than opening the wounds had. He kept his hands flat upon the table while she cleaned grit from each cut.

Her touch was firm and exact. When both hands were clean, she wrapped his left one. The final strip of cloth remained beside the bowl.

Then she stopped.

Her gaze lifted from his palms but did not settle upon his face. She seemed to be looking at the air immediately around him.

“What is your『Affinity』?”

“I don’t have one.”

“How weak?”

“Not weak. None.”

Marta’s eyes narrowed.

“Those are not the same thing.”

“I know.” Alaric looked towards Gerolt’s token. “He made me reach for it. Nothing answered.”

Marta stepped away from the table. Distance did not change what she was searching for.

“What did he say?”

“He called me Empty.”

She remained still for several breaths, then turned towards the passage leading to the yard.

“Silas.”

The stable-store door opened a short while later. Silas appeared beyond the inner doorway and stopped without crossing it.

“On the road,” Marta said, “did you feel his mana?”

Silas looked at Alaric.

“I thought he was spent.”

“That was not my question.”

Silence stretched between them.

“No,” Silas said at last. “I felt nothing.”

“And you said nothing.”

“He had crossed a flooded river and could barely stand. I had better reasons to explain it than impossible ones.”

Marta looked back at Alaric. The caution in her face had changed. It no longer resembled suspicion alone.

“My Wind『Affinity』is ranked Eminent,” she said. “It is strong enough that I can feel the mana other people release. I have spent most of my life around people who lie about how strong they are. Some possess so little that the law calls them Faint. Exhausted mages sometimes insist they have nothing left.”

She shook her head once.

“They are always wrong. Weak or exhausted, something remains. A pressure. A direction. Enough to know what answers when they reach inward.”

Alaric’s throat tightened.

“And me?”

Marta held his gaze.

“My uncle was right,” she said quietly. “You truly are Empty.”

The word struck harder than it had in Gerolt’s cabin.

Gerolt had spoken it like a question. Marta spoke it like a conclusion.

Empty.

Alaric looked down at his injured hands. The name he had fought to remember suddenly felt too small to fill the absence she had found inside him.

“Is that what I am?”

“I don’t know what you are.”

Marta returned to the table and picked up the final strip of cloth.

“There has never been a need for a word for someone like you. Everyone has an『Affinity』. Even the Faint possess something.”

She wrapped the cloth around his right palm. Her first pull was too tight. Alaric flinched, and she loosened it without apology.

“The law knows what to do with the Faint,” she continued. “It calls them dependants, servants or property and places them beneath someone stronger. You do not fit even that cruelty.”

“What would they do to me?”

Marta tied the bandage and sat back.

“Men do not leave impossible things alone. They would lock you away first and decide what to call you afterwards. Priests would examine you. The court would ask whether you were dangerous. If Natharul demanded you, Mydea might surrender you and call it peace.”

Alaric looked towards Silas.

“Would any of them help me understand?”

“No.” Marta’s expression hardened. “They would hate you. The Faint are treated as property for possessing too little. You possess nothing at all. They would place you beneath even them, then call every cruelty that followed your rightful place.”

The fire warmed the room, but Alaric no longer felt it.

“No one can know,” she said. “Not a guard. Not a priest. Not anyone who might sell your secret for coin or favour.”

“Silas knows.”

Marta’s gaze moved to the doorway.

“For his sake, he will remember how to keep silent.”

Silas did not react to the threat.

“I had already planned to.”

“Back to the store.”

He obeyed. The door closed behind him, leaving Alaric alone with Marta and the sound of Wena breathing beside the hearth.

“Are you sending me away?” Alaric asked.

Marta looked at the split shield between them.

“Gerolt bought you a night, and I said I would honour it. Tonight, you stay.”

She carried the empty meat bowl to Wena and crouched beside her. One palm settled against the dog’s ribs, following the uneven rise and fall beneath the drying coat.

“She needs warmth and sleep. If the coughing worsens, wake me.”

Wena pressed her nose against Marta’s wrist.

For one moment, grief returned to Marta’s face.

She lowered her head and hid it in the dog’s fur.

---

The room Marta gave Alaric stood beneath the sloping roof.

It contained a narrow bed, a washstand and one chair with a crack through its seat. No one had slept there recently. Dust marked the window latch, and the blanket smelled faintly of dried lavender.

Wena circled the bed once before lowering herself beside it.

Alaric sat upon the mattress. Pain found him differently now that he had stopped moving. His ankle pulsed beneath its wrapping. Both heels burned. The bruises across his ribs tightened whenever he bent towards Wena.

Alaric placed Gerolt’s token upon the washstand.

He lowered himself beside Wena instead of lying upon the bed. Her coat had begun to warm. When he placed one hand between her eyes, she opened them and looked towards the door as though expecting someone else to enter.

No one did.

Alaric stayed beside her until her breathing slowed.

He intended to rise afterwards.

Darkness took him first.

---

When Alaric woke, night had filled the room.

Laughter rose through the floorboards. Tankards struck tables, chairs scraped across wood and several voices fought their way through the same song. For one disoriented moment, the noise became the breaking of Gerolt’s cabin around him.

He sat up too quickly.

Pain closed around his ribs and shoulder. His breath stopped halfway in. The room steadied only after he leaned back against the bed and waited for the darkness at the edges of his sight to withdraw.

Wena raised her head beside the bed.

Her coat had dried into uneven ridges. She coughed once, swallowed and stood when Alaric did.

“Stay.”

Wena followed him to the door.

The dark coat Silas had given him, a clean linen shirt and a pair of plain trousers waited upon the chair. Alaric did not remember any of them being brought upstairs. He had managed to pull on the shirt and trousers when someone knocked upon the door.

Wena turned towards it.

Marta entered carrying a cup of water. She had changed into a fitted dark-green dress with long sleeves, and her hair had been pinned away from her face. Voices followed her through the doorway until she closed it behind her.

“Keep Wena away from the passage,” she said. “Half of Kelmend appears to have decided thirst is an emergency tonight.”

She set the cup upon the washstand beside Gerolt’s token.

“Silas remains in the stable store until I close the inn.”

“You let him stay.”

“I kept him from being seen outside my inn. Do not make those the same thing.”

Marta looked at Alaric.

“What do you intend to do tomorrow?”

His eyes moved towards the iron shield.

“I don’t know.”

“Then decide before morning.”

“How long can I stay?”

“Until first light, whether you do anything for me or not. That is what Gerolt’s token earns.”

“And after?”

“After that, I cannot keep risking this inn for someone who has no intention of leaving it.”

Marta turned towards the door.

“You said you knew someone who might know where to begin.”

Her hand stopped upon the latch.

“I do.”

“Can you bring them here?”

Marta’s fingers tightened around the latch. “No. Her name is Darcy. The garrison keeps her beneath the fort, and at dawn they intend to move her beyond Kelmend.”

A burst of shouting rose from the inn below, followed by the crash of something wooden striking the floor. Marta closed her eyes for one brief moment.

“Drink. Stay here until I come for you, and do not let Wena announce you to every drunk beneath this roof.”

The door closed before Alaric could ask what she meant.

Marta did not return until the inn had fallen silent.

By then, the lamps in the passage had burned low. Alaric took Gerolt’s token from the washstand and followed her downstairs with Wena at his heel.

The inn bore the remains of a crowded night. Rings of foam marked the tables. Chairs stood crooked across the floor, and the air smelled of smoke, spilled ale and damp wool.

Silas sat at the opposite end of the longest table. Three sheets of paper lay before him beside a rough map of Kelmend, each corner weighted with a cup.

He watched Alaric descend the stairs one careful step at a time.

“You look worse for wear.” A slight smile pulled at one corner of his mouth.

“He crossed a flooded river and walked here on torn feet,” Marta said. “Keep your amusement to yourself.”

Wena went to her first.

Marta placed a hand against the dog’s neck, then pointed Alaric towards a chair at the table. A bowl of broth waited before it.

“Eat.”

“How long has the garrison kept Darcy?”

Alaric sat.

Marta remained standing across from him.

“Since she was ten,” Marta said.

“Why?”

Her fingers pressed against the table.

“We grew up together. When Mydea measured our『Affinities』, the examiner placed a marked silver cup beneath Darcy’s hands. She held them over it until her arms began to shake. Three drops fell. They did not reach the lowest measure.”

Alaric looked at her hand upon the wood.

“What happened to her?”

“The examiner emptied the cup and made her try again. Then a third time, while every child waiting behind her watched.”

“That made her Faint,” Alaric said.

Marta pulled out the chair opposite Alaric but did not sit.

“It gave the law the word it needed.” Marta’s fingers remained around the chair. “By the end of that week, men she had never met were deciding where she would sleep, what work she would perform and whether she was permitted to see her family.”

“The law calls it protective stewardship.” Marta’s mouth tightened around the words. “The garrison calls her property. Neither believes there is a difference.”

Silas kept his attention upon the map. The stillness in him was not indifference.

“If she is so weak,” Alaric asked, “why does the garrison want her?”

“Because her mind has won battles her owners were incapable of understanding.”

Marta pushed one of the papers across the table. Lines crossed it in tight columns, forming roads and formations Alaric did not recognise.

“She sees how armies move before they begin moving. She can look at stores, weather and distance and tell a commander where his soldiers will starve. She has ended sieges without breaching walls and won battles without allowing the enemy to choose where they were fought.”

“Then why is she still treated like property?”

“They value what she gives them,” Marta said. “They do not value her.”

The words came quickly.

Too quickly.

Marta drew the paper back and flattened a corner which was already flat.

“Officers place their seals beneath her strategies. When those strategies succeed, they are promoted. When she corrects them before other men or refuses to repair the consequences of their stupidity, the captain reminds her that intelligence did not raise her above her『Affinity』.”

Alaric lowered his spoon.

“How?”

Marta’s fingers tightened against the table.

“They keep her awake until she can no longer remember what day it is. They withhold food until holding a quill makes her hand shake. If she still refuses, they beat her where her clothes will conceal it.”

Her voice grew quieter.

“Never the hands. They need those to write the victories they intend to steal from her.”

Alaric forgot the broth between his hands.

“The last time they permitted me to visit, she had been awake for four days. They let me into the room because she had stopped answering them.”

Marta’s gaze dropped towards the map.

“She was sitting on the floor beside the bed. She could not lift the cup they had placed within reach.”

Her knuckles whitened against the table.

“The captain asked me to persuade her to finish a supply route before morning. He stood over her while he said it. Spoke as though the bruises beneath her clothes had appeared because she had been difficult.”

The room remained silent.

“I held the water for her,” Marta said. “She apologised for spilling it.”

Her hand left the map.

She crossed to the nearest table and gathered two abandoned cups. Their rims knocked together in her hands. She stilled them, then returned without looking at either man.

Alaric stared at the roads and military formations spread across the map.

The world had called her weak and taken everything her weakness could not defend.

“What happens tomorrow?”

Marta sat at last.

“She leaves Kelmend at dawn under military guard. Natharul has begun moving against Kozmagar’s beastfolk, and Mydea is sending troops to the frontier. The court believes helping the elves is safer than refusing them.”

“They are giving Darcy to Natharul?”

“They are sending her to Mydean officers serving Natharul’s campaign. Whatever flag hangs above them, they will use her mind and call it loyalty.”

Marta pulled the map closer.

“The transfer order arrived this afternoon. Once she reaches the frontier, the garrison will surround her with soldiers who do not know me, roads I cannot reach and commanders who understand exactly how valuable she is.”

“And you want to stop the transfer.”

“I want to give her a choice before it happens.”

Silas tapped one finger against the edge of the map.

“The order gives us the hour.”

“Find a way to reach her before dawn.” Marta’s gaze cut towards him. “I want Alaric close enough to speak to Darcy. You will get him there.”

Silas looked across the table.

“He can barely walk.”

“Then, for once in your life, adjust your pace to someone other than yourself.” Marta turned back to Alaric before Silas could answer.

“Why me?” Alaric asked.

“The people I trust are known to the garrison. My movements are noticed, and Silas has given enough guards reason to remember him. No one there knows your face.”

“They know what I look like.”

“They have a description. Dark hair, black clothing and a farm dog. Wena will not be beside you, and we can cover the clothing.”

Her gaze passed over his bandaged hands and swollen ankle.

“That does not make you prepared. You will carry my offer, not fight your way to her. If Darcy accepts, Silas gets both of you beyond the wall.”

Silas looked at Marta.

“You have already decided I am doing this.”

“You wanted to remain in Kelmend. Make yourself useful.” Marta held his gaze until he looked down at the map.

“Where do you want them taken?”

Marta placed one finger beyond Kelmend’s outer wall.

“The broken well at the outer edge of the Faint quarter, beyond the wall. I will wait there with Wena and provisions.”

Alaric looked down at the dog beside him. “How will you get her through the wall?”

“In my provision cart. The eastern workers’ postern admits supply carts before dawn, and its watch knows my seal better than it knows half its own guards.”

Wena rested her head against his knee.

“Has Darcy asked you to do this?” Alaric asked.

Marta’s hand stopped upon the map. “She has asked what freedom feels like. What lies beyond the roads she is forbidden to use. I believe she wants to leave, but I have not been able to ask her about this without placing her under suspicion.”

“Then she might refuse.”

“She might.” Marta looked towards Silas. “If she does, you bring Alaric back without her.”

Silas’s jaw tightened. “We may not get another chance.”

“Then do not waste this one arguing with her. You will not drag her from one group of armed men because you think your destination is kinder.”

Silas held her gaze before giving a short nod. “Understood.”

Alaric looked at the map. He could still picture the silver cup beneath Darcy’s hands. Three drops, and strangers had begun deciding the rest of her life.

That was what weakness earned here. He had nothing at all.

The thought frightened him. It also shamed him. Darcy was beneath the fort now, not some warning about what might happen to him.

“You have not answered,” Marta said more quietly.

Alaric rubbed his thumb against Wena’s ear. “I know.”

“What I told you upstairs stands. You and Wena have until first light whether you accept or not. I will show you a way out of Kelmend.”

Silas watched Alaric across the table.

“Think before answering.” The last trace of humour had left Silas’s face. “The men inside that fort will kill you the moment you become inconvenient.”

Marta’s eyes moved towards him.

“Warn him without making your fear sound like certainty.”

“It has kept me alive.”

Marta’s voice lowered. “It kept you alive.”

The distinction struck whatever remained behind Silas’s expression.

He looked away.

Wena’s fur had warmed beneath Alaric’s hand. He remembered it cold and slick in the river, her body sinking because she had followed him. Gerolt had asked him to keep her alive. Dying beneath the fort would not repay either of them.

But Marta was asking him to reach Darcy, not defeat the people holding her. Silas would be there. For once, Alaric could choose what he was willing to attempt before someone else chose for him.

“I cannot promise I can get her out,” he said.

“I did not ask you to.”

“I can carry the offer. Whatever she answers, I will listen.”

Marta’s hands became still.

She studied him before nodding.

“There is something else you should know.”

Silas looked towards her.

Marta kept her attention upon Alaric.

“Darcy may be able to help you understand what happened on Gerolt’s farm.”

Alaric’s hand stopped against Wena’s neck.

“How?”

“She knows history and old wars. Describe the bodies, the machines and that circle. She may recognise something we cannot. That is all I can promise.”

Alaric looked at her.

“You knew that before you asked me.”

“Yes.”

“Why didn’t you say it?”

“Because I needed you to understand that she can refuse you, too.” Marta kept her eyes upon his. “I will not promise you anything she has not agreed to give.”

Alaric drew Gerolt’s token from his pocket and set it beside the papers.

The split shield faced upwards. He wanted to ask whether Marta truly thought Darcy could help him, but she had given him the only answer she possessed.

Silas gathered the pages into a single stack.

“I need to see the garrison before the watch changes.”

“Then go,” Marta said. “Come back with a route he can manage.”

She turned back to Alaric.

“You listen when he tells you to stop or run. If anyone draws steel, get out of sight. Wena and I will be waiting at the well.”

Alaric nodded.

There was no complete plan. No strength inside him he knew how to offer. By dawn, he would be walking towards the garrison while the people searching for him controlled every gate in Kelmend.

His hands trembled when he placed them upon the table.

He left them there.

“Then tell me what Darcy needs to hear.”
