# The Voices We Follow

Redd knocked the heel of bread against the wall. A small piece came away from the stone.

He examined both.

“I’m going to need a knife.”

“You have one,” Silas said.

“I was hoping yours was sharper.”

Silas passed it across without looking up from the water skins. They sat beneath a window that had melted into a single dark fold, several hours beyond Foramen. Sunlight reached the opposite wall but had not yet found their patch of shade. Alaric could still smell burned reeds in his cloak.

His sword lay against his pack. He had cleaned it during their first stop, using sand and a scrap of cloth until Silas showed him how to keep the grit from the sheath. Even now, with the blade covered, Alaric preferred to sit where the hilt did not touch his leg.

Redd worked the knife through the bread and offered him the larger piece.

“Take that before he decides we’re finished resting.”

“I’m the one who told you to eat,” Silas said.

“Yes, but you’ve been ready to leave since we sat down.”

Silas stopped arranging the skins. He leaned back against the wall, stretched his legs and closed his eyes.

Redd nodded approvingly. “There. You look almost bearable.”

Alaric bit into the bread. It was better inside than the crust suggested. Seeds caught between his teeth, and he had to chew for a while before he could swallow.

Wena left Redd and sat before him.

“You’ve had yours,” Alaric told her.

She rested her chin upon his knee.

Redd cut another piece with Silas’s knife. “She doesn’t believe you.”

“You fed her.”

“I know. I wouldn’t believe either of us.”

Alaric broke off a little bread. Wena took it carefully, then pressed closer. He rubbed the place beneath her ear until her weight began to push his leg sideways.

Silas opened one eye. “Don’t give her the rest. She’ll have you walking on an empty stomach.”

“I wasn’t going to.”

“Keep saying that.”

Redd set his portion beside him to pour water into the shallow tin dish from his sack. Wena moved before he had finished. By the time he put the skin down, the bread was gone.

“Oh, you rotten girl.”

She chewed with her head lowered, watching him.

Alaric laughed. It surprised him enough that he tried to stop. Redd pointed the knife at him.

“You were supposed to be holding her.”

“You took the rope.”

“I feed her, and this is what I get.”

Silas divided his own bread and threw half into Redd’s lap.

“Eat it this time.”

Redd brushed grit from the crust. He sat with it held high against his chest while Wena drank.

“When we get to Favale, I’m ordering something that needs a plate. A proper one. None of this eating off my trousers.”

“With what money?” Silas asked.

“We’ll have saved a fortune on inns by then.”

“I’ll have saved it.”

“Well, I’m glad we’ve worked out who’s paying.” Redd turned to Alaric before Silas could answer. “There was a stall in Kelmend that sold little pies. Meat and onion, with the edges folded over so you could carry them. You had to bite a hole and let the steam out first.”

“You mean the one behind the stables?” Silas asked.

“The woman with the red awning.”

“She watered the filling.”

“I didn’t say they were good pies. I said I remembered them. Favale must have better ones.”

Alaric pictured the folded pastry. “Did you buy them often?”

“When I had coin. If I didn’t, I tried walking down a different street so I wouldn’t smell them.” Redd picked a seed from his teeth. “Usually went past anyway. What would you have?”

Alaric had been about to finish the last of his bread.

“I don’t know.”

“You can have more than one thing.”

“No, I mean I don’t know what I like.”

Redd considered him, then reached over and took back the piece of bread before Alaric could offer it to Wena.

“Well, don’t judge anything by this.”

“I liked the food at Marta’s.”

“Then you’ll be all right,” Silas said. “Most places sell something you can recognise. Just ask what’s in it before Redd orders for you.”

“Why?” Alaric asked.

“Because he’s already spent money he doesn’t have.”

Redd finished the stolen piece. “You could help by telling us where to eat.”

“I know a place near the western market. Depends whether it’s still there.”

“Does it have chairs?”

“It had chairs.”

Redd settled more comfortably against the wall. “I’m going to sit in one until somebody complains.”

They stayed long enough for the sunlight to reach Silas’s boots. Alaric listened while the other two argued about whether the woman beneath the red awning had owned a dog or an exceptionally ugly cat. Redd was certain it had bitten him. Silas remembered feeding it fish.

When they finally rose, Alaric brushed the crumbs from his coat and fastened his sword where Silas had taught him to wear it. Redd returned the knife, rinsed the dish with the water Wena had left and hung it from his sack.

Beyond the wall, a pale street ran west between windows sealed with stone.

---

The buildings thinned after midday. Long stretches of sand separated one standing wall from the next, and the line Kurdag had described became something Silas had to stop and search for.

They walked close enough to speak without raising their voices. Silas went ahead, Redd behind him with Wena, while Alaric watched the ground they had crossed. At each turn, one of them waited until the others could see where to follow.

A broken tower stood beside their route. Its upper half had fallen through an arch, leaving a wedge of masonry suspended between two columns. Redd refused to pass beneath it. They went around instead, stepping through the remains of several smaller rooms.

Alaric found himself trying to guess their purposes. A shallow shelf could have held cups. The round hollow beneath it might have been a hearth, though no chimney remained. He had thought there would be more things left inside the houses. Even the spaces where doors had hung were empty.

“Keep looking where you put your feet,” Silas told him.

Alaric stepped over a crack just before Wena nosed it. Below lay another floor, far enough down that the light barely reached it.

He took her rope from Redd while they passed the opening.

“Have you been along this part before?” he asked Silas.

“No. I stayed farther out.” Silas pointed across the pale ground. “There were farms within a day’s walk on my last crossing. You could turn back if you had to.”

“And here?”

“Here I’m following Kurdag.”

Redd wiped the back of his neck. “I’d like to follow him somewhere with trees next.”

Silas stopped upon the edge of a worn foundation. Through the dust ahead, two dark heights rose above everything else. Alaric took them for cliffs, though their tops were too blurred to make out.

“Those might be the figures,” Silas said. “We’ll check from higher ground.”

Alaric tried to imagine statues large enough to be visible from here. Perhaps the buildings near them were smaller than they appeared.

“And if you hear someone,” Silas continued, “tell me before you answer. Even if you think it’s one of us.”

Redd stopped wiping his neck.

“You could have mentioned that over breakfast.”

“You’ve asked twice whether there are people ahead.”

“I heard talking.”

“That’s why I’m mentioning it now.”

Alaric listened. Wind dragged grains of sand along the exposed foundations. Somewhere to their left, a thin sound rose and fell, almost like a person trying to speak through a closed door. It broke apart when he turned towards it.

“I can hear something,” he said.

Silas waited while he tried to describe it.

“I can’t make out words.”

“Good. Don’t go looking for them.”

They continued along the darker sand. Twice, Alaric glanced back at the crack through the floor, expecting it to remain visible. He could no longer distinguish it from the other broken rooms.

An hour later, Redd caught Silas’s sleeve.

“We’ve gone around.”

The tower stood ahead of them.

Its broken crown still hung inside the arch. Alaric could see the wedge between the columns and the smaller rooms through which they had passed.

Silas turned slowly, checking the sun and the buildings behind them.

“We might have curved south.”

“We went past that side,” Redd said. “I remember the crack. Wena nearly put her head into it.”

Alaric had Wena beside him now. He could remember taking the rope. He could remember the places where they had turned. None of them seemed large enough to have brought them here.

“I don’t think we went around,” he said.

“Then how is it there?” Redd asked.

Alaric had no answer.

Grit shifted beneath Silas’s boot.

He stepped back at once and put an arm across Redd. A narrow line had opened in the sand before them. It widened, filling itself as quickly as it appeared, and the dark grains began to move sideways.

**Krrrrrrm.**

The sound came from beneath the road. Alaric pulled Wena onto the foundation as a wall across from them shuddered. Sand rose against its base, gathered, and carried it towards the tower.

The wall stayed upright.

A window passed before another window. Alaric watched a corner turn through ground he had almost stepped upon. There was no wind strong enough to move it. Dust drifted the other way, catching upon the wall’s broken top while the whole structure travelled beneath it.

Redd crouched beside Wena.

“Silas.”

“Stay on the stone.”

“I am on the fucking stone.”

The current of sand broadened until it filled the street. Fragments of paving rolled through it, vanished, then appeared farther along. One struck the foundation beneath Alaric with a noise like a hammer upon a door.

He moved Wena back another step.

Silas watched the edge closest to them. His sword remained sheathed.

They waited. The moving wall travelled beyond the tower and slowly turned away. By the time the sand settled, the shadow of the foundation had lengthened across their boots.

Silas tested the ground with a loose piece of stone before stepping down. Redd followed, then stopped.

“Wait. Where’s—”

He looked back at Alaric.

Wena tugged towards him. Redd counted them under his breath, touching a finger for each man and lowering the last towards the dog.

“What is it?” Alaric asked.

“I thought someone was behind you.”

“There isn’t.”

“I can see that.” Redd looked past him again. “I know. Come on.”

He took three steps before looking back once more.

---

By late afternoon, the tower had disappeared behind a curtain of sand. Silas no longer allowed more than a few paces between them.

They found the darker ground again along a row of sealed windows. It led towards a low building with most of its roof intact. Silas had begun checking the entrance when sand spilled over the sill from within.

He retreated. A pale mound rose through the doorway, folding over itself until the lower half of the opening vanished.

Redd watched it fill.

“We’re not sleeping in that.”

“No,” Silas said.

Alaric looked along the row. Farther on, broken stairs climbed the face of a taller building. The upper doorway stood well above the street, cut into a wall without windows.

Redd had already seen it.

“Up there. The floor’s above the sand.”

They approached without leaving the line of dark ground. The stair had lost its upper section. From the last intact landing, a gap nearly two paces wide separated them from the doorway, with a drop below it taller than Silas.

Under the landing lay two planks joined by wooden crosspieces. Someone had dragged them beneath the stone to keep them out of the weather. Their cut ends still showed the grain; the wood could not have belonged to the ancient building.

Silas pulled them free and pressed his weight upon the middle.

“People have used this.”

“Recently?” Alaric asked.

“Recent enough to leave wood that hasn’t rotted.”

Redd helped him raise the joined boards. Together they slid the far end onto the sill. Silas crossed first, testing each step, and disappeared through the doorway.

His voice returned from inside.

“Pass the packs over. One at a time.”

Wena disliked the crossing. She planted her forefeet upon the landing and pulled against the rope while Redd knelt inside the doorway and called her. Alaric could see the ground through a narrow split between the boards. He put one foot upon them, felt how little space there was to either side, and understood her reluctance.

“Come on,” he murmured. “I’m coming with you.”

He kept her close. Halfway over, Wena decided that reaching Redd was better than staying where she was. Her claws scraped the wood; Alaric stumbled after her and caught the doorjamb with his free hand.

Redd steadied him by the elbow.

“She’s been waiting all day to do that.”

Inside, the room was wider than Redd’s cottage, with shallow recesses along two walls and a stone bench beneath the third. Sand lay in the corners. A few blackened sticks and an empty cloth wrapper marked the remains of an earlier camp.

Silas checked the ceiling while Alaric moved towards a pale opening at the back. It was a window, low enough to step through, looking out over a sloping roof that had fallen against the building. The far side of the roof met the sand. Several holes interrupted the slope, but a strip along the wall remained whole.

“We could get down here,” Alaric said.

Silas came to look. He tested the stone beneath the window with his heel.

“If we need to. Watch those gaps.”

Outside, the light had begun to turn brown.

“I’d rather stay up here than try another building,” Alaric said. “We can pull the boards in, can’t we?”

“That’s what I was thinking.” Silas looked towards Redd. “Anything moving below us?”

Redd stood beside the front opening, listening.

“Redd?”

“No. I was just…” He leaned out, then drew back. “Nothing.”

They pulled the crossing inside and laid it along the wall. A fallen length of stone shelf lay near the entrance. Redd suggested using it to block the lower part of the doorway. With the boards as levers, he and Silas dragged it close enough that one end rested beside the jamb, ready to be pushed across if anything climbed the stairs.

Alaric spread a blanket beside the bench and looped Wena’s rope around a stone support. She could reach their bedding but neither opening. The satchel stayed across his body, the wrapped core resting against his hip as he sat.

They ate without making a fire. Below, the sand rasped against the wall. Alaric gave Wena her share, then checked the dish while Silas measured out water for the men.

Redd took his cup but did not drink.

“Yes?” he said towards the doorway.

Silas lowered the skin.

“What did you hear?”

Redd looked at him as though the question had interrupted another conversation.

“I thought you said something.”

“I didn’t.”

“All right.”

“What did it say?”

Redd rubbed the heel of his boot against the floor.

“Something about coming in. I couldn’t hear properly.”

Alaric listened again. A murmur reached him from the window behind them. He thought he caught his own name, but when he tried to separate it from the wind, there was only the scrape of loose grains upon stone.

“I’m hearing things too,” he told Redd.

“I’m tired. That’s all.” Redd drank, swallowed too quickly and coughed into his sleeve. “I haven’t slept since we got to that damned city.”

Silas took the cup when he finished.

“Lie down for a while. I’ll watch the front.”

“What about you?”

“Alaric can take over later.”

Redd settled beside Wena. For a while he scratched behind her ears, using the slow movement that usually sent her to sleep. Then his hand stopped and she lifted her head to push beneath it.

Alaric remained by the rear window. He could see a line of dull light across the sloping roof, and beyond it a court slowly filling with shadow. Nothing moved there except dust. He checked the front of the room when Silas shifted, then looked out again.

The court seemed farther below him.

He leaned back and examined the sill. His boots were still in the same place. He waited, looking at the stone rather than the distance, until the falling sensation eased.

“You all right?” Silas asked.

“I think so.” Alaric described what had happened.

“Sit down to watch. Don’t lean out.”

He obeyed. Across the room, Redd had turned his face away from Wena and towards the door.

The last daylight left the ceiling.

---

The first figure passed beneath the broken stair without raising its head.

Silas beckoned to Alaric from the doorway. Alaric moved far enough from the rear window to see the street through the entrance.

For several steps, the figure looked like a person carrying a heavy load. Its back bent forward and one arm dragged almost to the ground. Then it turned beneath the remaining light, and Alaric saw that there was no load. Its shoulders had grown around the side of its head. Plates of pale, ridged matter overlapped across its back, with dark skin stretched between them.

One foot struck the ground heel first. The other folded beneath it and unfolded when it stepped again.

Alaric drew away from the opening.

“What is that?”

“I don’t know,” Silas whispered. “Stay back.”

Another appeared beyond the first. It moved on three limbs, keeping a fourth pressed against its chest. Neither came towards the stair. Alaric watched them disappear behind a fallen wall, then returned to the rear window.

Wena had risen. Her nose worked towards the doorway and a low growl vibrated through her chest.

Alaric knelt beside her.

“Easy. Stay here.”

He rested a hand upon her back. The growl continued, quieter now, while her ears followed something he could not hear.

Behind him, wood scraped stone.

Redd was lifting the joined boards.

“What are you doing?” Alaric asked.

“He can’t find the way up.” Redd pushed the far end towards the space beside the fallen shelf. “Help me get this out.”

Silas turned from the doorway.

“Put it down.”

“Just for a moment. He’s down there.”

“There’s nobody down there you know.”

Redd’s expression sharpened with impatience.

“I heard him.”

Alaric caught the nearer crosspiece before Redd could slide it farther. The boards were heavier than he expected. He had to brace them against his thigh.

“Who did you hear?”

“My father.”

Redd said it plainly, still trying to move the wood. Alaric almost looked outside for the man before he remembered the cottage, the sword, and what Redd had told them beside the road.

“Redd, you said—”

“I know what I said. I can hear him now.” Redd dragged the boards sideways. “He’s telling me to come in. He always calls when it gets dark.”

Silas reached across and gripped the far end.

“Listen to me. I need you away from the door.”

“He doesn’t know where I am.”

“Redd.”

“Let me answer him.”

He pulled. The boards struck the stone shelf with a sharp crack, and Wena barked.

Below, something hit the lower stair.

Silas released the boards and drew his sword. “Get him back.”

Alaric set his end down rather than let it fall. Redd twisted past him, slipping into the gap beside the shelf. He was looking into the street, searching from one patch of darkness to another.

“I’m up here,” he called.

Alaric caught his coat.

“You can’t go out there.”

“I’m not going anywhere. I’m showing him.”

“Please come back.”

Redd tried to prise Alaric’s hand loose. He kept looking outside, frustrated by the cloth holding him short of the edge.

“He’s waiting for me. He’ll think I’ve gone down to the stream again.”

A pale hand closed upon the outer sill.

The fingers were long enough to curl over the stone and touch the shelf. Beneath them, a forearm lengthened out of the dark. Its skin drew taut between the ridges running down it.

Silas struck.

The blade bit into the arm, but the fingers held. He planted one boot against the shelf and drove the sword down again.

Alaric tried to pull Redd past him. Redd turned to look at the noise, his heel caught the boards, and both men lurched towards the jamb. Alaric struck it shoulder first. His right foot slipped over the sill into empty air.

**THOOM.**

His heartbeat hammered inside his head. The street lay below his boot. He could feel the pull of the satchel, the cold weight dragging at his side, and Redd’s coat beginning to slip from his grasp.

“Redd. Help me.”

Redd looked down.

Alaric could not find the floor with his foot.

“Please.”

Redd seized the front of his coat. He stumbled backwards, pulling hard enough that Alaric’s knee struck the sill. Alaric caught the shelf and hauled himself through the gap. They fell together against the boards.

Fire flashed above them.

Silas had pressed his free palm against the gripping fingers. The stink of scorched skin entered the room. The fingers opened, and he kicked the damaged arm clear of the sill. Something struck the lower landing with a heavy, uneven thud.

“The stone,” Silas said. “Help me.”

Alaric struggled upright. He and Silas pushed the shelf across the remaining gap while Redd dragged the boards out from beneath their feet. Stone scraped stone. The slab settled across the bottom of the doorway, leaving only the opening above it.

Silas stood behind it with his sword ready.

“Back to the window, Alaric.”

Wena was straining against the rope. Alaric caught her collar, made himself check the knot, and crossed to the rear opening. The sloping roof remained empty. He stared until he could distinguish its broken edges again.

Behind him, Redd sat upon the floor.

“He’s still there.”

Alaric turned. Redd was looking past Silas.

“I know it can’t be him. I know that.” His voice faltered. “Why does it sound like him?”

Silas did not lower the sword.

“Sit by Wena. Where I can see you.”

Redd moved on his hands and knees until he reached the blanket. He sat against the bench, his father’s sword lying awkwardly across his legs.

Outside, claws scratched the wall beneath the sill.

They did not stop for a long time.

Sometimes Alaric heard them low down, searching the joints between stones. Once the scraping climbed almost to the entrance, and Silas thrust his sword into the darkness. A fragment of pale matter fell inside the room. He hooked it upon the point and dropped it outside before resuming his watch.

The shelf blocked the lower doorway, but Silas still had to watch the opening above it. Alaric kept his sword across his knees at the rear window. Whenever the scratching paused, he expected to see a shape moving upon the roof below him.

Wena lay against Redd’s legs, watching the front. When he stopped stroking her, she pushed her nose beneath his wrist until he began again.

Alaric’s knee ached where he had struck it. He could bend it, and the pain did not grow worse. He left it alone.

A footstep sounded behind him.

He turned towards the bench.

“What?” Silas asked immediately.

“I thought someone moved.”

Redd and Wena were still upon the blanket. Their packs lay together beside the bench. Nothing stood between them and the wall.

Silas watched the same space.

“I keep hearing it too,” he said.

Alaric looked at him.

“From there?”

“Sometimes. Sometimes right behind me.” Silas shifted so that one shoulder rested against the jamb. “If you hear it, check. Don’t go following it.”

“Were there voices when you crossed before?”

“Once. I left before they got this clear.”

Redd lifted his head. “Could we leave now?”

Silas glanced towards Alaric’s window.

Alaric checked the roof again. Beyond it, he could make out neither the dark sand nor the sealed windows.

“I can’t see where we’d go.”

“Then we stay,” Silas said. “Until we can.”

Redd pressed his forehead against Wena’s head. She endured it briefly, then shifted until he was leaning upon her neck instead.

Later, when the scratching moved away, Silas gave Alaric the front watch and took the rear window. Redd shifted his blanket closer to him, bringing Wena with him as far as the rope allowed. Alaric heard water being poured and Redd refusing it.

“Have a little,” Silas said.

“I’m not thirsty.”

“Drink anyway.”

Redd accepted the cup. He held it between his knees afterwards, apparently forgetting to return it.

“Can you hear what he’s saying?” he asked.

Silas waited before answering. “No.”

“It’s the same thing. Every time.”

Alaric looked back from the door. Redd was listening again, turning his head slightly as though trying to hear through another person’s conversation.

“Redd,” Silas said. “Do you want me to keep talking?”

Redd nodded.

Silas began telling him about a leak he remembered above Marta’s kitchen. Redd interrupted to say it was still there. She had paid a man to repair it twice, but the water kept coming through a different cupboard. Silas knew the man. He asked whether he still brought his youngest boy along to hold the ladder.

“His youngest boy’s got children now,” Redd whispered. “It’s his grandson holding it.”

“Gods. I’ve been away a while.”

Their voices remained low. Occasionally Redd asked him to repeat something, though Alaric had heard it clearly from the doorway.

Wena slept across both their feet.

Alaric watched the lower stair, listening to the two men discuss roof tiles. He found himself picturing Marta putting bowls beneath a leak and swearing at the ceiling. The image was easy to hold. It remained when the murmur beyond the door tried to become words.

Later, Redd took the rear window while Silas returned to the front. He called whenever he was unsure of what he heard, but he stayed beside Wena. Alaric slept in short, uncomfortable stretches until it was his turn again. Each time he woke, both openings were still being watched.

---

Redd tried to joke while they packed.

“Well. Next time we pay for a room, I’ll…”

He folded the blanket again, though it had already been folded. Alaric waited for the rest. Redd pushed it into his sack without finishing.

Daylight lay upon the outer landing. The claw marks beneath the entrance were deep enough to hold narrow lines of shadow. None of the creatures remained in sight, but Redd still turned towards the street whenever the wind dropped.

Silas divided the remaining bread. He put Redd’s portion beside the sack and kept sorting the water.

“Eat before we leave.”

“I’m not going to do it again.”

“I’m asking you to eat.”

Redd picked up the bread.

Alaric unfastened Wena’s rope from the support. He had to work the loop loose where she had pulled it tight during the night. Redd watched him struggle and reached across.

“Push that bit back through first.”

Alaric tried. The knot loosened.

“Like this?”

Redd took it and showed him. Their fingers were clumsy with cold. Wena waited until they had finished, then shook dust over both men.

“Thank you,” Alaric told Redd, wiping his mouth with his sleeve.

Redd coiled the rope. He did not immediately offer to take it.

Silas passed him the smaller of the filled skins and lifted the rest himself.

“Tell us when you hear it. Don’t wait until you’re sure.”

“I was sure.”

“I know.”

Alaric checked the rear roof in daylight. A faint line of footprints crossed the sand beyond it, too far away to tell whether they belonged to the figures they had seen. He did not try to follow them.

They left through the window. Silas went down first, testing the unbroken strip beside the wall. Alaric passed the packs through before Redd followed with Wena. The dog needed help over the sill, but once upon the slope she picked her way down more steadily than any of them.

They left the heavy wooden crossing inside the room.

They found the dark sand beyond the building and resumed their way west.

For the first hour, Redd flinched at sounds Alaric barely noticed. Once, he stopped and shut his eyes so hard that his whole face creased. Silas waited beside him. When Redd could look ahead again, they went on at a slower pace.

Alaric began telling them which building he was walking towards before he moved. At first it felt foolish to name a wall everyone could see. Then Silas asked him to point out a doorway again, and Alaric realised they had been looking at different places.

He stayed between the two men after that. He still needed Silas to choose the route. When the sand changed colour beneath a shadow, Alaric would have followed the wrong strip if Silas had not stopped him.

By midday, the ruins had opened into lower, widely separated foundations. Wind moved freely across them. Redd had gone some time without stopping to answer anything the others could not hear.

They rested beneath an overhanging wall. Silas took off his boots to empty them while Wena lay upon her side with her paws stretched into the shade.

Redd sat beside Alaric.

“He used to call twice,” he said.

Alaric waited.

“My father. First time meant he wanted me home. Second time meant he’d already come looking.” Redd rubbed a patch of dust from his sleeve. “I’d go down to the stream after he told me not to. Come back with my boots full of mud and swear I’d been somewhere else.”

“Did he believe you?”

“Of course he didn’t. He washed the boots.”

A little air escaped Redd, almost a laugh. He looked across at the open ground.

“Last night I kept thinking he hadn’t called the second time yet. That I could get out before he started worrying.”

“Are you still hearing him?”

“Sometimes.” Redd hesitated. “Not right now.”

Silas pulled his boot back on.

“We’ll rest a little longer.”

Redd began to object, then leaned against the wall instead. Wena opened one eye when his boot touched her paw. He moved it and she went back to sleep.

Alaric watched the road while the other two rested. The distant heights appeared and vanished through the drifting dust. He could not make them into people, however long he looked.

---

They reached the first stones beneath the distant heights late that afternoon, near the end of their second day beyond Foramen.

At first Alaric thought the wind had uncovered the face of a cliff. Dark bands crossed it in long folds, descending towards the sand. Broken houses clustered beneath the lowest ridge, their empty windows barely larger than the marks upon its surface.

He followed the folds upwards.

Above them hung a hand.

Three fingers remained. The shortest was longer than the room in which they had spent the night. Their tips rested against a carved thigh, and higher still an arm joined a shoulder so broad that a fallen tower could have lain across it.

Alaric stopped walking.

The head emerged slowly from the thinning dust. Most of its face had broken away. One side retained a cheek, the hollow beneath a brow, and an ear worn almost smooth. It faced west, towards the lowering sun.

Beyond the avenue, the second figure faced east.

That one had lost both arms. A deep split ran from its shoulder through the carved clothing and down into the rock beneath its feet. Its head remained whole enough for Alaric to see that it had once worn something upon its brow. Whatever it had been was gone, leaving a rough ring of broken stone.

Neither figure stood upon a pedestal. Their feet widened into ridges that ran beneath the buried street. Alaric could follow an unbroken seam from a stone beside his boot into the nearer figure’s heel.

They had cut people from the land itself.

Redd stood with his sack hanging from one shoulder.

“He could have said they were this big.”

Silas shaded his eyes and looked up.

“Would you have believed him?”

Redd considered the surviving hand.

“Probably not.”

Alaric tried to picture someone working up there. A person upon a rope, small tools striking the side of a finger. How many times would they have climbed? How much of a life could disappear into carving something that large?

“Do you think they finished them?” he asked.

Silas studied the damaged head. “I can’t tell.”

“I’d want to see them before this happened.” Alaric indicated the missing face, then found that he meant the whole avenue. “All of it.”

Redd moved beside him. “I’d like to know how they got up to the nose.”

“You’d fall off,” Silas said.

“I didn’t say I wanted to go.”

They walked between the figures. Long shadows stretched east across the ruins, and Alaric passed beneath the nearer shoulder into shade that reached beyond the avenue. The sand had buried the street but left pieces of it showing: the edge of a broad step, a channel cut beside it, a stretch of paving worn hollow where feet might once have passed.

Wena sniffed the stone and sneezed.

Alaric laughed under his breath and followed her south.

The ground looked level beyond the avenue. Silas kept them upon a dark strip that curved around the edge of it. Halfway along, Redd dropped a small stone onto the paler surface. It broke through a thin crust and disappeared without reaching anything Alaric could hear.

They gave the edge more room.

A low ridge led them past the heel of the eastern figure. From there, Alaric could see the two backs rising above him, their enormous shoulders divided by open sky. He was still looking when Silas raised a hand.

“Listen.”

At first there was only wind.

Redd turned his head away from the ruins. Alaric watched him, but Silas had knelt beside a line of flat stones.

Under the wind came a thinner sound. It hurried, broke, and hurried again.

Running water.

“There,” Silas said. “Under that end.”

They followed the sound along the stones. Sand covered the joints, gathering more deeply where several slabs had settled. Redd crouched beside one and pushed his fingers through the loose grit.

“The corner’s broken. Help me clear it.”

Alaric set his pack above the drifting sand and knelt beside him, turning the satchel towards his back. They scooped the sand away with their hands. Redd loosened a lodged fragment with his knife, then Silas lifted it free.

Water moved below the opening, no farther down than Alaric’s forearm could reach. It ran through a stone channel towards the south, clear over the dark bottom.

Alaric reached for an empty skin.

“Let me check first,” Silas said.

He filled the tin dish, held it towards the light and smelled it. After a small taste, he set it beside him and waited while the other two finished clearing the opening. Alaric watched him more closely than the water.

“Does it seem all right?”

“It seems all right.” Silas took a longer drink. “Same channel Kurdag told us about. We fill here.”

Redd sat back upon his heels and let his head fall forward.

“Thank fuck.”

Wena pushed between them.

She had waited through the digging. She had waited through the tasting. Now she put one forepaw upon Alaric’s leg and tried to reach the opening over his knee.

“All right. Move back a little.”

She did not.

Redd filled the dish again and carried it into the shade. Wena followed so quickly that Alaric had to catch himself against the slab.

They filled every bottle and skin, passing them between one another without setting the uncapped containers in the sand. Alaric drank slowly at first, then had to stop himself and breathe. Cold water ran down his chin. He wiped it away and discovered he was smiling.

When he lifted his share, the weight surprised him.

“Three dry days from here,” Silas reminded them. “Keep the full skins out of the sun. We’ll carry what we can and go slowly.”

Redd adjusted the ties upon his sack. “I’m not complaining about heavy water.”

“Give it an hour.”

They rested upon a ridge of bare rock within sight of the channel. Before settling, Silas showed them the line they would take south when they left. No one suggested starting immediately.

Alaric slipped the satchel from beneath his cloak, set it beside his thigh and checked the fastening. The core remained wrapped, cold and quiet. He covered it with the edge of his cloak and leaned back upon his pack.

Above the ruins, the stone figures faced their separate horizons. A strip of evening light reached the damaged cheek of the west-facing one.

Redd sat close enough that Wena could stretch across the space between them. He had taken off his boots. Silas watched him empty a small mound of sand from each.

“You could have done that when we stopped earlier.”

“I was busy imagining chairs.”

Alaric looked towards him.

“I think I want one of those pies.”

Redd lowered the boot.

“The meat ones?”

“Yes. With the folded edges.”

“You have to let them cool. I wasn’t joking about that.”

“You said to bite a hole first.”

Redd nodded. “That’s right.”

Wena rolled onto her side and pressed her paws against Alaric’s leg. He moved his cloak out from beneath her, careful not to disturb the satchel.

“You’ll have to show me,” he said.

“I can do that.” Redd looked across at Silas. “Will your place have them?”

“If it doesn’t, we’ll find somewhere that does.”

“With chairs,” Redd said.

Silas settled his pack against the stone and sat down properly.

“Yes. With chairs.”
